The signals every pilot must know: distress and urgency, the restricted area warning, interception signals and words, unlawful interference, light signals from the tower and how to acknowledge them, signals laid out in the signal area, and marshalling signals.

Distress and urgency

SignalMeansHow it is given
DistressGrave and imminent danger, immediate help neededMAYDAY on radio, SOS (... --- ...) in Morse, transponder 7700, red rockets or shells fired one at a time at short intervals, a red parachute flare, or a data link message meaning MAYDAY
Urgency, first kindThe aircraft has difficulties that force it to land, but needs no immediate helpRepeated switching on and off of the landing lights, or of the navigation lights in a way that looks different from normal flashing nav lights
Urgency, second kindA very urgent message about the safety of a ship, aircraft, vehicle or personPAN PAN on radio, XXX in Morse, or a data link message meaning PAN PAN
  • Each of these words is said three times: MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY, PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN.
  • A distress message goes first on the frequency in use. If there is no answer, use 121.5 MHz.

Restricted area warning

A series of projectiles fired from the ground at 10 second intervals, each bursting into red and green lights or stars, tells an aircraft that it is flying in, or about to enter, a restricted, prohibited or danger area without permission. It must take action to fix this at once.

In India, an aircraft that finds itself over a prohibited area must give the distress signal, squawk 7700, not fly further in or descend over it, and land at the nearest aerodrome outside it.

Interception

  • Civil aircraft are intercepted only as a last resort, to identify them, or to send them back to their route, away from a restricted area, or to land somewhere.
  • The intercepted aircraft must follow the interceptor's instructions, tell ATS if it can, try to call the interceptor on 121.5 MHz (or 243.0 MHz), and squawk 7700 on Mode A unless ATS says otherwise.
  • If radio instructions from another source clash with the interceptor's visual signals, ask for clarification and follow the interceptor meanwhile.

Unlawful interference (hijack)

  • An aircraft under unlawful interference squawks 7500 where it can, and tries to tell ATS about it and anything that may affect the flight.
  • If it must leave its assigned track or level and cannot tell ATS, it tries to broadcast warnings on the frequency in use or 121.5 MHz, and uses other equipment such as data link.
  • Where it cannot follow its cleared level, it flies 500 ft away from the normal levels where 1,000 ft vertical separation is used, and 1,000 ft away where 2,000 ft separation is used.
  • ATS gives such an aircraft priority and keeps the information confidential as far as possible.

Memory trick

Green goes, red stops. Flashing green brings you back or lets you taxi. Flashing red sends you away. White flashes are about where to end up.

Marshalling signals

  • Only a trained and approved person may marshal an aircraft. He wears a fluorescent vest and faces the aircraft, in front of the left wingtip in view of the pilot.
  • By day he uses fluorescent wands, bats or gloves. At night, lit wands.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Distress: MAYDAY, SOS, 7700, red rockets or flare. Urgency: PAN PAN, XXX, landing or nav lights switched on and off.
  • Restricted area warning: projectiles every 10 seconds, red and green.
  • Interception: 121.5 or 243.0 MHz, squawk 7700, follow the interceptor.
  • Rock wings and slow turn: follow me. Break-away climbing turn of 90 degrees: proceed. Gear down over runway: land here.
  • You raise gear over the runway at 1,000 to 2,000 ft: aerodrome not suitable. Lights at regular intervals: cannot comply. Irregular: distress.
  • Hijack 7500, radio failure 7600, emergency 7700. Hijack level change: 500 ft (1,000 ft separation) or 1,000 ft (2,000 ft separation).
  • In flight: steady green land, steady red give way and circle, green flashes return, red flashes do not land, white flashes land and go to apron, red flare do not land for now.
  • On ground: steady green take off, steady red stop, green flashes taxi, red flashes clear the landing area, white flashes back to start.
  • Acknowledge in flight by day: rock wings. At night: landing or nav lights twice. On the ground by day: ailerons or rudder.
  • Red square, two yellow diagonals: no landing. One diagonal: take care.
  • Dumbbell: runways and taxiways only. With black bars: take-off and landing on runways only.
  • Double white cross: gliders. Black C on yellow: ATS reporting office.

Quick revision

The 8 revision slides for this module, as a list.

Distress and urgency

  • MAYDAY, SOS, 7700, red flares
  • Urgency: PAN PAN, XXX
  • Landing lights on and off: must land, no help needed
  • Say each word three times

Restricted area warning

  • Projectiles every 10 seconds
  • Red and green stars
  • You are in or near a restricted area
  • Act at once

Interception

  • 121.5 or 243.0 MHz
  • Squawk 7700
  • Rock wings: follow me
  • Break-away: proceed

Your signals to the interceptor

  • Gear up over runway: not suitable
  • Lights regular: cannot comply
  • Lights irregular: distress
  • Words: WILCO, CAN NOT, AM LOST

Squawks

  • 7500 hijack
  • 7600 radio failure
  • 7700 emergency
  • Hijack level offset: 500 or 1,000 ft

Light signals in flight

  • Steady green: land
  • Steady red: give way, circle
  • Green flashes: return
  • Red flashes: do not land

Light signals on the ground

  • Steady green: take off
  • Steady red: stop
  • Green flashes: taxi
  • White flashes: back to start

Ground signals

  • Red square, two diagonals: no landing
  • One diagonal: take care
  • Dumbbell: hard surfaces only
  • Double cross: gliders

Sample questions

5 of the 36 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. A red parachute flare seen from an aircraft means:

    1. Urgency
    2. Distress
    3. Do not land
    4. Restricted area
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Red rockets, shells or a red parachute flare are distress signals.

  2. Q2. The interceptor makes an abrupt climbing turn of 90 degrees or more without crossing your path. It means:

    1. Follow me
    2. You may proceed
    3. Land here
    4. You are in distress
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The break-away manoeuvre means you may go on.

  3. Q3. A steady green light from the tower to an aircraft in flight means:

    1. Return for landing
    2. Land and go to apron
    3. Give way
    4. Cleared to land
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Steady green in flight: cleared to land.

  4. Q4. White flashes to an aircraft in flight mean:

    1. Land at this aerodrome and proceed to the apron
    2. Give way
    3. Do not land
    4. Return for landing
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Clearances to land and taxi will follow.

  5. Q5. A double white cross in the signal area means:

    1. Runway closed
    2. Landing prohibited
    3. Gliders are flying
    4. ATS reporting office
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The double white cross shows glider flying.
