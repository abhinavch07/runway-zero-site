How gyros work, the attitude indicator, heading indicator and turn coordinator with their errors, the remote indicating (slaved) compass, and inertial navigation: the stable platform INS and the strapdown IRS with ring laser gyros.

Gyro properties

Gyros are driven by air (a vacuum pump draws air through the case, checked on a suction gauge) or by electric motors. Many aircraft use both so that one failure does not take out every gyro.

Attitude indicator

  • An earth gyro with its spin axis vertical. The case moves round the gyro, showing pitch and bank against the horizon bar.
  • An erection system keeps it upright: pendulous vanes on air-driven gyros, torque motors with levelling switches on electric ones.
  • Acceleration error: on take-off, an air-driven AI shows a slight climb and right bank. Turning error: after a turn, it shows a small bank the other way.
  • Older gyros topple past their pitch and bank limits and must be re-erected or caged.

Heading indicator (directional gyro)

  • A tied gyro with its spin axis horizontal. It has no magnetic sense: it must be set to the compass and checked about every 15 minutes.
  • Unlike the compass, it has no acceleration or turning error, so it is used for turns and steady heading.
  • Real wander: from friction and faults in the gyro.
  • Apparent wander: the gyro holds still in space while the Earth turns under it. Earth rate = 15 x sin latitude degrees per hour. Zero at the equator, 15 degrees per hour at the poles.
  • Transport wander: from flying east or west, as the meridians are not parallel.
  • A latitude nut (a small weight) can be set to cancel earth rate for one latitude.

Earth rate wander (degrees per hour) = 15 x sin latitude

Turn indicator, turn coordinator and slip ball

  • A rate gyro with one gimbal, held by a spring. Its precession shows the rate of turn.
  • Rate 1 turn = 3 degrees per second, which is 360 degrees in 2 minutes.
  • Bank angle for a rate 1 turn, rough rule: TAS / 10 + 7 degrees. At 120 kt: about 19 degrees.
  • A turn coordinator has its gimbal tilted about 30 degrees, so it also senses roll and shows a turn as soon as you bank.
  • The slip indicator (a ball in a curved tube) shows whether the turn is balanced. Ball to the inside of the turn: slipping. Ball to the outside: skidding. Fix it by stepping on the ball (rudder on the side the ball is).

Remote indicating (slaved) compass

  • A flux valve (detector unit), usually in a wingtip or tail away from aircraft magnetism, senses the direction of the horizontal part of the Earth's field.
  • Its signal slaves (slowly corrects) a directional gyro, so the display has the steadiness of a gyro and the north reference of a magnet.
  • This removes most acceleration and turning errors and much of the deviation. The heading is sent to many displays (HSI, RMI, autopilot).
  • An annunciator shows whether the gyro is in step with the flux valve. A synchronising knob brings it into step quickly.
  • In free (DG) mode the slaving is switched off, for example at very high latitudes.

Inertial navigation: INS and IRS

  • Inertial navigation measures acceleration in each direction. Integrating acceleration once gives velocity. Integrating again gives distance, and so position. It needs no outside signals.
  • INS (older): accelerometers sit on a stable platform, held level and pointing to true north by gyros on gimbals.
  • IRS (modern): a strapdown system with accelerometers and ring laser gyros fixed to the aircraft. A computer does the platform's work. No gimbals, more reliable.
  • A ring laser gyro sends two laser beams opposite ways round a closed path. When it turns, the paths differ in length (the Sagnac effect), and the beams' frequency difference gives the rate of turn. At very low turn rates the beams can lock in, so the gyro is dithered (vibrated slightly).
  • Schuler tuning (a period of about 84.4 minutes) keeps the platform level as the aircraft moves over the curved Earth.
ItemDetail
AlignmentThe aircraft must be still. The system finds level and true north by sensing gravity and the Earth's rotation (gyrocompassing). Present position is entered by the crew. Takes about 10 minutes, longer at high latitudes
ModesOFF, ALIGN, NAV, ATT (attitude only, if alignment is lost in flight)
OutputsPresent position, true heading, track, drift, ground speed, wind, attitude

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Rigidity: more mass, speed, radius. Precession: force acts 90 degrees on in the direction of spin.
  • AI: earth gyro, vertical axis. DI: tied gyro, horizontal axis. Turn indicator: rate gyro, one gimbal.
  • DI: set to compass every 15 min. Earth rate 15 x sin lat per hour.
  • Air-driven AI on take-off: false climb and right bank.
  • Rate 1 = 3 degrees per second, 2 min for 360. Bank about TAS/10 + 7.
  • Turn coordinator gimbal tilted about 30 degrees: senses roll too.
  • Ball inside: slip. Ball outside: skid. Step on the ball.
  • Slaved compass: flux valve in the wingtip, slaves a DG. Free mode at high latitudes.
  • INS: stable platform, gimbals. IRS: strapdown, ring laser gyros, Sagnac, dither against lock-in.
  • Alignment: still, about 10 min, position entered. Errors grow about 1 to 2 nm per hour.
  • Schuler period 84.4 min.

Sample questions

5 of the 27 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Gyro rigidity increases with:

    1. Less rotor speed
    2. More rotor mass and speed
    3. A smaller rotor radius
    4. Precession
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Mass, speed and radius of gyration all add rigidity.

  2. Q2. The turn indicator uses:

    1. A rate gyro with one gimbal
    2. A space gyro
    3. An earth gyro
    4. A tied gyro
    Show answer

    Answer: A. A spring-restrained rate gyro.

  3. Q3. A rate 1 turn is:

    1. 1 degree per second
    2. 360 degrees per minute
    3. 6 degrees per second
    4. 3 degrees per second
    Show answer

    Answer: D. 360 degrees in 2 minutes.

  4. Q4. The flux valve is usually fitted:

    1. Under the cockpit floor
    2. In the engine
    3. On the instrument panel
    4. In the wingtip or tail
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Away from the aircraft's own magnetism.

  5. Q5. Lock-in of a ring laser gyro at low rotation rates is prevented by:

    1. Heating
    2. A latitude nut
    3. Dither
    4. Slaving
    Show answer

    Answer: C. A small vibration keeps the beams from locking together.
