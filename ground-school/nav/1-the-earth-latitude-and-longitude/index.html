The shape of the Earth, its poles, axis and equator, how latitude and longitude fix a position, the difference between geocentric and geodetic latitude, and the units used in navigation: the nautical mile, kilometre, statute mile and knot.

The shape of the Earth

  • The Earth is not a perfect sphere. It is slightly flattened at the poles and bulges at the equator. This shape is an oblate spheroid (also called an ellipsoid).
  • The flattening is called compression: (equatorial diameter minus polar diameter) divided by the equatorial diameter. It is about 1/298, roughly 0.3%.
  • The equatorial diameter is about 23 nm (about 43 km) more than the polar diameter.
  • The geoid is the shape of mean sea level carried on under the land. It is lumpy, so maps use a smooth model instead.
  • ICAO uses the WGS 84 model of the Earth for all positions. GPS also works in WGS 84.
  • For most navigation sums, we treat the Earth as a sphere, and the errors are small.

  • Latitude is the angle at the centre of the Earth, measured along a meridian, from the equator to the place.
  • Longitude is the angle at the pole (or along the equator) between the prime meridian and the meridian through the place.
  • Positions are written latitude first: 2834N 07706E is 28 degrees 34 minutes north, 77 degrees 06 minutes east (near Delhi).
  • The network of parallels and meridians is called the graticule.
  • The anti-meridian of a place is the meridian 180 degrees away. The 180 degree meridian is the anti-meridian of Greenwich.
  • The antipodes of a place is the point exactly opposite it through the centre: same latitude but in the other hemisphere, and longitude 180 degrees away. The great circle distance to it is 10,800 nm.
TermMeaning
Geocentric latitudeThe angle at the centre of the Earth between the equator and a line to the place
Geodetic (geographic) latitudeThe angle between the equator and the normal (the line at right angles) to the spheroid at the place. This is the latitude on charts
DifferenceZero at the equator and the poles. Most at about 45 degrees latitude, about 11.6 minutes of arc

Change of latitude and longitude

  • Change of latitude (d.lat): subtract if both places are on the same side of the equator. Add if they are on opposite sides.
  • Change of longitude (d.long): subtract if both are on the same side of Greenwich, add if opposite. If the answer is more than 180 degrees, take it from 360 and reverse the direction, as it is shorter the other way round.
  • Mean latitude is halfway between two latitudes. Same hemisphere: add and halve. Opposite hemispheres: take the difference and halve, and it lies in the hemisphere of the bigger latitude.

Worked example: Working it out

  1. From 20N to 35N: d.lat = 35 - 20 = 15 degrees north.
  2. From 10S to 25N: d.lat = 10 + 25 = 35 degrees north.
  3. From 070E to 150W: 70 + 150 = 220, more than 180. So 360 - 220 = 140 degrees east (going east across the 180 meridian).
  4. Mean latitude of 52N and 18N: (52 + 18) / 2 = 35N.

  • One degree of latitude = 60 nm. One minute = 1 nm. So a change of latitude in minutes is the distance north or south in nautical miles.
  • The Earth's circumference is about 21,600 nm (360 x 60) or 40,000 km. From the equator to a pole is 5,400 nm or 10,000 km.
  • A degree of longitude is 60 nm only at the equator. It gets shorter towards the poles (see departure in Module 2).

Memory trick

One minute of latitude is one nautical mile. That one fact solves most distance-along-a-meridian questions.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Shape: oblate spheroid. Compression about 1/298 (0.3%). Equatorial diameter about 23 nm more than polar.
  • World model: WGS 84.
  • Great circle: plane through the centre. Equator and all meridian pairs are great circles. Other parallels are small circles.
  • Latitude: 0 to 90 N or S from the equator. Longitude: 0 to 180 E or W from Greenwich.
  • Parallels get shorter towards the poles. Meridians are all equal and meet at the poles.
  • Geocentric and geodetic latitude differ most at 45 degrees (about 11.6'), zero at equator and poles.
  • d.lat and d.long: same side subtract, opposite sides add. d.long over 180: take from 360 and reverse.
  • 1 nm = 1,852 m, about 6,080 ft. 1 minute of latitude = 1 nm. 1 degree = 60 nm.
  • 1 sm = 5,280 ft = 1.609 km. 1 kt = 1 nm per hour. 1 m = 3.28 ft.
  • Antipodes: great circle distance 10,800 nm.

Sample questions

5 of the 30 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The shape of the Earth is best described as:

    1. A perfect sphere
    2. An oblate spheroid
    3. A prolate spheroid
    4. A cylinder
    Show answer

    Answer: B. It is flattened at the poles and bulges at the equator.

  2. Q2. Meridians are:

    1. Small circles
    2. Semi great circles, all of the same length
    3. Of different lengths, longest at the equator
    4. Parallel to each other
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Each meridian is half a great circle, pole to pole.

  3. Q3. The latitude shown on aeronautical charts is:

    1. Geocentric
    2. Magnetic
    3. Geodetic (geographic)
    4. Grid
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Geodetic latitude uses the normal to the spheroid.

  4. Q4. One nautical mile on a spherical Earth is the length of:

    1. One minute of longitude anywhere
    2. One degree of latitude
    3. One minute of latitude
    4. One second of arc
    Show answer

    Answer: C. One minute of arc along a great circle, so one minute of latitude.

  5. Q5. The great circle distance between a place and its antipodes is:

    1. 5,400 nm
    2. It depends on the latitude
    3. 21,600 nm
    4. 10,800 nm
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Half the circumference: 180 x 60 = 10,800 nm.
