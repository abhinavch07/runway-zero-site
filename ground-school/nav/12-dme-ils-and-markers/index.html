Distance measuring equipment: how it times pulses, slant range and its accuracy. Then the instrument landing system: the localiser and glide path, their frequencies, coverage and indications, marker beacons, false glide paths and protected areas.

Distance measuring equipment (DME)

  • DME works on UHF, 962 to 1,213 MHz. It is a secondary radar: the aircraft's interrogator sends pairs of pulses, and the ground transponder replies after a fixed delay of 50 microseconds.
  • The interrogator times the round trip. Radio waves take about 12.36 microseconds to go 1 nm and back.
  • Each aircraft's interrogator uses a random pulse rate (jitter), so it can pick out its own replies from those to other aircraft.
  • First it searches (more pulses), then it tracks (fewer pulses) once it has found its replies.
  • A ground station can handle about 100 aircraft at once. Beyond that, it ignores the weakest interrogations.
  • DME channels are paired with VOR or ILS frequencies, so the pilot tunes the VOR or ILS and the DME comes with it.
  • The DME identity is sent about once every 30 seconds, in a different tone from the VOR it is paired with.

Slant range (nm) = time (microseconds) - 50, divided by 12.36

  • DME shows slant range: the straight line distance from the aircraft to the station, not the ground distance.
  • The difference is largest when close to and high above the station. Directly overhead, the DME reads your height in nm (6,080 ft is about 1 nm).
  • Accuracy is within about 0.25 nm plus 1.25% of the distance (modern sets better, about 0.2 nm).
  • Ground speed from the DME is only correct when flying directly to or from the station.

Worked example: Slant range

  1. Aircraft at 36,000 ft (about 6 nm) directly over a DME: it reads about 6 nm, though the ground distance is zero.
  2. Aircraft at 6,080 ft with a DME reading of 10 nm: ground distance = square root of (100 - 1) = about 9.95 nm. At this range the error is small.

Instrument landing system (ILS)

Localiser (LOC)Glide path (GP)
GivesLateral guidance on the runway centre lineVertical guidance on the approach slope
FrequencyVHF 108.1 to 111.95 MHz, odd first decimal (108.1, 108.15, 108.3...)UHF 329.15 to 335.0 MHz, paired automatically with the LOC
AntennaAt the far end of the runway, on the centre lineTo the side of the runway, near the touchdown point
Lobes90 Hz on the left and 150 Hz on the right, as seen on the approach90 Hz above and 150 Hz below the slope
Coverage10 degrees each side to 25 nm, and 35 degrees each side to 17 nm8 degrees each side to 10 nm. Vertically from about 0.45 to 1.75 times the GP angle
Full scale deflectionAbout 2.5 degrees each sideAbout 0.7 degrees above or below
  • The receiver compares the two tones (the difference in depth of modulation) to show how far off the centre line or slope you are.
  • The standard glide path is 3 degrees. The aircraft crosses the threshold at about 50 ft (the ILS reference datum).
  • False glide paths form above the real one, at about 6 and 9 degrees (multiples of the angle). Always join the glide path from below.
  • The localiser identity is 2 or 3 letters with an I in front (Morse), on the localiser frequency.
  • ILS critical and sensitive areas near the antennas must be kept clear of aircraft and vehicles, or the beams bend. ATC protects them in low visibility.
  • A back course (the localiser beam on the other side) gives reverse sensing on a normal CDI.

Memory trick

Fly towards the needle. On the localiser, needle right means the runway centre line is to your right.

Marker beacons

All markers transmit on 75 MHz, in a fan straight up. Many ILSs now use a DME instead of markers.
MarkerDistance from thresholdTone and MorseLight
Outer (OM)About 4 to 7 nm400 Hz, dashesBlue
Middle (MM)About 3,500 ft (1,050 m)1,300 Hz, dot dashAmber
Inner (IM)Close to the threshold (CAT II and III)3,000 Hz, dotsWhite

The microwave landing system (MLS) works on about 5 GHz and allows curved approaches, but is little used. GBAS (GNSS landing system) is its modern replacement.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • DME UHF 962 to 1,213 MHz. Ground delay 50 microseconds. 12.36 microseconds per nm round trip.
  • DME gives slant range. Overhead, it reads height in nm. About 100 aircraft per station.
  • DME groundspeed is right only to or from the station. Ident every 30 s.
  • LOC 108.1 to 111.95 MHz, odd first decimal. 90 Hz left, 150 Hz right. Coverage 10 degrees to 25 nm, 35 degrees to 17 nm.
  • GP UHF 329 to 335 MHz, paired. 90 Hz above, 150 below. Coverage 8 degrees to 10 nm. Full scale 0.7 degrees.
  • LOC full scale about 2.5 degrees. GP 3 degrees, threshold at about 50 ft.
  • False glide paths above the real one. Join from below.
  • Markers 75 MHz: OM blue, 400 Hz dashes. MM amber, 1,300 Hz dot dash. IM white, 3,000 Hz dots.

Sample questions

  1. Q1. DME operates in the band:

    1. 108 to 118 MHz
    2. 962 to 1,213 MHz
    3. 329 to 335 MHz
    4. 190 to 1,750 kHz
    Show answer

    Answer: B. UHF.

  2. Q2. An aircraft at 36,000 ft passes directly over a DME station. The DME reads about:

    1. 0 nm
    2. 6 nm
    3. 36 nm
    4. 3 nm
    Show answer

    Answer: B. 36,000 / 6,080 is about 6 nm.

  3. Q3. The ILS localiser operates on:

    1. 108.1 to 111.95 MHz, odd first decimal
    2. 108.0 to 117.95 MHz, even decimal
    3. 329 to 335 MHz
    4. 75 MHz
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Even decimals in that range are VORs.

  4. Q4. The glide path coverage is 8 degrees each side to:

    1. 10 nm
    2. 17 nm
    3. 25 nm
    4. 5 nm
    Show answer

    Answer: A. 10 nm.

  5. Q5. The outer marker shows a:

    1. White light and dots
    2. Amber light and dot dash
    3. Blue light and dashes
    4. Green light
    Show answer

    Answer: C. 400 Hz, two dashes a second.
