The two kinds of line a pilot flies: the great circle, which is shortest, and the rhumb line, which keeps the same track. Then the sums that link them: departure along a parallel, convergency of meridians, and the conversion angle between great circle and rhumb line tracks.

Great circles and rhumb lines

Great circle (GC)Rhumb line (RL)
DistanceShortest distance between two points (the shorter arc)Longer, except along a meridian or the equator, where both are the same line
TrackChanges as you go, because the meridians convergeConstant: it cuts every meridian at the same angle
Where it liesOn the polar side of the rhumb lineOn the equator side of the great circle
Special casesMeridians and the equator are both great circles and rhumb linesParallels of latitude are rhumb lines (track 090 or 270)
  • Only one great circle joins two points, unless they are antipodal, when there are endless ones.
  • A great circle cuts the equator at two points 180 degrees apart, and divides the Earth into two equal halves.
  • The vertex of a great circle is its highest latitude. There the track is exactly 090 or 270.
  • A rhumb line that is not a meridian or parallel spirals towards the pole without reaching it. That spiral is called a loxodrome.
  • Long flights follow (or nearly follow) great circles to save distance. Short flights and airways are close enough to straight lines that the difference is small.

Departure

Departure is the east-west distance in nautical miles between two meridians, measured along a parallel of latitude. Since a parallel is a rhumb line, departure is a rhumb line distance.

Departure (nm) = d.long (minutes) x cos latitude

  • At the equator, cos 0 = 1, so departure equals d.long in minutes. Biggest here.
  • At 60 degrees, cos 60 = 0.5, so departure is half the d.long in minutes.
  • At the poles, cos 90 = 0, so departure is zero.
  • To find d.long from a known departure: d.long (minutes) = departure / cos latitude.

Worked example: Departure sums

  1. d.long 10 degrees (600 minutes) at 60N: 600 x 0.5 = 300 nm.
  2. An aircraft flies 240 nm due east at 40N (cos 40 = 0.766). d.long = 240 / 0.766 = 313 minutes = 5 degrees 13 minutes east.
  3. An aircraft at 0000N flies 600 nm east, then 600 nm north, then 600 nm west, then 600 nm south. At 10N, 600 nm west is 600 / cos 10 = 609 minutes, more than the 600 minutes it went east. So it ends about 9 minutes west of where it started.

Convergency and conversion angle

  • Convergency is the angle between two meridians at a given latitude. It is also the change in great circle track between them.
  • It is zero at the equator (meridians are parallel there) and equals the d.long at the poles.

Convergency = d.long x sin (mean latitude)

Conversion angle = 1/2 convergency = 1/2 d.long x sin (mean latitude)

  • The conversion angle is the angle between the great circle track and the rhumb line track at either end.
  • The rhumb line track lies halfway between the initial and final great circle tracks.
  • Initial GC track and final GC track differ by the convergency.
  • In the northern hemisphere, great circle tracks increase as you go east and decrease as you go west. The great circle always starts and ends on the polar side of the rhumb line.
  • In the southern hemisphere it is the other way round.

Worked example: Convergency sums

  1. Two places at 30N, d.long 20 degrees: convergency = 20 x sin 30 = 20 x 0.5 = 10 degrees. Conversion angle = 5 degrees.
  2. Rhumb line track 090(T) between them, eastbound in the north: initial GC track = 090 - 5 = 085, final GC track = 090 + 5 = 095.
  3. Same flight westbound: RL 270. Initial GC = 270 + 5 = 275, final GC = 270 - 5 = 265.

Memory trick

Draw it. In the northern hemisphere, the great circle bulges up towards the pole. Starting east, you must first head a little north of the rhumb line, so the GC track is less than the RL track.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • GC: shortest, track changes, on the polar side. RL: constant track, longer, on the equator side.
  • Meridians and the equator are both GC and RL.
  • Vertex: highest latitude of a GC, track 090 or 270 there.
  • Departure = d.long (min) x cos lat. Biggest at equator, zero at poles. Half at 60 degrees.
  • Convergency = d.long x sin mean lat. Zero at equator, equals d.long at poles.
  • Conversion angle = half the convergency. RL track is halfway between initial and final GC tracks.
  • Northern hemisphere: GC track increases going east. Southern: the opposite.

Sample questions

5 of the 25 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The shortest distance between two points on the Earth is along:

    1. A rhumb line
    2. The shorter arc of the great circle through them
    3. A parallel of latitude
    4. A loxodrome
    Show answer

    Answer: B. A great circle gives the shortest path.

  2. Q2. Departure is:

    1. The time of take-off
    2. The east-west distance between two meridians along a parallel
    3. The change of latitude
    4. The angle between two meridians
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Measured in nm along a parallel.

  3. Q3. At what latitude is a d.long of 2 degrees equal to a departure of 60 nm?

    1. 30 degrees
    2. 45 degrees
    3. 60 degrees
    4. 0 degrees
    Show answer

    Answer: C. cos lat = 60 / 120 = 0.5, so latitude 60.

  4. Q4. The conversion angle is:

    1. Half the convergency
    2. Twice the convergency
    3. Equal to the convergency
    4. The variation
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The angle between GC and RL tracks is half the convergency.

  5. Q5. An aircraft at 0000N flies 600 nm east, 600 nm north, 600 nm west and 600 nm south. It ends:

    1. At the start point
    2. A little east of the start point
    3. A little west of the start point
    4. 600 nm north of the start
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The westbound leg at 10N covers more longitude (600 / cos 10 = 609 minutes) than the eastbound leg at the equator.
