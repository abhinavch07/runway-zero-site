Why ice matters

  • Ice on the wing changes the aerofoil shape and roughens the surface: lift falls, drag rises, the stall speed rises and the stalling angle falls. The stall warning may not work as designed.
  • Ice adds weight, can jam controls, unbalance propellers, block pitot and static ports (wrong instruments), and damage engines when it breaks off.
  • Even a thin layer of frost like sandpaper can cut lift a lot. Ice, snow or frost must be removed before take-off.
  • Airframe icing needs supercooled water droplets. It is rare above about 40,000 ft because there is hardly any liquid water there.

Anti-icingPrevents ice from forming. Switched on before entering icing

Protection systems

  • Boots are inflated in cycles by air from the engines or an engine driven pump, and deflated by vacuum so they lie flat and do not add drag. Old advice was to wait for some ice to build so it cracks cleanly (to avoid ice bridging, where ice forms round the inflated boot). Current advice for many types is to use them as soon as icing starts. Follow the flight manual.
  • Engine intake anti-ice must be on before icing, as ice from the lip going into the engine can damage it.
  • Propellers: electric heater mats on the blade roots (cycled between blades), or fluid (isopropyl alcohol) fed through a slinger ring on the hub, which spreads along the blades by centrifugal force. Ice flung off the blades hits the fuselage, so the fuselage there is strengthened.
  • Pitot heat must be on in visible moisture. A blocked pitot makes the ASI act like an altimeter. Pitot heat should not be run long on the ground, as it can burn out without cooling air.

Ice detection

  • Pilots look for ice on the wiper arms, windscreen edges, or a lit ice detector rod (visual probe) near the windscreen.
  • Vibrating rod (Rosemount) detector: a probe vibrates at its natural frequency (about 35 to 40 kHz). Ice on the probe lowers the frequency. At a set drop a warning comes on and the probe heats itself to shed the ice, then the cycle restarts.
  • Other detectors sense ice by a pressure difference (ice blocks small holes in a probe) or a rotating drum whose motor works harder when ice builds.
  • Many aircraft switch the anti-ice systems on automatically when ice is detected.

Windscreens and rain

  • Windscreens are electrically heated by a thin conductive film inside the outer layers. This prevents ice and fog and keeps the vinyl layer warm and tough against bird strikes. Temperature sensors control the heat. Overheat can crack or craze the glass.
  • Wipers clear rain. They must not be run on a dry screen (scratches).
  • Rain repellent: a fluid sprayed on the screen in heavy rain with the wipers, making water bead and run off. It must not be used on a dry screen or in light rain, as it smears and blocks the view.
  • Hydrophobic coatings do the same job permanently on modern screens.
  • Some aircraft blow hot bleed air across the screen to clear rain.

Ground de-icing and anti-icing

  • Ice, snow and frost are removed on the ground with heated de-icing fluid (glycol and water). In falling snow or freezing rain, an anti-icing fluid is then applied to stop new ice forming.
  • One step: one hot fluid both de-ices and anti-ices. Two steps: de-ice first, then anti-ice straight after.
  • Holdover time: how long the anti-icing fluid will protect the surfaces. It depends on the fluid, its mix, the outside air temperature and the type and rate of precipitation. If it runs out before take-off, the aircraft must be checked and de-iced again.
FluidTypeHoldover
Type IThin (unthickened), applied hot. Mostly for de-icingShort
Type II and IVThickened. Stay on the surface until they shear off during the take-off run. Mostly for anti-icingLonger (Type IV the longest)
Type IIIThickened, for slower aircraft that rotate at lower speedsIn between

Cold soaked fuel: after a long flight, very cold fuel in the wing tanks can make frost or ice form under the wing on the ground, even in rain above freezing. Limits on upper and lower surface frost are in the flight manual.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Ice: lift down, drag up, stall speed up, stall angle down, weight up.
  • Anti-icing prevents. De-icing removes.
  • Jet wings and intakes: hot bleed air (anti-icing).
  • Boots: de-icing, inflate in cycles, held flat by suction. Turboprops and twins.
  • Electric heat: pitot, static, probes, windscreens, propellers.
  • Propeller fluid: isopropyl alcohol through a slinger ring.
  • Vibrating rod detector: ice lowers the frequency.
  • Rain repellent: heavy rain only, never on a dry screen.
  • Type I: thin, hot, short holdover. Type II and IV: thickened, longer holdover.
  • Holdover time over: de-ice again.
  • Icing rare above about 40,000 ft.

Quick revision

The 6 revision slides for this module, as a list.

Effects of ice

  • Lift down, drag up
  • Stall speed up, stall angle down
  • Weight up, controls may jam
  • Frost too: remove before take-off

Anti-ice and de-ice

  • Anti-ice: prevent
  • De-ice: remove
  • Jets: hot bleed air on slats, intakes
  • Electric: pitot, probes, screens

Boots and fluid

  • Boots inflate in cycles
  • Held flat by suction
  • TKS: fluid through porous panels
  • Prop: slinger ring, isopropyl alcohol

Detection

  • Wiper arms, ice detector rod
  • Vibrating rod: frequency falls
  • Self heats, then restarts
  • May switch anti-ice on automatically

Rain

  • Heated screens: ice, fog, bird strike
  • Wipers: not on a dry screen
  • Rain repellent: heavy rain only
  • Hydrophobic coatings

Ground de-icing

  • Type I: hot, thin, short holdover
  • Type II, IV: thickened, longer
  • Holdover depends on weather and fluid
  • Expired: de-ice again

Sample questions

5 of the 22 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Ice on a wing leading edge will:

    1. Lower the stall speed
    2. Raise the stall speed and lower the stalling angle
    3. Reduce drag
    4. Have no effect if it is thin
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Ice spoils the aerofoil shape. Lift falls, drag rises, and the wing stalls earlier and at a higher speed.

  2. Q2. Pneumatic boots are a:

    1. Anti-icing system
    2. De-icing system
    3. Rain removal system
    4. Ice detection system
    Show answer

    Answer: B. They inflate to crack off ice that has already formed.

  3. Q3. A vibrating rod ice detector senses ice because ice on the probe:

    1. Raises its temperature
    2. Changes its colour
    3. Stops all electrical current
    4. Lowers its vibration frequency
    Show answer

    Answer: D. The added mass lowers the natural frequency. At a set drop the warning comes on and the probe heats itself.

  4. Q4. Airframe icing is unlikely above about:

    1. 10,000 ft
    2. 20,000 ft
    3. 40,000 ft
    4. 4,000 ft
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Above about 40,000 ft there is almost no supercooled liquid water.

  5. Q5. After a long flight, frost forms under the wing on the ground in rain at +5°C. The likely cause is:

    1. Wrong fluid
    2. A fault in the pitot heat
    3. Cold soaked fuel in the wing tanks
    4. Engine bleed leak
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Very cold fuel chills the wing skin below freezing even when the air is warmer.
