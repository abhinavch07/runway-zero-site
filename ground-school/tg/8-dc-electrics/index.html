Basic quantities and Ohm's law

QuantitySymbolUnitMeasured with
Voltage (electromotive force, potential difference)V or EVolt (V)Voltmeter, connected in parallel

Resistance (opposition to current)ROhm (Ω)Ohmmeter, with the circuit off
PowerPWatt (W)

Ohm's law: V = I x R, so I = V / R and R = V / I

Power: P = V x I = I² x R = V² / R

Worked example: A 28 V lamp draws 2 A

  1. Resistance R = V / I = 28 / 2 = 14 Ω
  2. Power P = V x I = 28 x 2 = 56 W
  • Electric current has three main effects: heating (fuses, lamps), magnetic (motors, relays, generators) and chemical (battery charging).
  • Resistance depends on the material (silver is a better conductor than copper), length (longer, more resistance), cross section (thicker, less resistance) and temperature.
  • Most metals have a positive temperature coefficient (PTC): resistance rises as they get hot. Carbon and many semiconductors have a negative coefficient.

Series and parallel circuits

Aircraft loads are wired in parallel, so each gets full voltage and one failure does not stop the others.
SeriesParallel
CurrentThe same through every partSplits between the branches. Total = sum of branch currents
VoltageShared between the parts. Total = sum of the dropsThe same across every branch
Total resistanceR1 + R2 + ... (bigger than any one)1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... (smaller than the smallest)
If one part fails openEverything stopsThe other branches keep working

Worked example: Two 6 Ω resistors

  1. In series: 6 + 6 = 12 Ω
  2. In parallel: 1/R = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6, so R = 3 Ω
  3. Equal resistors in parallel: one value divided by how many there are.
  • Cells in series: voltages add, capacity (Ah) stays that of one cell. Cells in parallel: voltage of one cell, capacities add.
  • An open circuit is a break, so no current flows. A short circuit is a path with almost no resistance, so a very large current flows, which a fuse or breaker must stop.
  • A capacitor stores charge between two plates. It blocks steady DC but passes AC, and smooths voltage ripples. Unit: farad.

Fuses and circuit breakers

  • Protection devices are fitted in series with the load, as close to the power source (bus bar) as possible, to protect the wiring from overheating.
  • A fuse is a thin wire that melts when the current is too high. It is rated in amps. It usually opens before the full fault current is reached. It must be replaced, and only with one of the same rating. A high rupture capacity (HRC) fuse is used for very large currents.
  • A circuit breaker (CB) opens on a thermal (bimetal) or magnetic trip. It can be reset. Its time to trip is shorter for bigger overloads. It opens a little after the full fault current is reached.
  • A trip free CB cannot be held closed against a fault. Holding the button in does not stop it tripping. A non trip free CB can be held closed, which is only used where the load must be kept at any risk.
  • Rule for pilots: a tripped CB may be reset once, after a cooling pause, if the checklist allows. Never hold it in, and never reset a fuel pump CB in flight unless the checklist says so.
  • A current limiter is a slow blowing fuse for heavy loads that can stand a short overload.

Batteries

  • A primary cell cannot be recharged (torch cell). A secondary cell can be charged and discharged many times. Aircraft batteries are secondary cells.
  • The aircraft battery gives power for engine starting (and APU), emergency power when generators fail, and steadies the bus voltage.
  • Capacity is in ampere hours (Ah): current x time. A 40 Ah battery should give 40 A for 1 hour (at the 1 hour rate). It must be able to supply the essential loads for 30 minutes after a generator failure.
  • A capacity test is done regularly (about every 3 months). If the battery gives less than 80% of its rated capacity it is taken out of service.
A 24 V lead acid battery has 12 cells. A 24 V NiCad has 19 or 20 cells.

  • Thermal runaway (mainly NiCad): a hot battery's resistance falls, so it takes more charging current, which makes it hotter still. It can boil, catch fire or explode. Shown by a battery temperature warning or a high charge current. The battery charger is switched off.
  • Never mix lead acid and NiCad tools or electrolytes, and keep them in separate rooms.
  • Lead acid batteries are stored charged. They slowly lose charge on the shelf (shelf life).

Magnetism, relays and DC generation

  • A current through a wire makes a magnetic field round it. A coil (solenoid) with a soft iron core makes a strong electromagnet.
  • A relay is a switch worked by an electromagnet: a small current in the coil switches a big current in another circuit. A solenoid with a moving core can work a valve or a heavy switch (contactor).
  • Electromagnetic induction: moving a conductor through a magnetic field, or changing the field round it, induces a voltage. This is how generators and transformers work.
  • A DC generator makes AC in its turning coils and changes it to DC with a commutator and brushes. A light aircraft alternator makes AC and changes it to DC with diodes (rectifiers). Alternators give good output even at idle and are lighter.
  • A starter-generator on small turbine engines is one machine: a motor to start the engine, then a generator.
  • The voltage regulator keeps the output steady (about 28 V on a 24 V system, 14 V on a 12 V system) by controlling the field current as engine speed and load change. Generator voltage is set about 2 V above the battery for every 12 V, so the battery charges.
  • A reverse current cut-out (relay) disconnects the generator from the bus when its output falls below the battery voltage, so the battery does not discharge through the generator (motoring it). In alternator systems the diodes do this job.
  • Over-voltage protection trips the generator off line if the regulator fails.

Distribution, bonding and static

  • Power goes from the sources to bus bars, and from them through fuses or CBs to the loads. Typical buses: main, essential (loads needed to fly safely) and battery or hot battery bus (always live from the battery, for things like fire bottles and clocks).
  • Load shedding: when power is short, the less important loads (galley, cabin) are switched off automatically so the essential ones keep power.
  • Single wire (earth return) system: the aircraft's metal structure carries the return current back to the source, saving the weight of return wires. Composite parts need separate return wires.
  • Bonding: all metal parts are joined electrically so they are at the same potential. This stops sparks between parts, cuts radio interference, gives a safe path for lightning, and provides the earth return.
  • Static dischargers (static wicks) on the trailing edges let static charge built up in flight leak away to the air quietly, reducing radio noise.
  • The ammeter shows the battery charging (plus) or discharging (minus), or the generator load. A load meter shows how much the generator is giving.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • V = I x R. P = V x I = I²R.
  • Voltmeter in parallel, ammeter in series.
  • Metals: resistance rises with temperature (PTC).
  • Series: add resistances, same current. Parallel: 1/R adds, same voltage, smaller total.
  • Aircraft loads are in parallel.
  • Fuse: replace with the same rating. Trip free CB cannot be held in.
  • Capacity in Ah. Must give essential loads 30 minutes. Under 80% at test: remove.
  • Lead acid: 2 V cell, sulphuric acid, SG 1.27 charged. Neutralise with sodium bicarbonate.
  • NiCad: 1.2 V cell, potassium hydroxide, SG no guide. Thermal runaway. Neutralise with boric acid.
  • Relay: small current switches a big one.
  • Voltage regulator controls the field current. 28 V on a 24 V system.
  • Reverse current cut-out: stops the battery discharging through the generator.
  • Bonding: same potential, no sparks, less radio noise, lightning path. Static wicks: less radio noise.

Sample questions

5 of the 30 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The unit of electrical resistance is the:

    1. Volt
    2. Ampere
    3. Ohm
    4. Watt
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).

  2. Q2. Two 10 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. The total resistance is:

    1. 20 Ω
    2. 10 Ω
    3. 5 Ω
    4. 100 Ω
    Show answer

    Answer: C. 1/R = 1/10 + 1/10 = 2/10, so R = 5 Ω.

  3. Q3. Compared with a circuit breaker, a fuse normally opens:

    1. After the full fault current is reached
    2. Before the full fault current is reached
    3. Only on a short circuit to earth
    4. Never
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The fuse wire melts quickly as the current rises. A circuit breaker opens a little after the full fault current.

  4. Q4. Thermal runaway is a danger mainly with:

    1. Nickel cadmium batteries
    2. Lead acid batteries
    3. Fuses
    4. Alternators
    Show answer

    Answer: A. A hot NiCad's resistance falls, so it draws more current and gets hotter, which can end in fire or explosion.

  5. Q5. A reverse current cut-out is fitted to stop:

    1. Over-voltage
    2. Lightning damage
    3. Radio noise
    4. The battery discharging back through the generator when its output is low
    Show answer

    Answer: D. When generator voltage falls below battery voltage it disconnects the generator from the bus.
