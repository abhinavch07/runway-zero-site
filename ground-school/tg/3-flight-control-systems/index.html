Primary and secondary controls

All three axes pass through the centre of gravity.
ControlMoves the aircraft inAbout theCockpit control
AileronsRollLongitudinal axisControl wheel turned, or stick sideways
Elevator (or stabilator)PitchLateral axisControl column or stick fore and aft
RudderYawNormal (vertical) axisRudder pedals
  • Secondary controls: flaps and slats (more lift for slow flight), spoilers and speed brakes (dump lift, add drag), and trim (removes control forces).
  • A stabilator (all-moving tailplane) does the job of tailplane and elevator in one. Elevons combine elevator and aileron (on tailless aircraft). Ruddervators combine rudder and elevator (V-tail).
  • Large aircraft may have inboard ailerons (used at high speed, to avoid twisting the wing tip) and outboard ailerons (locked out at high speed, used at low speed), plus roll spoilers.

Mechanical controls

  • Controls are linked to the surfaces by cables (over pulleys, through fairleads), push-pull rods, bell cranks and torque tubes.
  • Turnbuckles set the cable tension. Cable tension regulators keep the tension steady as the airframe (aluminium) and cables (steel) expand and contract differently with temperature.
  • Control stops limit the surface movement: primary stops at the surface, secondary stops at the cockpit control.
  • Cable runs are set up so a cable failure is unlikely to jam the control, and each control is checked for full and free movement in the correct sense before flight.
  • Gust locks hold the surfaces still on the ground so wind cannot slam them against the stops. They must be removed before flight. Many lock the throttles too, so take-off with locks in is impossible.

Powered controls and artificial feel

On large or fast aircraft the air loads are too high for the pilot to move the surfaces by hand. Hydraulic actuators (power control units, PCUs) move them instead.

SystemHow it worksFeel
Power assisted (reversible)The pilot moves the surface, and a hydraulic jack helps. Some of the air load comes back to the pilotNatural feel, a share of the real load
Fully powered (irreversible)The jack does all the work. Air loads cannot move the control backNone from the surface, so artificial feel is needed
  • Artificial feel gives the pilot a sense of how hard he is pulling. Simple systems use springs. Q-feel systems make the force grow with dynamic pressure (q), so the controls feel heavier at high speed, like a manual aircraft.
  • Powered controls need more than one hydraulic system (often 2 or 3), with each surface driven by more than one actuator, so a single failure does not lose control.
  • Mach trim moves the elevator or stabiliser automatically at high Mach numbers to stop the nose dropping (Mach tuck, see Module 19).

Fly-by-wire

  • In fly-by-wire (FBW) the pilot's control sends an electrical signal to flight control computers, which command the actuators. There is no direct mechanical link (or only a small backup).
  • Advantages: less weight, less maintenance, flight envelope protection (limits on bank, pitch, load factor, high angle of attack and overspeed), automatic trim and gust load relief.
  • Control is usually through a sidestick. Many systems command a rate of pitch or roll or a load factor, not a surface position.
  • Systems have several computers and power sources. If they fail, the aircraft drops to lower laws with fewer protections, and finally to a mechanical backup (for example trim and rudder).
  • Signals may also go by optical fibre (fly-by-light), which is immune to electrical interference.

Trim

  • Trim removes the steady force the pilot would have to hold. It does not change the aircraft's stability.
  • Trim tabs on light aircraft: a small tab on the elevator, moved opposite to the way the elevator should go. To hold the elevator up (nose up), the tab goes down.
  • Large aircraft trim pitch with a trimmable horizontal stabiliser (THS): the whole tailplane changes its angle, driven by a screw jack. This keeps full elevator travel for manoeuvres and is powerful enough for big CG and speed ranges.
  • Stabiliser trim runaway is an emergency. Crews stop it with the trim cut-out switches and trim by hand.
  • Before take-off the stabiliser trim must be set in the green band for the CG. A wrong setting sets off the take-off configuration warning.

Memory trick

Tabs work backwards: tab down, surface up. Move the trim wheel the way you are pushing or pulling and the force goes away.

Flaps, slats, spoilers and speed brakes

SurfaceJobNotes
Trailing edge flapsMore lift (and drag) for take-off and landing, so lower speedsUsually hydraulic or electric. Asymmetry protection stops them if one side gets ahead
Leading edge slats and Krueger flapsLet the wing reach a higher angle of attack before stallingExtend before the trailing edge flaps and retract after them
Flight spoilers (speed brakes)Raised together in flight to add drag and lose lift, to descend or slow downLimited in flight. Also help the ailerons in roll
Roll spoilersRise on the down-going wing only, to help rollUsed with the ailerons
Ground spoilers (lift dumpers)Rise fully on landing to dump lift, put weight on the wheels and help the brakesArmed before landing. Deploy on touchdown signal (wheel spin-up or squat switch)
  • Flap and slat speed limits (VFE) protect the structure. Some aircraft retract flaps automatically if the speed is too high (flap load relief).
  • The take-off configuration warning sounds if the thrust levers are advanced on the ground with flaps, slats, spoilers, stabiliser trim or parking brake not set correctly for take-off.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Ailerons: roll about the longitudinal axis. Elevator: pitch about the lateral axis. Rudder: yaw about the normal axis.
  • Cable tension regulators: keep tension steady as the temperature changes.
  • Gust locks: hold surfaces on the ground. Remove before flight.
  • Reversible: pilot feels part of the load. Irreversible: no feedback, needs artificial feel.
  • Q-feel: force grows with dynamic pressure (speed).
  • FBW: electrical signals and computers, envelope protection, lighter.
  • Trim tab moves opposite to the surface.
  • Big jets trim pitch with the trimmable stabiliser.
  • Slats: higher stalling angle. Flaps: more lift. Ground spoilers: dump lift on landing.
  • Take-off configuration warning: flaps, slats, trim, spoilers or parking brake wrong.

Sample questions

5 of the 25 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The ailerons control the aircraft in:

    1. Pitch, about the lateral axis
    2. Roll, about the longitudinal axis
    3. Yaw, about the normal axis
    4. Roll, about the lateral axis
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Ailerons roll the aircraft about the longitudinal (nose to tail) axis.

  2. Q2. In a fully powered (irreversible) flight control system:

    1. The pilot feels the real air loads
    2. Air loads on the surface cannot move the pilot's control, so artificial feel is needed
    3. There is no hydraulic system
    4. The surfaces cannot move at high speed
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The actuator does all the work and blocks feedback, so feel is made by springs or a q-feel unit.

  3. Q3. To trim an aircraft nose up with an elevator trim tab, the tab moves:

    1. Up
    2. Down
    3. It depends on the speed
    4. It does not move, the elevator moves
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The tab moves opposite to the surface. Tab down pushes the elevator up, which raises the nose.

  4. Q4. Leading edge slats:

    1. Reduce lift for landing
    2. Control roll at high speed
    3. Act as speed brakes
    4. Allow the wing to reach a higher angle of attack before it stalls
    Show answer

    Answer: D. They re-energise the airflow over the top surface, delaying separation.

  5. Q5. On some large aircraft the outboard ailerons are locked out at high speed to:

    1. Save hydraulic fluid
    2. Reduce drag only
    3. Increase roll rate
    4. Stop the wing tip twisting and aileron reversal
    Show answer

    Answer: D. At high speed the outboard wing twists under aileron load. Inboard ailerons and spoilers do the work instead.
