Equilibrium and kinds of stability

  • Equilibrium: all the forces and all the moments about the CG balance. In steady level flight, lift = weight and thrust = drag, and the pitching moments cancel (the tailplane load balances the wing's pitching moment and the lift/weight couple).
  • Trim keeps that balance without the pilot holding a force. Methods: trim tabs, an adjustable stabiliser, moving fuel (trim tanks), or spring bias.
  • Stability is how the aircraft behaves after a disturbance (such as a gust), without the pilot doing anything.
An aircraft must be statically stable to be dynamically stable, but a statically stable aircraft can still be dynamically unstable.
KindMeaning
Static stabilityThe first tendency after a disturbance. Positive: tends to return. Neutral: stays in the new position. Negative: moves further away
Dynamic stabilityWhat happens over time. Positive: the oscillations die away (damped). Neutral: they carry on the same. Negative: they grow (divergent)

More stability means the aircraft is steadier but harder to manoeuvre. Less stability means it is more manoeuvrable but needs more pilot attention. Designers aim for enough stability, not as much as possible.

Longitudinal stability (pitch)

  • Stability in pitch, about the lateral axis. Given mainly by the tailplane.
  • If a gust pitches the nose up, the tailplane's AoA rises, so it makes more lift (or less down force). Acting a long way behind the CG, this gives a nose down moment, bringing the aircraft back.
  • The neutral point is the CG position at which the aircraft would have neutral longitudinal stability. The CG must be ahead of the neutral point for stability.
  • The distance from the CG forward of the neutral point is the static margin. A bigger static margin means more stability.
  • Stability increases with a larger tailplane or a longer tail arm, and with the CG further forward.
CG positionEffect
ForwardMore stable, heavier control forces (more stick force per g), harder to flare, higher stall speed, more trim drag
AftLess stable, lighter forces, risk of over-controlling and overstressing, lower stall speed, harder spin recovery
At the neutral pointNeutral stability
Behind the neutral pointUnstable in pitch
  • Stick force per g (manoeuvre stability): how hard the pilot must pull to raise the load factor by 1 g. It falls as the CG moves aft. Too low and the pilot can overstress the aircraft easily.
  • Pitching moment curve: a stable aircraft has a negative slope of pitching moment against CL (or AoA): more AoA gives a nose down moment.
  • In a stable aircraft, a higher speed needs the elevator more nose down (and a slower speed more nose up), and needs a push force to hold it faster than trimmed.
  • Phugoid: a long, slow oscillation of speed and height at almost constant AoA, lasting a minute or so. It is lightly damped, but slow enough for the pilot to control easily.
  • Short period oscillation: a fast pitching movement (a second or two) at almost constant speed. It is heavily damped. The pilot should not try to follow it, as that can make it worse (pilot induced oscillation). Hold the controls still.
  • Ice on the tailplane reduces its effectiveness and can make it stall when the flaps are lowered (more downwash), giving a sudden nose down pitch.
  • Airframe deformation and heavy rain also change the stability slightly.

Directional stability (yaw)

  • Stability in yaw, about the normal axis. Also called weathercock stability.
  • If the aircraft yaws, it is in a sideslip, and the airflow hits the fin at an angle. The fin makes a sideways force behind the CG that yaws the nose back into the airflow.
  • Directional stability is increased by a larger fin or longer arm, more side area behind the CG, a dorsal fin, a ventral fin, and sweepback (the advancing wing has more drag).
  • Side area ahead of the CG (long nose, large engine nacelles) reduces directional stability.

Lateral stability (roll)

  • Stability in roll, about the longitudinal axis. There is no direct restoring force for a bank angle on its own. But a bank leads to a sideslip towards the lower wing, and lateral stability is the tendency to roll away from the sideslip, levelling the wings.
FeatureHow it helps lateral stability
DihedralIn a sideslip, the lower (leading) wing meets the air at a higher AoA and makes more lift, rolling the wings level
High wingIn a sideslip the airflow round the fuselage raises the AoA of the leading wing (and the low CG acts like a pendulum). High wings need less dihedral
SweepbackThe leading wing in a sideslip has less effective sweep, so it makes more lift. Swept wings may even need anhedral to stop too much lateral stability
Tall finThe fin's sideways force acts above the CG, giving a rolling moment away from the slip
  • Anhedral (wings angled down) reduces lateral stability. It is used on high wing, swept aircraft that would otherwise be too stable laterally (and so prone to Dutch roll).
  • A low wing gives less lateral stability than a high wing, so low wing aircraft need more dihedral.

Roll and yaw together: spiral and Dutch roll

Rolling and yawing are linked. Yaw makes one wing go faster, which makes it roll. Roll causes sideslip, which makes the aircraft yaw. So the balance between directional and lateral stability decides how the aircraft behaves.

MotionCauseWhat happens
Spiral instabilityStrong directional, weak lateral stabilityA wing drops, the fin yaws the nose into the turn, the outer wing speeds up and rolls the aircraft further. A slowly tightening spiral dive. Easy for the pilot to correct
Dutch rollStrong lateral, weak directional stability. Common on swept wingsA yawing and rolling oscillation: the wing tips trace an ellipse. It is worse at high altitude. Corrected by a yaw damper
  • A yaw damper senses yaw rate with a gyro and moves the rudder automatically to damp out Dutch roll. If it fails, the crew may need to fly lower and slower, and correct with ailerons (not rudder, which can make it worse).
  • At high altitude, air damping is weaker (lower density, higher TAS), so oscillations last longer. Dutch roll is worse up high.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Static: first tendency. Dynamic: over time (damped, undamped, divergent).
  • Static stability is needed for dynamic stability, but does not guarantee it.
  • Longitudinal: pitch, lateral axis, tailplane.
  • CG must be ahead of the neutral point. Static margin = CG to neutral point.
  • Forward CG: more stable, heavier forces, higher stall speed. Aft CG: less stable, light forces.
  • Stick force per g falls as the CG moves aft.
  • Phugoid: long, slow, speed and height, lightly damped. Short period: fast pitch, heavily damped.
  • Directional: yaw, normal axis, fin, side area behind the CG, sweepback.
  • Lateral: roll, longitudinal axis. Dihedral, high wing, sweepback, tall fin. Anhedral reduces it.
  • Spiral instability: strong directional, weak lateral.
  • Dutch roll: strong lateral, weak directional. Yaw damper. Worse at altitude.

Sample questions

5 of the 25 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Static stability describes:

    1. How oscillations change over time
    2. The aircraft's first tendency after a disturbance
    3. The aircraft's top speed
    4. The CG range
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Static is the initial tendency. Dynamic is what happens over time.

  2. Q2. For an aircraft to be longitudinally stable, its CG must be:

    1. Behind the neutral point
    2. At the neutral point
    3. Ahead of the neutral point
    4. At the trailing edge
    Show answer

    Answer: C. At the neutral point it is neutral. Behind it, it is unstable.

  3. Q3. The phugoid is:

    1. A fast pitching oscillation, heavily damped
    2. A yaw and roll oscillation
    3. A long, slow oscillation of speed and height, lightly damped
    4. A spiral dive
    Show answer

    Answer: C. It lasts a minute or so and is easily controlled by the pilot.

  4. Q4. Sweepback has what effect on lateral stability?

    1. Reduces it
    2. Makes it neutral
    3. No effect
    4. Increases it
    Show answer

    Answer: D. In a sideslip the leading wing has less effective sweep, so it makes more lift.

  5. Q5. Dutch roll is damped by a:

    1. Mach trim
    2. Stick shaker
    3. Yaw damper moving the rudder
    4. Trim tab
    Show answer

    Answer: C. It senses the yaw rate and applies rudder automatically.
