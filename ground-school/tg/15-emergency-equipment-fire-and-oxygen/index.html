Hypoxia and useful consciousness

  • As altitude increases, the partial pressure of oxygen falls, so less oxygen reaches the blood. This is hypoxia. It comes on quietly: the person often feels fine while judgement fails.
  • Time of useful consciousness (TUC): the time a person can still recognise the problem and act, after the oxygen supply is lost.
Times are much shorter after a rapid decompression and with exercise.
AltitudeTime of useful consciousness (about)
18,000 ft30 minutes
25,000 ft3 to 5 minutes
30,000 ft45 to 90 seconds
35,000 ft30 to 45 seconds
45,000 ftAbout 12 seconds

Oxygen systems

  • Crew oxygen is usually gaseous oxygen in cylinders charged to about 1,800 psi. If a cylinder is over-pressured (for example by heat), a bursting disc (often green) on the outside of the fuselage blows out and vents it overboard. A missing disc on the walk-round means the bottle has vented.
  • Some military and older aircraft use liquid oxygen (LOX), which stores much more in a small space.
  • Only aviator's breathing oxygen (very dry) is used. Oil or grease near oxygen can ignite by itself. No smoking near oxygen.
Crew regulator settingWhat it gives
Normal (diluter demand)Oxygen mixed with cabin air, only when breathing in. The share of oxygen rises with cabin altitude until about 32,000 ft cabin altitude, where it gives 100%
100%Pure oxygen whatever the altitude. Used with smoke or fumes
Emergency (pressure)100% oxygen under positive pressure, to clear smoke from the mask and goggles, and at very high cabin altitudes
  • Crew use quick donning masks that can be put on with one hand within 5 seconds.
  • Passenger oxygen in most airliners comes from chemical oxygen generators above each seat row. Pulling a mask down fires the generator. It burns sodium chlorate and iron, making oxygen for about 12 to 15 minutes, and gets very hot. Once started it cannot be stopped.
  • Some aircraft feed passenger masks from gaseous cylinders instead.
  • Masks drop automatically at about 14,000 ft cabin altitude (Module 5).
  • Portable oxygen bottles (about 1,800 psi) for first aid and for cabin crew moving about the cabin.
  • Smoke hoods (PBE, protective breathing equipment) give the crew protection from smoke and fumes for about 15 minutes, with their own oxygen source and a neck seal. Used to fight a cabin fire.

Fire and smoke detection

DetectorHow it works
Unit (spot) detectorsSeparate switches that close at a set temperature (bimetallic strips, or a tube that expands more than its bow). Watch one small area. Older ones (melting links) do not reset
Continuous loop (electrical)A tube with a centre wire in a filling whose resistance falls (or capacitance changes) as it gets hot. Heat anywhere along the loop sets off the warning. Resets when cool
Continuous loop (pneumatic)A tube filled with gas. Heat raises the gas pressure, which closes a switch. Also senses an overall average rise
Smoke detectors: photoelectricSmoke scatters light onto a photocell
Smoke detectors: ionisationSmoke particles reduce the current through ionised air
  • Engines, APUs and main wheel wells use fire or overheat loops, often two loops (both must agree, so one faulty loop does not give a false warning). A test switch checks the loops and warnings.
  • Cargo holds, lavatories and avionics bays have smoke detectors. Lavatory waste bins have a small automatic extinguisher.

Engine and APU fire protection

  • A fire warning gives a bell, a master warning and a lit fire handle for that engine.
  • Pulling the fire handle closes the LP fuel valve, the hydraulic and bleed valves, trips the generator, and arms the extinguishers. Turning it fires an extinguisher bottle by an electric squib (cartridge).
  • Engines are usually protected by two bottles. If the warning stays on after about 30 seconds, the second bottle is fired.
  • Discharge indicators on the outside of the fuselage: a red disc missing means the bottle discharged by overheating (thermal relief). A yellow disc missing means it was fired normally.
  • Typical drill, from the checklist: thrust lever to idle, start lever to cut-off, pull and turn the fire handle, wait 30 seconds, second bottle if needed, land as soon as possible.

  • For a metal fire use dry powder made for metals or dry sand. Water may make it worse.

Doors, exits and survival equipment

  • Transport aircraft must be able to be evacuated in 90 seconds with half the exits working, at night.
  • Doors are often plug doors that are held shut by cabin pressure. Emergency exits are marked and lit, and have instructions.
  • Escape slides are armed (a girt bar attached to the floor) before departure so they deploy automatically when the door opens. They inflate in a few seconds from a gas bottle (CO₂ and nitrogen) drawing in outside air. Slide rafts can be detached and used as a raft in a ditching.
  • Over-wing exits lead onto the wing, with marked escape paths, and slides or ropes on some types.
  • Life jackets: one for each person on overwater flights. Inflate outside the aircraft, never inside, or the person may be trapped by rising water. They have a light and whistle. Crew jackets are often a different colour.
  • Life rafts carry survival kits: sea anchor, water, flares, first aid, a locator beacon.
  • Emergency locator transmitters (ELTs): automatic (fixed, triggered by impact g force) or portable. They transmit on 121.5 MHz (homing) and 406 MHz (satellite, with a coded identity). Some also on 243 MHz.
  • Emergency lighting works from its own batteries if aircraft power fails. Floor path lighting guides people to the exits in smoke.
  • Other kit: crash axe, torches (flashlights), megaphones, fire resistant gloves, first aid kits, emergency medical kit, and portable oxygen.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • TUC: 18,000 ft 30 min, 25,000 ft 3 to 5 min, 35,000 ft 30 to 45 s, 45,000 ft ~12 s.
  • Crew oxygen: about 1,800 psi. Green bursting disc blown: bottle vented.
  • Oil or grease near oxygen can ignite.
  • Diluter demand: 100% at about 32,000 ft cabin altitude. Emergency: 100% under pressure.
  • Chemical generators: sodium chlorate, about 15 minutes, hot, cannot be stopped.
  • Smoke hood (PBE): about 15 minutes.
  • Continuous loop: heat anywhere along it. Resistance falls as it heats.
  • Smoke detectors: photoelectric (light scattered) or ionisation (current falls).
  • Fire handle: LP fuel valve shut, arms bottles. Second bottle after 30 seconds.
  • Red disc: thermal discharge. Yellow disc: normal discharge.
  • Water: Class A only. CO₂: electrical, liquid. Dry powder: wheel and brake fires. Halon: all types.
  • Compartments: A cabin, C engines and holds with built-in extinguishers.
  • Evacuation 90 seconds, half the exits. Life jackets: inflate outside.
  • ELT: 121.5 homing, 406 MHz satellite.

Sample questions

5 of the 27 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The time of useful consciousness at 25,000 ft is about:

    1. 12 seconds
    2. 30 to 45 seconds
    3. 3 to 5 minutes
    4. 30 minutes
    Show answer

    Answer: C. About 3 to 5 minutes at 25,000 ft, and only about 30 minutes even at 18,000 ft.

  2. Q2. A diluter demand oxygen regulator in the Normal setting supplies 100% oxygen at a cabin altitude of about:

    1. 8,000 ft
    2. 14,000 ft
    3. 32,000 ft
    4. 45,000 ft
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The share of oxygen rises with cabin altitude, reaching 100% at about 32,000 ft.

  3. Q3. A smoke hood (PBE) gives protection for about:

    1. 15 minutes
    2. 2 minutes
    3. 1 hour
    4. As long as needed
    Show answer

    Answer: A. About 15 minutes, enough to fight a cabin fire.

  4. Q4. On the walk-round, a red disc is missing from the fire extinguisher discharge port. This shows the bottle:

    1. Discharged because of overheating (thermal relief)
    2. Was fired normally by the crew
    3. Is fully charged
    4. Was never fitted
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Red: thermal discharge. Yellow: normal discharge.

  5. Q5. A class C cargo compartment:

    1. Cannot be reached, and has detection and a built-in extinguishing system
    2. Can be reached by the crew
    3. Has no fire protection
    4. Is the flight deck
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Engines and most large cargo holds are class C.
