Engine layouts and parts

LayoutNotes
Horizontally opposed (flat)Cylinders in two rows lying flat, opposite each other. Most common in light aircraft: smooth, compact, low frontal area, good cooling
In-lineCylinders in one row. Slim but long, rear cylinders cool poorly
V typeTwo rows in a V. Powerful, liquid cooled in older fighters
RadialCylinders round the crankshaft like a star, always an odd number per row so the firing order works. Good cooling, big frontal area

The four-stroke cycle

Remember: suck, squeeze, bang, blow. The crankshaft turns 720° (two turns) per cycle.
StrokePistonValvesWhat happens
1. InductionDownInlet openFuel and air mixture drawn in
2. CompressionUpBoth closedMixture squeezed. Spark just before TDC
3. PowerDownBoth closedBurning gas pushes the piston down. The only stroke that does work
4. ExhaustUpExhaust openBurnt gas pushed out
  • TDC and BDC: top and bottom dead centre, the ends of the stroke. Each stroke is 180° of crank turn.
  • Valve lead: a valve opens early. The inlet opens before TDC, the exhaust opens before BDC (so the gas starts to leave while still under pressure).
  • Valve lag: a valve closes late. The inlet closes after BDC (using the inertia of the incoming mixture to fill the cylinder more). The exhaust closes after TDC.
  • Valve overlap: around TDC at the end of exhaust and start of induction, both valves are open together. The outgoing exhaust helps draw in the fresh charge (scavenging).
  • Ignition timing: the spark comes before TDC (advance) because the mixture takes time to burn. Peak pressure should come just after TDC.

Compression ratio, power and efficiency

  • Swept volume: the volume the piston sweeps through, = piston area x stroke. The engine's capacity is the swept volume of all cylinders.
  • Clearance volume: the space left above the piston at TDC.
  • Stroke = 2 x crank throw.

Compression ratio = (swept volume + clearance volume) / clearance volume

Worked example: Swept volume 700 cc, clearance volume 100 cc

  1. Total volume at BDC = 700 + 100 = 800 cc
  2. CR = 800 / 100 = 8 : 1
  • A higher compression ratio gives better thermal efficiency and more power, but needs fuel with a higher anti-knock rating, or detonation follows (Module 11).
  • Indicated power: the power made in the cylinders. Brake power: what reaches the propeller shaft. The difference is friction and pumping loss.
  • Mechanical efficiency = brake power / indicated power. Thermal efficiency of a piston engine is only about 25 to 30%. Most of the fuel's heat goes out of the exhaust and cooling.
  • Power falls with altitude and with high temperature, because the air is less dense, so less air (and fuel) enters each cylinder. Density altitude is what matters.
  • Typical firing order of a four cylinder engine: 1, 3, 4, 2.

Lubrication

  • Oil lubricates, cools (it carries away a lot of heat), cleans (carries dirt to the filter), seals (between rings and cylinder) and protects against corrosion.
  • Wet sump: oil is kept in a sump under the crankcase and pumped round. Simple, used on most light aircraft.
  • Dry sump: oil drains to the bottom and scavenge pumps return it to a separate tank. Scavenge pumps have more capacity than the pressure pump so the sump stays dry. Used in aerobatic and larger engines, as oil supply holds in any attitude.
  • A pressure relief valve limits oil pressure. A filter bypass keeps oil flowing if the filter clogs. An oil cooler has a thermostatic bypass, so cold oil skips the cooler and warms up quickly.
  • Viscosity is the oil's thickness. It falls as the oil gets hot. Use the grade the manufacturer says. Cold thick oil gives high pressure readings on start.
  • Oil pressure should rise within about 30 seconds of starting (sooner in warm weather). If it does not, shut down at once.
  • Low oil pressure with high oil temperature suggests oil loss or failure: land as soon as possible. A high oil temperature on its own may be from low oil, a blocked cooler, or a lean mixture and high power in a long climb.

Cooling

  • Most aircraft piston engines are air cooled. Fins on the cylinders give more surface area, and baffles guide the air round the cylinders.
  • Cowl flaps at the back of the cowling control the cooling airflow. Open on the ground, in take-off and climb (high power, low speed). Closed in descent to avoid shock cooling, and partly closed in cruise.
  • Cylinder head temperature (CHT) shows how hot the hottest cylinder is.
  • Overheating causes detonation and pre-ignition, loss of power, damage to valves and pistons, and thin oil.
  • Shock cooling: a big, fast drop in power in a fast descent cools the cylinders too quickly and can crack them. Reduce power gradually.
  • A rich mixture helps cooling, as extra fuel absorbs heat. So take-off and climb use a rich mixture.

Ignition

  • Aircraft use magnetos: small engine driven generators that make their own high voltage. They need no battery or aircraft electrics, so the engine keeps running if the electrical system fails.
  • Each engine has two magnetos and two spark plugs per cylinder (dual ignition). This gives safety (one can fail) and better burning, since two flame fronts burn the mixture faster, giving a little more power.
  • A magneto has a primary winding (low voltage), contact breaker points, a capacitor (stops arcing at the points and speeds the collapse of the field) and a secondary winding (thousands of turns, high voltage). A distributor sends the spark to each cylinder in turn.
  • The magneto switch works the opposite way to a normal switch. OFF earths the primary through the P-lead, so the magneto cannot spark. ON opens that earth.
  • A broken or loose P-lead means the magneto is live even with the switch OFF. Turning the propeller by hand could start the engine. Always treat a propeller as live.
  • An impulse coupling helps starting: at low cranking speed it holds back the magneto shaft and then snaps it forward, giving a stronger spark that is retarded (later), so the engine does not kick back.
  • Magneto check at run-up: switch to L, then BOTH, then R, then BOTH. There should be a small rpm drop on each. A typical limit is about 125 to 175 rpm drop, with no more than about 50 rpm difference between the two (use the flight manual).
  • No drop on one magneto: that magneto's P-lead may be broken (it is always live), or the switch is faulty. Do not fly.
  • Large drop or rough running: a fouled plug or magneto fault. A rich mixture burn-off at higher rpm may clear lead or carbon fouling.
  • Dead cut check: briefly switching to OFF at idle, before shutdown, to check that both magnetos are earthed (the engine should start to stop). Only if the manual allows it.

Starting and handling

  • Before starting: fuel on, mixture rich, primer (puts fuel straight into the cylinders or inlet), throttle slightly open, propeller area clear, then start.
  • Over-priming can wash oil off the cylinder walls and cause an induction fire if fuel collects in the intake. If a fire starts in the intake during the start, keep cranking to suck the fire into the engine.
  • Kick back: the engine fires before TDC during cranking and turns backwards. The impulse coupling's retarded spark prevents it.
  • Warm up at a moderate rpm (about 1,000 to 1,200 rpm) so oil reaches everything and plugs do not foul.
  • In cruise, open the throttle smoothly. Avoid big, fast power changes. Watch rpm, oil pressure and temperature, CHT and EGT.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Light aircraft: horizontally opposed. Radials: odd number of cylinders.
  • Four strokes: induction, compression, power, exhaust. 720° of crank, camshaft at half speed.
  • Valve lead: opens early. Lag: closes late. Overlap: both open round TDC.
  • CR = (swept + clearance) / clearance. Stroke = 2 x crank throw.
  • Higher CR: more efficiency, needs higher grade fuel.
  • Thermal efficiency about 25 to 30%.
  • Oil: lubricate, cool, clean, seal, protect. Dry sump: scavenge pumps bigger than pressure pump.
  • Oil pressure within 30 seconds of start, or shut down.
  • Cowl flaps: open for ground, take-off and climb, closed for descent.
  • Magnetos: self powered. Two plugs per cylinder: safety and better burning.
  • Switch OFF earths the P-lead. Broken P-lead = live magneto.
  • Impulse coupling: strong, retarded spark for starting.
  • Mag check: small drop on each. No drop = broken P-lead or switch fault.

Sample questions

5 of the 28 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The most common engine layout in light training aircraft is:

    1. Radial
    2. In-line
    3. Horizontally opposed
    4. V type
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Flat engines are compact, smooth and easy to cool, with a small frontal area.

  2. Q2. The inlet valve closes after BDC in order to:

    1. Cool the valve
    2. Use the inertia of the incoming mixture to fill the cylinder more
    3. Reduce compression
    4. Help the exhaust
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The moving mixture keeps flowing in for a while after the piston starts to rise.

  3. Q3. The thermal efficiency of a typical aircraft piston engine is about:

    1. 5%
    2. 25 to 30%
    3. 60%
    4. 90%
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Most of the fuel's energy is lost as heat in the exhaust and cooling.

  4. Q4. In flight, oil pressure falls and oil temperature rises. The most likely cause is:

    1. Loss of oil
    2. A faulty gauge
    3. Too rich a mixture
    4. Cold weather
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Less oil means less pressure and less cooling. Land as soon as possible.

  5. Q5. A broken P-lead means that:

    1. The magneto will not work at all
    2. The engine will stop
    3. The magneto stays live even with the switch OFF
    4. The battery will drain
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The switch can no longer earth the primary. Moving the propeller by hand could start the engine.
