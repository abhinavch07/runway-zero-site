Boundary layer and separation

  • The boundary layer is the thin layer of air next to the surface, slowed by friction. Its speed is zero at the surface and full speed at its outer edge.
  • It starts laminar (smooth layers, thin, less skin friction), then at the transition point becomes turbulent (eddies, thicker, more skin friction, but more energy near the surface).
  • Behind the thickest point, the air flows into rising pressure (an adverse pressure gradient). If the boundary layer runs out of energy, it stops and the flow separates from the surface.
  • A turbulent boundary layer resists separation better than a laminar one because of its extra energy. This is why vortex generators stir up the boundary layer on purpose.
  • As the angle of attack increases, the separation point moves forward from the trailing edge.

The stall

  • A wing stalls when it exceeds its critical (stalling) angle of attack, about 15 to 16°. The flow separates over much of the upper surface, lift falls sharply and drag rises sharply.
  • A wing always stalls at the same angle of attack (for a given configuration), but it can stall at any speed and any attitude, for example in a steep turn or a sharp pull-up.
  • Signs: buffet (separated flow shaking the tail), stall warning, controls getting sluggish, a nose drop, and sometimes a wing drop. The centre of pressure moves back at the stall, which gives the nose drop.
  • Stall warning must come early enough (at least about 5 kt or 5% above the stall). Devices: a flapper switch on the leading edge (the stagnation point moves down and back under the leading edge as the AoA rises, lifting the vane), an AoA vane, or a stick shaker. Large T-tail aircraft may also have a stick pusher.
  • Recovery: reduce the angle of attack (move the control column forward), add power, keep the wings level with rudder (avoid big aileron inputs near the stall, as a down-going aileron can stall that wing), then recover to normal flight without a second stall.

Where the wing stalls first

The stall should start at the wing root and spread outwards, so the ailerons at the tips still work, the wings stay level, and the turbulent flow off the root buffets the tail as a warning.

PlanformStalls first at
RectangularRoot (good). Used on many trainers
EllipticalEvenly along the span, with no warning
TaperedTowards the tip
Swept backThe tip, made worse by spanwise flow towards the tips
  • Ways to make the root stall first: washout (tip at lower incidence), stall strips (small sharp wedges on the inboard leading edge), slots or slats near the tips, and a different aerofoil section at the tip.
  • On a swept wing, a tip stall loses lift behind the CG, so the nose pitches up (the CP moves forward), making the stall deeper. Wing fences, vortilons and saw tooth (dog tooth) leading edges cut the spanwise flow.
  • Deep (super) stall: on a T-tail aircraft with rear engines, the stalled wing's wake can cover the tailplane, so the elevator has no effect and recovery may be impossible. A stick pusher prevents it.

Spins and spiral dives

  • A spin is a stalled condition with autorotation: one wing is more stalled than the other. The more stalled wing has less lift and more drag, so the aircraft rolls and yaws towards it, and keeps doing so.
  • A spin needs a stall plus yaw (for example, too much rudder or uncoordinated flight at the stall).
  • In a spin the airspeed is low and stays about steady. In a spiral dive the wings are not stalled and the airspeed rises fast, with a growing bank.
  • Standard spin recovery (follow the manual): throttle closed, ailerons neutral, full opposite rudder, then control column forward until the spin stops, then rudder neutral and recover from the dive gently.
  • Spiral dive recovery: reduce power, level the wings, then raise the nose gently.

  • Small flap settings give more lift for little drag: used for take-off. Large settings add a lot of drag as well: used for landing, to steepen the approach and slow down.
  • Retracting flaps at low speed causes a sink and raises the stall speed. Retract in stages, above the minimum speed for each.
  • Spoilers and speed brakes reduce CLmax, so they raise the stall speed.
  • Vortex generators: small vanes on the wing that make little vortices, mixing fast air into the boundary layer and delaying separation.
  • Boundary layer control: sucking away the slow air, or blowing fast air over the flaps, to keep the flow attached.

Contamination and special cases

  • Ice, frost or snow on the wing, even a little, lowers CLmax and the stalling angle, raises the stall speed and drag, and may make the stall warning come too late (it is set for a clean wing).
  • Heavy rain roughens the surface with a water film, raising drag and slightly lowering CLmax.
  • Airframe deformation, dents, poor repairs and an ageing aircraft can change the stall behaviour.
  • An accelerated stall happens at a higher speed than normal because of a high load factor, such as a steep turn or a sharp pull out of a dive.
  • A power on stall has a lower stall speed, but the slipstream and torque can cause a sharp wing drop.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Turbulent boundary layer: more energy, resists separation, more skin friction.
  • Stall at the critical AoA (~15 to 16°), at any speed or attitude.
  • Stall warning: flapper switch, stagnation point moves down and back. Stick shaker, stick pusher.
  • Recovery: reduce AoA first, then power.
  • Vs turn = Vs x √n. n = 1/cos bank. 60°: n = 2, Vs x 1.41.
  • Vs goes with √weight.
  • Stall speed up: weight, load factor, forward CG, ice. Down: flaps, power, aft CG.
  • Rectangular: root first. Tapered and swept: tip first.
  • Root first by washout, stall strips, tip slots.
  • Swept wing tip stall: pitch up. Fences, vortilons, dog tooth.
  • Deep stall: T-tail in the wing wake. Stick pusher.
  • Spin: autorotation, low steady speed. Spiral dive: speed rising.
  • Flaps: CLmax up, stalling angle down, nose lower. Slats: stalling angle up, nose higher.
  • Fowler: adds area, biggest CLmax rise.
  • Vortex generators: delay separation.

Sample questions

5 of the 28 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Compared with a laminar boundary layer, a turbulent boundary layer:

    1. Has less skin friction and separates more easily
    2. Has more energy near the surface and resists separation better
    3. Is thinner
    4. Does not exist on aircraft
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Its mixing brings fast air close to the surface, though it causes more skin friction.

  2. Q2. An aircraft stalls at 60 kt in level flight. In a level 60° bank turn its stall speed is about:

    1. 60 kt
    2. 69 kt
    3. 85 kt
    4. 120 kt
    Show answer

    Answer: C. n = 2, Vs x √2 = 60 x 1.41 = 85 kt.

  3. Q3. A rectangular wing tends to stall first at the:

    1. Tip
    2. Ailerons
    3. Whole span at once
    4. Root
    Show answer

    Answer: D. This is a good trait: the ailerons keep working and the tail gets buffet warning.

  4. Q4. During a spin, the airspeed is:

    1. Low and fairly steady
    2. Rapidly increasing
    3. Above VNE
    4. Zero
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The wing is stalled. In a spiral dive the speed rises fast.

  5. Q5. With flaps down, at the same speed, the aircraft flies with:

    1. A higher nose attitude
    2. A wing down
    3. The same attitude
    4. A lower nose attitude
    Show answer

    Answer: D. The flaps give the lift needed at a lower AoA, which also gives a better view on approach.
