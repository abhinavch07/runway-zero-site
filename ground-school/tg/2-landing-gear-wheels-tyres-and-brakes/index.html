Gear layouts, the oleo-pneumatic shock strut, retraction, locks and emergency lowering, position lights and air/ground logic, nosewheel steering and shimmy, tyres (construction, pressures, creep, heating, fusible plugs), aquaplaning and its speed, disc brakes, brake fires, anti-skid and autobrake.

Layouts and shock absorbers

  • The landing gear supports the aircraft on the ground, absorbs the landing shock, and allows taxiing, steering and braking.
  • Tricycle (nosewheel) layout: main wheels just behind the CG, carrying about 90% of the weight, and a nosewheel. Easy to steer, good view, stable on the ground with no tendency to ground loop.
  • Tailwheel (tail dragger) layout: main wheels ahead of the CG. Light and suited to rough strips, but the CG behind the main wheels makes it unstable on the ground and prone to ground looping.
  • Light aircraft often use spring steel legs, or rubber blocks. Larger aircraft use oleo-pneumatic struts.
  • In an oleo-pneumatic strut, compressed gas (nitrogen or dry air) acts as the spring, and oil forced through an orifice absorbs the energy (damping), so the aircraft does not bounce.
  • A recoil (snubbing) valve slows the strut's extension after the bump.
  • Struts are serviced with nitrogen and hydraulic oil. Extension is checked against a chart for the weight.
  • Torque links (scissors) stop the inner cylinder turning in the outer one, and keep the wheel lined up.

Retraction, locks and indication

  • Retracting the gear reduces drag, improving speed, climb and fuel use.
  • Most retractable gear is raised and lowered by hydraulic jacks. Some light aircraft use electric motors.
  • Uplocks hold the gear up. Downlocks (often over-centre locks) hold it down so it cannot fold. Ground locks (pins) are fitted on the ground for safety.
  • Emergency lowering: the uplocks are released (by a handle, cable or electrically) and the gear free-falls under its own weight and the airflow. The downlocks engage by spring. Some types use a hand pump or a stored gas bottle instead.
  • A sequence valve makes sure the gear doors open before the gear moves, and close after it.
  • Air/ground logic (squat or weight-on-wheels switches) on the struts tell the systems whether the aircraft is on the ground. Uses: prevent gear retraction on the ground, arm ground spoilers and thrust reversers, switch pressurisation and some warnings.
  • A gear warning horn sounds if the throttles are closed or flaps are set for landing and the gear is not down and locked.

Steering and shimmy

  • Light aircraft steer through the rudder pedals linked to the nosewheel, or by differential braking with a free castoring nosewheel.
  • Large aircraft use hydraulic nosewheel steering with a tiller for large angles and the rudder pedals for small corrections at speed.
  • Shimmy is a rapid side to side oscillation of the nosewheel. Causes include worn parts, low tyre pressure and wheel imbalance. It is cured by a shimmy damper, torque links, and correct tyre pressure.
  • The nosewheel is centred by cams inside the strut as it extends after take-off, so it fits in the wheel well.

Tyres

  • Tyres are inflated with nitrogen: it is inert, so it does not support a fire, does not corrode the wheel, and does not change pressure much with temperature as it holds no water.
  • Construction: cross-ply (bias) or radial, with plies of cord, beads of steel wire, and a tread. The ply rating shows strength, not the actual number of plies.
  • Tubeless tyres are common: an inner liner holds the gas.
  • Chines on some nose tyres throw water sideways, away from engines mounted at the rear.

Aquaplaning

Aquaplaning (hydroplaning): a wedge of water builds under the tyre and lifts it off the runway. Braking and steering are lost. It happens above a certain speed on standing water.

Aquaplaning speed (kt) = 9 x √(tyre pressure in psi)

Worked example: Tyre pressure 144 psi

  1. √144 = 12
  2. 9 x 12 = 108 kt
  3. Above about 108 kt on standing water the tyre may aquaplane.
TypeCause
DynamicStanding water lifts the tyre at high speed. Speed from the formula above
ViscousA very thin film of water on a smooth surface or with rubber deposits. Can happen at lower speeds
Reverted rubberA locked wheel skids, friction heat boils the water to steam and melts the rubber. Shows as white marks on the runway

A tyre that is not turning (spun up) on touchdown can aquaplane down to a lower speed (about 7.7 x √P) than one that is already rolling. Grooved runways and good tread reduce the risk.

Brakes, anti-skid and autobrake

  • Brakes turn the aircraft's kinetic energy into heat by friction.
  • Light aircraft: a single disc with calipers. Large aircraft: multi-disc brakes, with stators (fixed to the axle) and rotors (turning with the wheel) pressed together by hydraulic pistons.
  • Carbon brakes are lighter and take and lose heat better than steel, though they wear more when cold.
  • Wear indicator pins show how much brake material is left, checked with the brakes applied.
  • Hot brakes after a heavy stop or rejected take-off can cause a fire or melt the fusible plugs. Approach hot wheels from the front or rear, never from the side, as a tyre or wheel may burst outwards.
  • A wheel or brake fire is best put out with dry powder. Water or CO₂ on a very hot wheel can make it shatter by cooling it suddenly.
  • The parking brake traps hydraulic pressure in the brakes, often from a brake accumulator.
Anti-skid featureWhat it does
Skid controlSenses a wheel slowing too fast (about to skid) and releases brake pressure on it, then reapplies. Gives the shortest stop without locking wheels
Locked wheel protectionCompares wheel speeds. If one wheel is much slower than the others, its brake is released
Touchdown protectionStops braking until the wheels have spun up (or a few seconds after weight on wheels), so the aircraft cannot land with locked wheels
Below about 20 mph (around 10 kt)Anti-skid switches off so the aircraft can be brought to a stop and turned
  • Anti-skid reduces take-off (accelerate-stop) and landing distances. With it inoperative, those distances increase and the performance must be corrected.
  • Autobrake applies a chosen deceleration automatically after touchdown (several settings) or full braking in a rejected take-off (RTO setting). It works through the anti-skid. The pilot takes over by pressing the pedals or switching it off.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Tricycle: main wheels behind the CG, about 90% of the weight. Tailwheel: ground loop risk.
  • Oleo: gas is the spring, oil through an orifice damps.
  • Torque links: keep the wheel aligned, stop the piston turning.
  • Emergency extension: uplocks released, gear free-falls, downlocks engage.
  • Three green = down and locked. Red = in transit or disagree.
  • Squat switch: stops gear retraction on the ground.
  • Shimmy: nosewheel oscillation. Cure: shimmy damper.
  • Tyres: nitrogen, checked cold. Loaded +4%, hot after landing +10%.
  • Creep: tyre turns on the rim. Fusible plugs melt to stop a burst.
  • Aquaplaning speed = 9 √P (kt). Non-rotating tyre: lower speed.
  • Hot brakes: approach from the front or rear. Fire: dry powder.
  • Anti-skid: skid control, locked wheel, touchdown protection. Off below about 20 mph.
  • Anti-skid inoperative: longer take-off and landing distances.

Sample questions

5 of the 28 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. On a tricycle landing gear aircraft, the main wheels are placed:

    1. Just ahead of the CG
    2. Just behind the CG
    3. Exactly at the CG
    4. Under the tail
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Behind the CG, carrying about 90% of the weight. This makes the aircraft stable on the ground.

  2. Q2. In a typical emergency gear lowering, the gear comes down by:

    1. Free-fall under its own weight after the uplocks are released
    2. The main hydraulic pump
    3. The pilot pushing it with a lever
    4. The squat switch
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Releasing the uplocks lets gravity and airflow lower the gear, and spring downlocks engage.

  3. Q3. Tyre pressures should be checked:

    1. Immediately after landing
    2. When the tyre is cold
    3. Only after a long flight
    4. With the aircraft jacked up only
    Show answer

    Answer: B. A hot tyre reads high. The rated pressure is for a cold tyre.

  4. Q4. Aquaplaning caused by a locked wheel, with steam formed under the tyre, is called:

    1. Dynamic aquaplaning
    2. Viscous aquaplaning
    3. Reverted rubber aquaplaning
    4. Creep
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Friction heat turns the water to steam and melts the rubber, leaving white marks.

  5. Q5. An anti-skid system that is inoperative will:

    1. Shorten the landing distance
    2. Have no effect on performance
    3. Increase the take-off (accelerate-stop) and landing distances
    4. Only affect the nosewheel
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Without anti-skid the pilot must brake less hard to avoid skids, so the stopping distances grow.
