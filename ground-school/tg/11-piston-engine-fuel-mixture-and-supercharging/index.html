Avgas

100LL and 100 have the same performance number, 100/130, and are told apart by colour.
GradeColourLead
Avgas 100LL (low lead)BlueLow. The common grade today
Avgas 100GreenHigh
Avgas 80RedVery low
Jet A-1 (for comparison)Clear or strawNone. Must never go in a piston engine
  • The octane rating or performance number shows the fuel's resistance to detonation. In 100/130, the first number is the lean (weak) mixture rating and the second the rich mixture rating.
  • Tetraethyl lead (TEL) is added to raise the anti-knock rating. Lead deposits can foul spark plugs, especially at low power with a rich mixture.
  • Use the grade in the flight manual or a higher one. A lower grade risks detonation.
  • Volatility: how easily the fuel turns to vapour. Too volatile gives vapour lock (vapour bubbles block the fuel lines when hot or at altitude) and more carburettor icing. Too little volatility gives hard starting and poor mixing.
  • Avgas density is about 0.72 kg per litre.
  • Check fuel before flight by draining a sample from each drain: right colour, no water (water sits at the bottom as beads or a layer), no dirt.

Detonation and pre-ignition

DetonationPre-ignition
WhatThe last part of the mixture explodes instead of burning smoothly, after the sparkThe mixture is lit before the spark by a hot spot (glowing carbon, a hot plug or valve)
CausesToo low a fuel grade, too lean a mixture, high CHT, high manifold pressure at low rpm, high compressionOverheating, carbon deposits, wrong plugs. Often follows detonation
SignsRough running, rising CHT, power loss. Often cannot be heard in the cockpitRough running, power loss, kick back
CureRichen the mixture, reduce power (manifold pressure), open cowl flaps, lower the nose for more cooling airReduce power and temperature

Both cause sudden pressure and heat that can burn holes in pistons, crack cylinders and break the engine.

Mixture

MixtureAir : fuel by weightUse
Chemically correct (stoichiometric)About 15 : 1All the fuel and oxygen used. Hottest burning
Best power (rich)About 12.5 : 1Take-off and climb. Extra fuel also cools
Best economy (weak)About 16 : 1Cruise
Rich limit / weak limitAbout 8 : 1 / 20 : 1Beyond these the mixture will not burn
  • As the aircraft climbs, the air gets less dense but the carburettor still meters fuel by volume, so the mixture gets richer. The pilot leans it with the mixture control.
  • Too rich: rough running, plug fouling, black smoke, wasted fuel, lower CHT. Too lean: rough running, high CHT, detonation, and the engine can stop.
  • Leaning with EGT: lean until the exhaust gas temperature peaks, then richen to the manual's setting (for example 50°F rich of peak). Without an EGT gauge, lean until rpm peaks and just starts to fall, then richen a little.
  • A weak mixture burns more slowly, so the ignition may need to be advanced.
  • Mixture to idle cut-off (fully lean) is the normal way to stop the engine. It starves the cylinders, so no fuel is left to fire if the propeller is moved.
  • At low airfields in India the mixture is usually full rich for take-off. At hot, high airfields, lean for best power before take-off as the manual says.

The float carburettor

PartJob
Float chamberKeeps a steady fuel level, with a float and needle valve that let in fuel as it is used
Venturi (choke)A narrow throat. Air speeds up there and its pressure drops, which draws fuel from the jet
Main jetMeters the fuel to the venturi
Diffuser (emulsion tube)Mixes air into the fuel so it atomises better and keeps the mixture steady as airflow changes
Throttle valve (butterfly)After the venturi. Controls how much mixture goes to the engine, so controls power
Slow running (idle) jetSupplies fuel near the edge of the nearly closed throttle at idle
Accelerator pumpSquirts extra fuel when the throttle opens quickly, to stop a weak cut (the engine faltering because air speeds up faster than the fuel)
Economiser (power enrichment)Adds extra fuel at high power to keep the engine cool
Mixture controlChanges the fuel flow (or the pressure in the float chamber) to lean the mixture with altitude
  • A float carburettor can stop feeding fuel in negative g, so aerobatic aircraft use fuel injection or special carburettors.
  • A fuel primer puts fuel straight into the inlet for starting.

Carburettor icing

  • Air in the carburettor cools by up to about 30°C: the fuel evaporating takes heat (the biggest effect), and the air expands as its pressure drops in the venturi. Water in the air freezes on the venturi and throttle valve.
  • It can happen with outside temperatures from about -10°C to +30°C, worst in high humidity, even in clear air. It is most likely at low power (descent, approach), with the throttle nearly closed.
  • Signs: fixed pitch propeller: rpm slowly falls. Constant speed propeller: manifold pressure falls, rpm stays steady. Then rough running and the engine may stop.
  • Carburettor heat sends hot, unfiltered air from round the exhaust to the carburettor. The hot air is less dense, so the mixture gets richer and power drops a little (rpm drop).
  • Using carb heat with ice present: rpm drops, may run rough as the melted ice goes through, then rises as the ice clears. With no ice: rpm drops and stays down.
  • Use full carb heat, not partial (partial heat can raise the air temperature into the icing range). Do not use it on the ground for long, as the air is unfiltered and dust can enter the engine.
  • Fuel injection engines are much less prone to icing (no venturi fuel evaporation), but can still get impact ice on the air intake. An alternate air door opens for this.

Memory trick

Carb heat on: rpm drops then rises = there was ice. Rpm drops and stays down = no ice.

Fuel injection

  • Fuel is sprayed into each cylinder's inlet port by nozzles, metered by a fuel control unit.
  • Advantages: less icing, more even fuel distribution between cylinders, better fuel economy and response, works in any attitude.
  • Disadvantages: hot starting can be hard because fuel boils in the lines (vapour lock) after shutdown in hot weather. More complex and costly.

Manifold pressure, superchargers and turbochargers

  • Manifold pressure (MAP) is the pressure in the inlet manifold, in inches of mercury. With the engine stopped, it reads the outside air pressure (about 30 in Hg at sea level). Running, an unboosted engine's MAP is less than outside pressure.
  • As a normally aspirated aircraft climbs, power falls because the air is less dense. On a hot day power is also less (density altitude).
  • A supercharger is a compressor that pushes more air into the cylinders. It is gear driven from the crankshaft, so it takes some engine power.
  • A turbocharger is a compressor driven by a turbine in the exhaust, using energy that would be wasted. A waste gate lets some exhaust bypass the turbine to control the boost. Closing the waste gate sends more exhaust through the turbine and increases boost.
  • With a turbocharger, sea level power can be kept up to the critical altitude: the height at which the waste gate is fully closed. Above it, power falls as in a normal engine.
  • Full throttle height: the height at which full throttle is needed to keep the set manifold pressure.
  • Compressing air heats it, which makes it less dense and raises the risk of detonation. An intercooler cools the charge after the compressor.
  • Overboost (too much MAP) can cause detonation and damage. A relief valve or controller limits it. Open the throttle gently on turbocharged engines.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • 100LL blue, 100 green, 80 red. Same 100/130 for 100 and 100LL.
  • Performance number: lean / rich anti-knock rating. Use the right grade or higher.
  • Detonation: explosion after the spark. Low grade, lean, hot, high MAP at low rpm. Cure: richen, reduce power.
  • Pre-ignition: hot spot lights it before the spark.
  • 15 : 1 chemically correct. 12.5 : 1 best power. About 16 : 1 best economy.
  • Climbing: mixture goes rich, so lean it.
  • Venturi: speed up, pressure down, draws fuel. Accelerator pump: stops a weak cut.
  • Carb ice: -10 to +30°C, high humidity, low power. Fixed pitch: rpm falls. CSU: MAP falls.
  • Carb heat: hot, unfiltered, richer, rpm drop. Ice present: drop then rise.
  • Fuel injection: less icing, better distribution, hot start problems.
  • MAP with engine stopped = outside pressure.
  • Turbocharger: exhaust driven. Waste gate closed = more boost. Critical altitude: waste gate fully closed.
  • Supercharger: gear driven. Intercooler cools the charge.

Sample questions

5 of the 27 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Avgas 100LL is coloured:

    1. Red
    2. Green
    3. Blue
    4. Clear
    Show answer

    Answer: C. 100LL is blue, 100 is green, 80 is red.

  2. Q2. If detonation is suspected in flight, the pilot should:

    1. Lean the mixture and increase power
    2. Richen the mixture and reduce power
    3. Close the cowl flaps
    4. Climb steeply
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Extra fuel cools the charge and less power lowers cylinder pressure and temperature.

  3. Q3. Leaning the mixture using the EGT gauge, peak EGT occurs at about the:

    1. Chemically correct mixture
    2. Rich limit
    3. Weak limit
    4. Best power mixture
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The hottest burning is near the chemically correct ratio. The pilot then sets the mixture rich or lean of peak as the manual says.

  4. Q4. In an aircraft with a fixed pitch propeller, the first sign of carburettor icing is usually:

    1. A rise in rpm
    2. A fall in manifold pressure with steady rpm
    3. A rise in oil pressure
    4. A gradual fall in rpm
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Ice restricts the airflow so power and rpm fall. With a constant speed propeller, MAP falls instead.

  5. Q5. With the engine stopped on the ground, the manifold pressure gauge reads:

    1. Zero
    2. Twice the outside pressure
    3. The outside air pressure
    4. The oil pressure
    Show answer

    Answer: C. With no engine suction, the manifold is at the same pressure as outside, about 30 in Hg at sea level.
