Loads on the structure

LoadWhat it doesMember that resists it
TensionTries to stretch a memberA tie
CompressionTries to shorten or squash a memberA strut
ShearTries to make one face slide over the nextRivets are designed to take shear
BendingTension on the outer (stretched) side, compression on the inner side, and shear acrossSpars, longerons
TorsionTwisting. Tension at the outside, compression inside, and shear acrossThe torsion box
  • Stress is the internal force per unit area, measured in N/mm² or MN/m².
  • Strain is the change in size caused by stress, given as a fraction or percentage of the original size.
  • Elasticity: a material goes back to its original shape when the load is removed. Beyond the elastic limit the deformation is permanent.
  • Buckling: thin sheet under a compressive end load bends out of shape sideways.

Design loads and design philosophy

TermMeaning
Design limit load (DLL)The highest load the aircraft is expected to meet in service. No permanent deformation is allowed up to it
Safety factorUltimate load divided by limit load. At least 1.5
Design ultimate load (DUL)DLL x safety factor. The structure must carry it (for a few seconds) without breaking, though it may deform

DUL = DLL x 1.5 (safety factor)

PhilosophyIdeaIn practice
Safe lifeThe part will not fail within a known lifeReplaced after a set number of hours, landings or pressure cycles, whatever its condition. Used for parts like landing gear
Fail safeIf one member fails, another takes its load (redundancy, more than one load path)Heavier because of the extra members. Damage found at inspection
Damage tolerantLoad is spread over a large area, so a crack grows slowly and is found at a normal inspection before it is dangerousLighter than fail safe. Needs a set inspection programme

Fatigue is weakening of a material by repeated changes of stress. A part can break after many cycles even though each stress was far below its ultimate strength. Each pressurisation and landing is a fatigue cycle. Cracks often start at stress raisers such as holes, sharp corners and scratches.

The fuselage

ConstructionHow it worksUsed on
Truss (framework)Welded steel tubes form a frame that takes the loads. Covered with fabric or light alloyLight, unpressurised aircraft
MonocoqueThe skin carries all the loads. Only light formers give the shape. A small dent badly weakens itSmall aircraft, fuel tanks, engine cowls
Semi-monocoque (stressed skin)Skin plus frames, stringers and longerons share the loads. A damaged area can be carried by the members around itMost aircraft today
PartJob
Frames (formers)Ring shaped. Give the fuselage its shape and take hoop stress. Stringers and skin are fixed to them
StringersLight members running lengthwise. Stiffen the skin so it does not buckle
LongeronsHeavier lengthwise members that take bending loads
BulkheadsLike frames but solid (may have a door). Pressure bulkheads close the pressure hull at the front and rear
FirewallSeparates the engine from the cabin and crew
SkinIn a stressed skin aircraft it is primary structure, carrying much of the load
  • Pressurisation puts the fuselage in tension. Hoop stress tries to make the cross section bigger. Axial (longitudinal) stress tries to make it longer. Hoop stress is about twice the axial stress.
  • A circular cross section carries pressure best, as the load is shared evenly with no flat panels to bulge.
  • Flight deck windows are layers of toughened glass with vinyl interlayers (often five layers), fixed with rubber pressure seals. A conductive coating heats them, which stops ice and fogging and makes them more resilient to bird strikes.
  • The direct vision (DV) window can be opened when the aircraft is unpressurised to give a view if the windscreen is obscured. On some types it is also an emergency exit.
  • Doors that open inwards (plug doors) are pushed into their frame by cabin pressure, so they cannot open in flight.

Wings and tail

PartJob
SparsThe main spanwise members. Take bending (and with the skin, torsion) loads. Bend down on the ground and up in flight
RibsKeep the aerofoil shape, support the skin and stringers against buckling, and pass concentrated loads (engines, gear, flaps) into the spars and skin. Lightening holes save weight and stiffen the rib
StringersSpanwise. Stiffen the skin, especially in compression
SkinTakes the air loads and, between the spars, forms the torsion box
  • A cantilever wing is held at the root only, with no external struts or wires. A braced wing has struts. A biplane is very stiff but has high drag.
  • The largest bending moment is at the wing root.
  • Bending relief: engines on the wing and fuel in the wing put weight where the lift is. So the wing tank fuel is used last.
  • The maximum zero fuel mass (MZFM) limits the load in the fuselage, because with no fuel in the wings there is no bending relief.
  • Tail surfaces: a fixed tailplane (horizontal stabiliser) and fin (vertical stabiliser) with movable elevator and rudder. A V-tail combines both into two surfaces with ruddervators. A T-tail puts the tailplane on top of the fin.

Flutter is a fast, self-feeding vibration of a wing or control surface, caused by the interaction of air loads, the elastic structure and its inertia. It can destroy the structure in seconds. It is prevented by stiff structure and mass balance: a weight ahead of the hinge line so the surface's CG is on or ahead of the hinge. Flutter risk rises with speed.

Materials and locating parts

MaterialWhere and why
Aluminium alloy (such as duralumin, aluminium with copper)Most of the skin, frames and rivets. Light and strong. Alclad sheet has a thin pure aluminium coat against corrosion
TitaniumHot areas and high strength parts, such as engine mounts and firewalls
SteelWhere very high strength is needed: landing gear, bolts, fittings
Composites (carbon or glass fibre in resin)Control surfaces, fairings, and now whole wings and fuselages. Light, strong and do not corrode, but damage can be hidden inside
Used to find structure and parts for maintenance and repair.
ReferenceGives the position
Station (fuselage station)Along the length, in inches from a datum
Water lineHeight above a horizontal datum
Buttock lineLeft or right of the centre line

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Tie resists tension. Strut resists compression. Rivets take shear.
  • Stress = force per unit area (N/mm²). Beyond the elastic limit: permanent set.
  • Ultimate load = limit load x 1.5. Must not break at ultimate load.
  • Safe life: replaced at a set life. Fail safe: more than one load path. Damage tolerant: load spread, cracks found at inspection.
  • Fatigue: repeated stress cycles. Each pressurisation is one cycle.
  • Most aircraft: semi-monocoque (stressed skin). Monocoque: skin takes all the load.
  • Frames: shape and hoop stress. Stringers: stiffen the skin. Bulkheads: solid, pressure bulkheads seal the hull.
  • Pressurised fuselage: circular, hoop and axial stress.
  • Spar: bending and torsion. Rib: shape and support. Max bending at the root.
  • Wing fuel used last for bending relief. MZFM protects the wing root.
  • Flutter: prevented by mass balance ahead of the hinge and stiffness.
  • Station = along, water line = up, buttock line = sideways.

Sample questions

5 of the 28 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. A structural member designed to resist tension is called a:

    1. Strut
    2. Tie
    3. Longeron
    4. Former
    Show answer

    Answer: B. A tie resists tension. A strut resists compression.

  2. Q2. A safe life structure is one that:

    1. Has more than one load path
    2. Is replaced after a set number of hours or cycles, whatever its condition
    3. Is never inspected
    4. Spreads the load over a large area
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Safe life parts are removed when their life is used up, before fatigue failure is expected.

  3. Q3. In monocoque construction:

    1. Steel tubes carry the loads
    2. The skin carries all the loads, with only light formers for shape
    3. Stringers carry all the loads
    4. The floor carries the loads
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Even a small dent weakens a monocoque, so it is used only for small structures.

  4. Q4. The main purpose of a wing spar is to:

    1. Keep the aerofoil shape
    2. Stiffen the skin
    3. Take bending and torsion loads
    4. House the fuel pumps
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Spars are the main spanwise beams. With the skin they form the torsion box.

  5. Q5. The maximum zero fuel mass is set mainly to protect the:

    1. Wing root from too much bending
    2. Landing gear
    3. Engines
    4. Tyres
    Show answer

    Answer: A. With no fuel in the wings, all the payload weight in the fuselage bends the wing root. MZFM limits it.
