Heating and ventilating light aircraft

  • Unpressurised aircraft take ram air through forward facing intakes for fresh air.
  • Heat comes from a heater muff (exhaust shroud): ram air passes over the hot exhaust pipe inside a jacket. A crack in the exhaust can let carbon monoxide into the cabin, which has no smell and can kill. A CO detector is a good idea.
  • Some aircraft use a combustion heater burning engine fuel. It must have an automatic fuel shut-off, overheat cut-out and fire protection.

Air conditioning on transport aircraft

  • The cabin air on most jets comes from engine bleed air (or the APU on the ground). Some aircraft use electric compressors or engine driven cabin blowers instead.
  • Bleed air is far too hot, so it is cooled in packs (air conditioning units).
  • Comfort: cabin temperature about 18 to 24°C, humidity about 30% (air at cruise altitude is very dry, only 1 to 2%). Some aircraft have a humidifier. A water separator takes moisture out of the cold air so it does not mist or freeze in the ducts.
  • Recirculation fans reuse about half the cabin air through filters, saving bleed air and fuel.
  • Trim air: a little hot bleed air is added to the cold pack air for each zone to get the right temperature.
  • Ozone converters break down ozone, which is high at cruise levels and irritates the lungs.
The main cooling in an air cycle machine is from the air doing work as it expands through the turbine.
Cooling systemHow it works
Air cycle machine (bootstrap)Bleed air is cooled in a primary heat exchanger (by ram air), then compressed (it heats up), cooled again in a secondary heat exchanger, and then expanded through a turbine, which cools it a lot. The turbine drives the compressor on the same shaft. A water separator follows
Vapour cycleLike a home fridge: a refrigerant (such as Freon) is compressed, condensed, and evaporated to absorb heat. Used on some smaller aircraft, and on the ground
  • A duct relief valve protects the ducts from too much pressure. Overheat sensors shut the pack if the air gets too hot.
  • Ram air can be used for ventilation if both packs fail, after depressurising.

Why and how the cabin is pressurised

  • Jets fly high for efficiency. The cabin is kept at a lower altitude so people get enough oxygen without masks.
  • Cabin altitude: the pressure altitude inside the cabin. The usual maximum in normal flight is 8,000 ft.
  • Differential pressure: cabin pressure minus outside pressure. The maximum is set by the strength of the fuselage, typically 8 to 9 psi for large jets.
  • Pressure is controlled by keeping the air flowing in steady and changing how fast it is let out through the outflow (discharge) valve. Closing the valve raises cabin pressure (cabin altitude goes down). Opening it lowers cabin pressure.
  • In steady cruise, with steady inflow and steady differential, the outflow valve does not move.
  • Some outflow valves are shaped to give a little thrust as the air leaves.
Controller modeWhat it does
UnpressurisedLow altitude: outflow valve fully open, cabin altitude = aircraft altitude
IsobaricHolds the cabin at a constant cabin altitude while the aircraft climbs. Differential pressure increases
Constant differential (max diff)When maximum differential is reached, holds it. Above this the cabin altitude rises as the aircraft climbs
  • Rates of change are kept low for comfort, mainly for the ears: about 500 ft/min for the cabin climb and 300 ft/min for the cabin descent. A cabin rate of climb indicator shows it.
  • On the ground, a ground (squat) switch keeps the outflow valve open so the aircraft cannot be pressurised with the doors closed. Doors could otherwise blow open.
  • Before landing the crew sets the landing field elevation, so the cabin is at that pressure at touchdown.

Safety valves and decompression

ValveJob
Safety (positive pressure relief) valveOpens if cabin pressure goes above the maximum differential (by about 0.25 psi), to protect the structure
Inward relief (negative pressure relief) valveOpens if outside pressure is higher than cabin pressure (by about 0.5 to 1 psi), for example in a fast descent with the outflow valve shut. The fuselage is not built to take pressure from outside
Dump valveLets the crew depressurise quickly, for example for smoke removal or before an evacuation
Ditching controlCloses the outflow and other valves below the floor line to keep water out after a ditching
  • Cabin altitude warning: a horn or light at a cabin altitude of about 10,000 ft. The crew must put on oxygen masks at once.
  • Passenger masks drop automatically at a cabin altitude of about 14,000 ft (by 15,000 ft).
  • Rapid decompression (a few seconds) or explosive decompression (less than half a second): a loud bang, fog in the cabin as the air cools suddenly, dust and debris flying, ear and chest pain, and the time of useful consciousness becomes very short.
  • Crew action: masks on first, then an emergency descent to 10,000 ft or the minimum safe altitude if higher.
  • Cabin altitude rises and the differential falls to zero during a decompression.

Worked example: How long for the cabin to reach 8,000 ft?

  1. The cabin climbs at 500 ft/min from sea level to 8,000 ft
  2. 8,000 / 500 = 16 minutes
  3. The aircraft rate of climb does not matter for this.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Light aircraft heater muff: a cracked exhaust lets in carbon monoxide.
  • Comfort: 18 to 24°C, humidity about 30%.
  • Air cycle machine: heat exchangers, compressor, then expansion through the turbine cools the air. Turbine drives the compressor.
  • Water separator after the turbine.
  • Max cabin altitude 8,000 ft. Max differential about 8 to 9 psi, set by fuselage strength.
  • Outflow valve closes: cabin pressure up, cabin altitude down.
  • Isobaric: constant cabin altitude. Max diff: constant differential, cabin climbs with aircraft.
  • Cabin climb about 500 fpm, descent about 300 fpm.
  • Safety valve: too much positive differential. Inward relief: negative differential.
  • Dump valve: quick depressurisation. Ditching: close the valves.
  • Cabin altitude warning about 10,000 ft. Pax masks drop about 14,000 ft.
  • Decompression: bang, fog, debris. Masks on, emergency descent.

Sample questions

5 of the 25 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. A crack in the exhaust inside a heater muff on a light aircraft may let into the cabin:

    1. Oxygen
    2. Carbon monoxide
    3. Fuel vapour only
    4. Hydraulic fluid
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Cabin air is heated by passing it over the exhaust. A leak lets in odourless, poisonous CO.

  2. Q2. The comfortable range of cabin temperature is about:

    1. 5 to 10°C
    2. 18 to 24°C
    3. 28 to 34°C
    4. 35 to 40°C
    Show answer

    Answer: B. About 18 to 24°C (65 to 75°F).

  3. Q3. In the isobaric mode of pressurisation:

    1. The differential pressure stays constant
    2. The cabin is not pressurised
    3. The cabin altitude stays constant as the aircraft climbs
    4. The outflow valve is fully open
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Isobaric means constant pressure, so the cabin altitude does not change.

  4. Q4. The inward relief valve opens when:

    1. The cabin pressure is above maximum differential
    2. The packs fail
    3. The cabin altitude reaches 10,000 ft
    4. The outside pressure is higher than the cabin pressure
    Show answer

    Answer: D. The fuselage is not built to take pressure from outside, so negative differential is limited.

  5. Q5. A sign of a rapid decompression is:

    1. A loud bang and fog in the cabin
    2. The cabin getting warmer
    3. Lower cabin altitude
    4. Higher differential pressure
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The sudden drop in pressure cools the air, and the water in it condenses as fog.
