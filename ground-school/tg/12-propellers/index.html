Blade angles and pitch

A propeller blade is a rotating wing. Each section has an aerofoil shape and makes thrust (its lift, pointing forward) and torque (its drag, resisting rotation, which the engine must overcome).

TermMeaning
Blade angle (pitch angle)Angle between the chord line and the plane of rotation
Helix angleAngle between the blade's actual path through the air and the plane of rotation. Set by the forward speed and the rotational speed
Angle of attackAngle between the chord and the relative airflow (the helix). Blade angle = helix angle + AoA
Geometric pitchHow far the propeller would move forward in one turn if it moved like a screw in a solid
Effective pitchHow far it actually moves forward in one turn
SlipGeometric pitch minus effective pitch
  • Blade twist: the blade tip moves much faster than the root. To keep a similar, efficient angle of attack along the whole blade, the blade angle is large at the root and small at the tip.
  • For a fixed blade angle: more forward speed (or fewer rpm) raises the helix angle and lowers the AoA. Less speed or more rpm raises the AoA.
  • At the start of the take-off roll (no forward speed), a fixed pitch propeller's blades are at a high angle of attack and may be partly stalled.
  • A fine pitch (small blade angle) suits low speed and high rpm: take-off and climb. A coarse pitch (large blade angle) suits high speed: cruise.

Fixed pitch propellers

  • The blade angle cannot change. It is a compromise: a climb propeller (finer) gives good take-off and climb, a cruise propeller (coarser) gives better cruise speed.
  • Power is set with the throttle, shown on the rpm gauge. In a dive, rpm increases with speed and can overspeed the engine. In a climb, rpm falls.
  • It is efficient at only one speed. Above and below that speed the AoA moves away from the best value.

Constant speed propellers

  • A variable pitch propeller can change its blade angle. A constant speed propeller does it automatically to hold the rpm the pilot selects.
  • The constant speed unit (CSU) or governor uses flyweights against a speeder spring. If rpm rises above the set value, the flyweights fly out and the blades move coarser (more load), bringing rpm back. If rpm falls, the blades move finer.
  • On most single engine aircraft, governor oil pressure moves the blades coarse, and the blades' own twisting force moves them fine. If oil pressure is lost, the propeller goes to fine pitch (high rpm) so full power is still there. On most twins it is the other way: oil moves them fine, and springs and counterweights move them coarse, so an oil loss feathers the propeller.
  • Cockpit controls: the throttle sets manifold pressure (power). The propeller lever sets rpm.
  • Increasing power: rpm first (propeller lever forward), then manifold pressure (throttle). Reducing power: manifold pressure first, then rpm. This avoids high MAP at low rpm, which can cause detonation and overstress.
  • For take-off: propeller lever fully forward (fine pitch, max rpm). For cruise: reduce to a lower rpm (coarser pitch) for economy.
  • Advantages: the blade stays near its best AoA over a wide speed range, so efficiency is high at take-off, climb and cruise.
  • If the governor cannot keep up (for example at very low speed with low power), the blades go to their fine stop and the propeller acts like a fixed pitch one. This is being off the governor.

Memory trick

Power up: prop then throttle. Power down: throttle then prop. Keep the rpm up whenever the manifold pressure is up.

Feathering, windmilling and reverse

ConditionBlade angleEffect
WindmillingFine, after engine failureThe airflow drives the propeller, which turns the dead engine. Very high drag, the most drag of all
FeatheredAbout 90°, edge on to the airflowThe propeller stops turning. Least drag. Stops further engine damage
Ground fine / betaNear zeroUsed on the ground in turboprops. Little thrust, lots of drag, helps slow down
ReverseNegative angleThrust points backwards. Shortens the landing run
  • Feathering on a twin after an engine failure cuts drag a lot, improving single engine climb and reducing the yaw the pilot must hold. This is why the failed engine is feathered.
  • Unfeathering in flight usually uses an accumulator or electric pump to move the blades out of feather.
  • Auto-feather and autocoarsen systems feather the propeller automatically if an engine fails on take-off.
  • A windmilling propeller on a single engine aircraft gives more drag than a stopped one, which shortens the glide. Many cannot be stopped without slowing close to the stall, so the manual's glide speed allows for it.

Efficiency and design

Propeller efficiency = thrust power / shaft power = (thrust x TAS) / shaft power

  • Good propellers reach about 80 to 85% efficiency. It is zero with no forward speed (thrust but no movement), and falls at high speed as the blade tips approach the speed of sound (shock waves, noise, drag).
  • Tip speed limits propeller diameter and rpm. Large propellers turn slowly through a reduction gear.
  • To absorb more power without a larger diameter: more blades, wider blades (higher solidity), or contra-rotating propellers. More blades also mean less noise and smoother running.
  • A propeller that is iced loses efficiency and can vibrate badly.

Turning effects of the propeller

For a propeller turning clockwise when seen from the cockpit (most Western engines), the effects are:

All four push the nose left on take-off for a clockwise propeller. Right rudder is needed. Anticlockwise propellers do the opposite.
EffectCauseResult (clockwise propeller)
Torque reactionThe engine turns the propeller one way, the aircraft is pushed the other wayRolls left. On the ground, more weight on the left wheel and a swing left
Slipstream (spiral)The spiralling slipstream hits the left side of the finYaws left. Largest at high power, low speed (take-off)
Gyroscopic precessionA force on a spinning disc acts 90° later in the direction of rotationRaising the tail (tailwheel take-off) yaws left. Pitching up yaws right
Asymmetric blade effect (P-factor)At a high AoA, the down-going blade (right side) meets the air at a bigger angle than the up-going blade, so it makes more thrustYaws left in a nose high climb
  • Twins often have the right engine's propeller turning the other way (counter-rotating) so these effects cancel and there is no critical engine (Module 21).
  • On a twin with both propellers turning clockwise, the left engine is the critical engine, as the right engine's down-going blade has the longer arm from the centre line.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Blade angle = helix angle + AoA. Blade angle: chord to plane of rotation.
  • Blade twist: big angle at the root, small at the tip, for a similar AoA along the blade.
  • Fixed pitch, more speed: AoA falls. Fixed pitch, dive: rpm rises, can overspeed.
  • Fine pitch: take-off, climb. Coarse pitch: cruise.
  • CSU: rpm high, blades go coarser. Throttle sets MAP, propeller lever sets rpm.
  • Power up: rpm then MAP. Power down: MAP then rpm.
  • Windmilling: most drag. Feathered (about 90°): least drag, propeller stopped.
  • Efficiency = thrust x TAS / shaft power. Best about 80 to 85%. Tip speed limits it.
  • Clockwise propeller on take-off: torque rolls left, slipstream, gyroscopic and P-factor yaw left.
  • Gyroscopic precession: force acts 90° later in the direction of rotation.

Sample questions

5 of the 26 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The blade angle of a propeller is the angle between the:

    1. Chord line and the relative airflow
    2. Chord line and the plane of rotation
    3. Helix and the plane of rotation
    4. Blade and the hub
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Blade (pitch) angle is chord to plane of rotation. AoA is chord to the relative airflow.

  2. Q2. With a fixed pitch propeller, if the aircraft is put into a dive at constant throttle, the rpm will:

    1. Fall
    2. Rise, and may overspeed the engine
    3. Stay the same
    4. Fall to idle
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The AoA falls, the load on the propeller falls, and the engine speeds up.

  3. Q3. To reduce power on an engine with a constant speed propeller, you should:

    1. Reduce rpm first, then manifold pressure
    2. Feather the propeller
    3. Reduce rpm only
    4. Reduce manifold pressure first, then rpm
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Throttle back first, then the rpm, so MAP is never high with low rpm.

  4. Q4. The failed engine on a twin is feathered mainly to:

    1. Restart it
    2. Save oil
    3. Reduce drag and the yaw the pilot must hold
    4. Cool the engine
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Less drag improves single engine performance and control.

  5. Q5. For a propeller turning clockwise seen from the cockpit, the torque reaction tends to:

    1. Roll the aircraft right
    2. Have no effect
    3. Pitch the nose up
    4. Roll the aircraft left
    Show answer

    Answer: D. The aircraft is pushed the opposite way to the propeller's rotation.
