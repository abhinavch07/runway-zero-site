Fuel tanks

Tank typeWhat it is
Integral (wet wing)Part of the wing structure itself, sealed with sealant between the spars and ribs. Used on most transport aircraft. Light, uses all the space
Rigid (cell)A separate metal tank fitted inside the structure. Light aircraft
Flexible (bladder)A rubber or nylon bag fitted in a cavity. Used where space is awkward
  • Transport aircraft carry fuel in the wings, a centre tank in the fuselage between the wings, and sometimes the tailplane (trim tank) and extra cargo hold tanks.
  • Fuel in the wings gives bending relief. The centre tank is used first and the wing tanks last (Module 1).
  • Baffles (ribs with flap valves) stop the fuel surging from side to side and keep fuel round the pump inlets in turns and manoeuvres.
  • Vents keep the air space at about outside pressure, so the tank does not collapse as fuel is used or in a descent, and does not burst in a climb or during pressure refuelling. A surge (vent) tank near the wing tip catches fuel that flows into the vent lines.
  • A sump and water drain at the lowest point let water and dirt be drained before flight.

Feeding fuel to the engines

AircraftFeed
High wing light aircraftOften gravity feed: fuel flows down to the engine. May have no pump at all
Low wing light aircraftAn engine driven pump, with an electric booster (auxiliary) pump used for take-off, landing, switching tanks and if the engine pump fails
Transport aircraftElectric booster pumps in each tank feed the engine driven pump at a positive pressure
  • Booster pumps are centrifugal, mounted at the bottom of the tank. They give positive pressure to the engine pump inlet, which prevents vapour lock and cavitation at high altitude where the fuel boils more easily. They also allow crossfeeding.
  • If booster pumps fail, the engine can usually suction feed at lower altitudes, but may flame out at high altitude.
  • Crossfeed lets any engine take fuel from any tank, for example to balance fuel between wings or after an engine failure.
  • Tank sequence: the order of using the tanks, set to keep the CG and structure within limits.
  • A low pressure (LP) fuel shut-off valve (fire valve) cuts fuel to the engine in a fire. It is closed by the fire handle.
  • Unusable fuel: the fuel that cannot be drawn from the tanks in flight. It is part of the basic empty mass, not the usable fuel.
  • Fuel heaters (or fuel/oil heat exchangers) warm the jet fuel so ice crystals from dissolved water do not block the filters. A filter bypass light warns of blocking.

Measuring fuel

  • Light aircraft use float type gauges and should check the tanks visually or with a dipstick before flight.
  • Transport aircraft use capacitance probes. Fuel has a different dielectric value from air, so the probe's capacitance changes with the fuel level. Because the dielectric changes with density, the system can show fuel mass (kg or lb), not just volume. A densitometer improves the accuracy.
  • Magnetic level indicators (dripsticks or drip sticks) under the wing let the ground crew measure fuel when the electrical gauges are not working.
  • Fuel flow meters show how much each engine is using, and a totaliser shows fuel used.
  • Fuel expands with heat: the same mass takes more volume when warm. Weight, not volume, matters for performance.

Refuelling and jettison

  • Pressure refuelling: a single point coupling under the wing, at about 50 psi. A panel lets the crew set the load for each tank. High level shut-off valves close each tank when it is full.
  • Over-wing (gravity) refuelling through filler caps on top of the wing is used on light aircraft and as a back-up.
  • Before refuelling, the aircraft and the fuel truck are bonded and earthed so a static spark cannot light the vapour (see Air Regulations Module 2 for the rules).
  • Many large aircraft have a fuel jettison (dump) system to bring the mass down to the maximum landing mass quickly in an emergency. It must stop with a set minimum fuel remaining, and dumping is normally done above about 6,000 ft so the fuel evaporates.

Fuel types

FuelTypeFreezing point
Jet A-1Kerosene. Used worldwide, including IndiaAbout -47°C
Jet AKerosene. Mainly in the USAAbout -40°C
Jet B (wide cut)Kerosene and petrol mix. For very cold climates. More volatile, more fire riskAbout -50°C or lower
Avgas 100LLPetrol for piston engines, dyed blueSee Module 11
  • In long cruise at high altitude the fuel temperature falls. It must stay a few degrees above the freezing point, or wax forms and blocks filters. Crews may descend or speed up (more kinetic heating) to warm it.
  • Additives in jet fuel: anti-icing (FSII) against ice crystals, anti-static, anti-oxidants, and biocides against microbes that grow at the fuel and water boundary and block filters and corrode tanks.
  • Putting jet fuel in a piston engine causes detonation and engine failure. Putting Avgas in a jet engine is allowed only in emergencies or as the manual says, as its lead harms the turbine.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Integral tank: the wing structure is the tank.
  • Baffles: stop surging. Vents: keep the tank near outside pressure, stop collapse or burst.
  • Centre tank first, wing tanks last.
  • Booster pumps: centrifugal, in the tank, stop vapour lock and cavitation.
  • Low wing light aircraft: engine pump plus electric boost pump for take-off and landing.
  • Crossfeed: any tank to any engine, to balance fuel.
  • Unusable fuel is part of the basic empty mass.
  • Fuel heaters stop ice crystals blocking filters.
  • Capacitance gauging measures mass. Dripsticks: manual check.
  • Pressure refuelling: single point, about 50 psi, high level shut-off valves.
  • Jettison: down to max landing mass, stops at a minimum fuel.
  • Jet A-1 freezes about -47°C. Jet B: wide cut, more volatile. Avgas 100LL: blue.

Sample questions

5 of the 23 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. An integral fuel tank is:

    1. A rubber bag in the wing
    2. A separate metal tank
    3. Part of the aircraft structure, sealed to hold fuel
    4. A tank in the cargo hold
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The wing box itself is sealed and becomes the tank, as on most transport aircraft.

  2. Q2. Fuel booster pumps in transport aircraft are usually:

    1. Gear pumps on the engine
    2. Centrifugal pumps at the bottom of the tanks
    3. Hand pumps
    4. Piston pumps in the cockpit
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Submerged centrifugal pumps push fuel to the engine pump under positive pressure.

  3. Q3. Capacitance fuel gauging works because:

    1. Fuel and air have different dielectric values
    2. Fuel floats
    3. Fuel conducts electricity
    4. Fuel is heavier than water
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The probe's capacitance changes with how much of it is in fuel. The system can then show mass.

  4. Q4. During pressure refuelling, each tank stops filling when full because of:

    1. The vent valve
    2. The crossfeed valve
    3. The booster pump
    4. A high level shut-off valve
    Show answer

    Answer: D. It closes automatically as the fuel reaches the full level.

  5. Q5. The fuel in the wing tanks of a transport aircraft is normally used:

    1. First
    2. Only in an emergency
    3. At the same time as the centre tank
    4. Last
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Keeping fuel in the wings reduces wing bending.
