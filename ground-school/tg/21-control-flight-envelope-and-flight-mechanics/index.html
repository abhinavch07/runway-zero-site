How the controls work

  • Moving a control surface changes the camber (and so the lift) of the tailplane, fin or wing section it is on.
  • Elevator: pitch. Its power depends on speed, the tail's AoA (wing downwash changes it) and the CG. With a forward CG, more up elevator is needed, which can limit the flare at low speed. Ice on the tail reduces its power.
  • Rudder: yaw. Needed to balance turns, crosswinds, and asymmetric thrust. A rudder ratio changer or limiter reduces the rudder travel at high speed so it cannot overstress the fin.
  • Ailerons: roll. Large aircraft use outboard ailerons at low speed only, and inboard ailerons plus roll spoilers at high speed.

Reducing control forces

DeviceHow it works
Horn balancePart of the surface sticks out ahead of the hinge, so the air pushes on it to help move the surface
Inset hingeThe hinge is set back from the leading edge of the surface, so the area ahead of it helps
Internal balanceA seal and a plate inside the wing use the pressure difference to help the surface move
Balance tabMoves opposite to the surface, automatically, to reduce the force needed
Anti-balance tabMoves the same way as the surface to increase the force and give feel. Used on stabilators, which would otherwise be too light
Servo tabThe pilot moves only the tab. The tab's air load moves the surface
Spring tabA spring links the control to the tab. At low speed the surface moves directly. At high speed the spring gives and the tab helps, like a servo tab
Trim tabSet by the pilot to remove a steady force (Module 3)
Mass balanceA weight ahead of the hinge. Prevents flutter, does not reduce stick force
  • Powered controls with artificial feel replace these on large aircraft (Module 3).
  • Trim rate is often slower at high speed, so a trim runaway cannot overstress the aircraft quickly.
  • Take-off trim must be set for the CG. Too nose down: hard to rotate. Too nose up: over-rotation and tail strike.

  • Gust loads: a vertical gust changes the wing's AoA suddenly. The load factor rise is bigger at higher speed, and for a lighter aircraft or one with lower wing loading. So in turbulence, slow to the turbulence speed.
  • Design gusts are largest at VB and smaller at VC and VD.

Memory trick

VA goes down as the aircraft gets lighter: a lighter aircraft stalls at a lower speed, so it can be overstressed at a lower speed too.

Climbing and gliding

  • In a steady climb, lift is a little less than weight (L = W cos γ), and thrust is more than drag: the extra thrust holds up the part of the weight acting back along the flight path (W sin γ).
  • Climb angle (gradient) depends on excess thrust (thrust minus drag) divided by weight. Rate of climb depends on excess power divided by weight.
  • Vx: best angle of climb, at the speed of greatest excess thrust. Used to clear obstacles. Vy: best rate of climb, at the speed of greatest excess power. Vx is slower than Vy.
  • As altitude increases, Vx rises and Vy falls (as IAS) until they meet at the absolute ceiling, where the aircraft cannot climb. The service ceiling is where the best rate of climb falls to 100 ft/min (500 ft/min for jets).
  • More weight, higher temperature or altitude, flaps and gear all reduce climb performance. A headwind steepens the climb angle over the ground but does not change the rate of climb.
  • In a glide with no power, the glide angle depends only on the lift/drag ratio. Glide ratio = L/D. Best glide range is at the best L/D speed (VMD).
  • Weight does not change the glide angle or the still air distance, but a heavier aircraft must glide faster to get it, so it arrives sooner.
  • A headwind shortens the glide distance over the ground. Fly a little faster than the best glide speed into a strong headwind.
  • Flaps, gear or a windmilling propeller lower the L/D and steepen the glide.

Worked example: Glide with an L/D of 10 from 6,000 ft

  1. Distance = height x L/D = 6,000 x 10 = 60,000 ft
  2. 60,000 / 6,080 = about 9.9 nm in still air

Turns

  • In a level turn, the lift is tilted by the bank. Its vertical part must still equal the weight, so the total lift must be greater than the weight. The horizontal part pulls the aircraft round the turn (the centripetal force).
  • Load factor n = 1 / cos(bank): 2 at 60°. More lift means more induced drag, so power must be added to hold speed in a steep turn, and the stall speed rises by √n (Module 18).
  • In a balanced turn the slip ball is in the middle. Slip: too little rudder, the aircraft slides into the turn. Skid: too much rudder, it slides out.

Turn radius r = V² / (g x tan bank). Rate of turn = g x tan bank / V

  • At a constant bank, a higher speed gives a larger radius and a lower rate of turn. (Radius goes with V².)
  • At a constant speed, more bank gives a smaller radius and a higher rate.
  • A rate one turn is 3° per second (360° in 2 minutes). The bank needed is about TAS / 10 + 7 degrees.

Worked example: Rate one turn at 120 kt TAS

  1. Bank ≈ 120 / 10 + 7 = 19°
  2. A 180° turn takes 60 seconds

Asymmetric thrust and minimum control speeds

  • If an engine fails on a twin, the live engine's thrust and the dead engine's drag give a strong yaw towards the dead engine. The yaw makes the live wing go faster, so the aircraft also rolls towards the dead engine. The pilot holds it with rudder towards the live engine (dead foot, dead engine) and aileron.
  • The critical engine is the one whose failure is worst. On a twin with both propellers turning clockwise (seen from behind), it is the left engine, because the right engine's down-going blade (P-factor) has the longer arm from the centre line.
  • Banking about 5° towards the live engine reduces the sideslip, so less rudder is needed and drag is less. With wings level, the aircraft flies with a sideslip, and a big sideslip with full rudder can stall the fin.
SpeedMeaning
VMCA (VMC)Minimum control speed in the air: the lowest speed at which the aircraft can be kept straight with the critical engine failed, using full rudder and no more than 5° bank towards the live engine
VMCGMinimum control speed on the ground, using rudder only (no nosewheel steering), keeping within a set distance of the centre line
VMCLMinimum control speed in the landing configuration
  • VMCA is highest with: maximum power on the live engine, an aft CG (shorter rudder arm), low weight, the dead propeller windmilling (not feathered), low altitude and cold air (more thrust), and wings level instead of 5° bank.
  • VMCA falls with altitude, as the live engine gives less thrust. At high altitude the stall may come before VMC, which is very dangerous in training.
  • Never fly slower than VMCA with asymmetric power. If control is lost, reduce power on the live engine.

Emergency descent and windshear

  • Emergency descent (after decompression): thrust idle, speed brakes out, and descend at a high speed, up to VMO/MMO, if the structure is not damaged. If damage is suspected, descend at a lower speed. Some types lower the gear to steepen the descent. Aim for 10,000 ft or the minimum safe altitude.
  • Windshear is a sudden change in wind speed or direction. A headwind increase (or tailwind decrease) raises the IAS and the aircraft goes above the glide path (performance increasing). A headwind decrease (or tailwind increase) lowers the IAS and the aircraft sinks below the path (performance decreasing). The second is dangerous near the ground.
  • In a microburst on the approach, the aircraft first meets a headwind (floats high and fast), then a strong downdraft, then a tailwind (sinks and slows): the most dangerous part.
  • Recovery: maximum thrust, raise the nose (often about 15°, or up to the stick shaker), do not change flap or gear until clear, follow the flight director windshear guidance. Avoid known windshear and thunderstorms.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Adverse yaw: down aileron, more induced drag. Cures: differential, Frise, spoilers, interconnect.
  • Aileron reversal: wing twist at high speed. Inboard ailerons and spoilers.
  • Balance tab: opposite, reduces force. Anti-balance: same way, adds feel (stabilators). Servo tab: pilot moves tab only. Spring tab: helps at high speed.
  • Mass balance: flutter, not force.
  • Limit loads: normal +3.8 / -1.52, utility +4.4, aerobatic +6, transport +2.5.
  • VA = VS √n, falls with less weight. VB: max gust. VNO: green arc top. VNE: red line.
  • Gusts: bigger load factor at higher speed and lighter weight.
  • Climb: lift < weight. Vx: excess thrust, angle. Vy: excess power, rate. Vx < Vy. Meet at the absolute ceiling.
  • Glide angle depends on L/D only. Weight: same distance, higher speed.
  • Level turn: n = 1/cos bank. r = V² / g tan bank. Rate one = 3°/s, bank ≈ TAS/10 + 7.
  • Engine out: yaw and roll towards the dead engine. Critical engine: left for clockwise props.
  • VMCA: max 5° bank, full rudder. Higher with aft CG, max power, windmilling prop, low altitude.
  • Windshear: headwind loss = sink. Microburst: headwind, downdraft, tailwind. Max thrust, pitch up, no config change.

Sample questions

5 of the 30 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Adverse aileron yaw is caused by:

    1. The up-going aileron having more drag
    2. The down-going aileron raising the induced drag on its wing
    3. The rudder
    4. The fin
    Show answer

    Answer: B. More lift on the rising wing means more induced drag there, so the nose yaws away from the turn.

  2. Q2. With a servo tab, the pilot's controls move:

    1. The control surface directly
    2. Only the tab, whose air load then moves the surface
    3. The trim wheel
    4. The hinge
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The surface floats and is driven by the tab.

  3. Q3. An aircraft stalls at 60 kt and its limit load factor is +4. Its VA is about:

    1. 120 kt
    2. 240 kt
    3. 84 kt
    4. 60 kt
    Show answer

    Answer: A. VA = 60 x √4 = 60 x 2 = 120 kt.

  4. Q4. The glide angle of an aircraft with no power depends only on its:

    1. Weight
    2. Altitude
    3. Lift/drag ratio
    4. Wind
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Glide ratio = L/D. Wind changes the distance over the ground but not the angle through the air.

  5. Q5. After an engine failure on a twin, the aircraft yaws and rolls:

    1. Towards the live engine
    2. Nose up
    3. Towards the dead engine
    4. It does not move
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Live engine thrust and dead engine drag yaw it towards the dead side, and the yaw makes it roll.
