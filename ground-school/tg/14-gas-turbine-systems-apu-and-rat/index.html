The engine fuel system and fuel control (FCU and FADEC), ignition, starting and the start sequence, start problems (hot, hung, wet start, tailpipe fire), lubrication, the accessory gearbox, engine instruments (N1, N2, EGT, EPR, fuel flow, vibration), thrust, flat rating and the effect of temperature, altitude and bleed air, the auxiliary power unit and the ram air turbine.

Engine fuel system and control

  • Fuel path: aircraft booster pumps, LP shut-off (fire) valve, engine LP pump, fuel heater (fuel/oil heat exchanger, which also cools the oil), filter, HP pump, fuel control unit (FCU), HP shut-off valve, fuel flow meter, then the spray nozzles.
  • The FCU meters fuel to match the thrust lever position, allowing for air pressure, temperature and rpm. It limits acceleration and deceleration so the engine does not surge or flame out, and protects against overspeed and over-temperature.
  • FADEC (full authority digital engine control): a computer that controls the engine fully, giving automatic starts, protection against limits, and best fuel use. It usually has two channels.
  • The HP fuel shut-off valve is worked by the start lever (fuel control switch) in the cockpit, and is used to start and stop the engine. The LP valve is closed by the fire handle.

Ignition

  • Once lit, a gas turbine flame keeps itself burning, so ignition is needed only for starting and as a safeguard.
  • High energy ignition units store energy in a capacitor and discharge it as a powerful spark through an igniter plug. Each engine has two igniters. The energy is dangerous: never touch the system when it is live.
  • Continuous (or flight) ignition is used when a flameout is possible: take-off and landing, heavy rain, icing, severe turbulence, and bird strike risk. On many engines it switches on automatically.
  • Relight in flight is done within a relight envelope of altitude and speed. A windmilling engine at higher speed may relight on its own ignition. Otherwise the starter helps (starter assisted relight).

Starting

  • Large engines use an air turbine starter (ATS) driven by air from the APU, another running engine (cross bleed) or a ground air cart. Small engines use an electric starter-generator.
  • The starter turns the HP spool (N2) through the accessory gearbox.

Lubrication and gearbox

  • Gas turbines use a self-contained recirculating system with its own tank, pressure pump, several scavenge pumps, filters and an oil cooler. The oil is synthetic (it copes with high bearing temperatures).
  • Oil lubricates and cools the main bearings, the gearboxes and seals.
  • Magnetic chip detectors in the scavenge lines catch metal particles, an early warning of bearing or gear wear.
  • Oil quantity is checked soon after shutdown (usually within about 30 minutes), as oil drains back to the tank. Wait too long and it may read wrongly.
  • Indications: oil pressure, temperature and quantity, low pressure warning and filter bypass.
  • The accessory gearbox, driven from the HP spool, drives the fuel pumps, oil pumps, hydraulic pump, generator (IDG) and is turned by the starter.

  • Thrust falls as air temperature rises (less dense air) and as altitude increases. A hot day, high airfield gives the least take-off thrust.
  • Flat rating: the engine is limited to the same rated thrust up to a set outside temperature (the flat rating temperature, often about ISA + 15°C). Below it the engine could give more, but is held back. Above it, EGT reaches its limit and thrust falls.
  • Taking bleed air (for air conditioning or anti-ice) takes air from the core that would have made thrust. So thrust falls (or for the same thrust, EGT and fuel flow rise).
  • Specific fuel consumption (SFC): fuel used per unit of thrust per hour. Lower is better. High bypass engines have low SFC.
  • Jet engine thrust stays fairly constant with speed, so its power (thrust x speed) increases with speed. A propeller's thrust falls with speed.

APU and RAT

  • The auxiliary power unit (APU) is a small gas turbine (often with a centrifugal compressor) usually in the tail cone. It gives electrical power (its own generator) and bleed air for engine starting and air conditioning, on the ground and in flight (within limits).
  • It means the aircraft does not need ground power or air carts, and gives a back-up generator in flight.
  • It runs at a constant speed (about 100%) whatever the load, controlled by its own ECU. The crew sees few indications.
  • It shuts down automatically for problems such as fire, overspeed, low oil pressure, high EGT or loss of control. On the ground an APU fire usually also fires the extinguisher automatically, and a horn sounds in the nose wheel bay.
  • APU bleed air is not normally used above a set altitude, as the thin air limits it. The electrical generator can be used higher.
  • The ram air turbine (RAT) is a small propeller that drops out of the fuselage or wing into the airflow in an emergency.
  • It deploys automatically if both engines fail or all main AC power or hydraulic power is lost, and can be deployed by hand.
  • It drives a hydraulic pump and/or an electrical generator, giving enough power for the flight controls and essential instruments. It needs airspeed to work, so its power falls as the aircraft slows.
  • Once deployed, it normally cannot be stowed in flight.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • FCU or FADEC: meters fuel, limits acceleration, protects limits.
  • Fuel heater (fuel/oil heat exchanger): stops ice crystals, cools oil.
  • Two high energy igniters per engine. Continuous ignition: take-off, landing, heavy rain, icing, turbulence.
  • Air starter turns N2. Fuel and ignition at about 15 to 25% N2. Starter cuts out about 50%.
  • Hot start: EGT too high, fuel off. Hung start: stuck below idle. Wet start: no light-up, dry motor.
  • Tailpipe fire: fuel off, dry motor.
  • Oil: self-contained, synthetic. Chip detectors show wear. Check quantity soon after shutdown.
  • N1 = fan speed, EPR = jet pipe / intake pressure. EGT is the key limit.
  • Thrust falls with higher temperature and altitude. Bleed air on: thrust down or EGT up.
  • Flat rated: constant thrust up to a set OAT, then falls as EGT hits its limit.
  • APU: small gas turbine, electric power and bleed air, auto shutdown for fire and limits.
  • RAT: drops out automatically if engines or power are lost. Hydraulic and electric. Needs airspeed.

Sample questions

5 of the 26 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The main job of the fuel control unit (FCU) of a jet engine is to:

    1. Pump fuel from the tanks
    2. Meter fuel to match the thrust demanded while keeping within limits
    3. Heat the fuel
    4. Start the engine
    Show answer

    Answer: B. It schedules fuel with pressure, temperature and rpm, and limits acceleration to prevent surge.

  2. Q2. On a large turbofan, the air turbine starter turns the:

    1. Fan (N1)
    2. HP spool (N2)
    3. Propeller
    4. APU
    Show answer

    Answer: B. It drives the HP spool through the accessory gearbox.

  3. Q3. The correct action for a tailpipe fire during a start is to:

    1. Fire both extinguisher bottles at once
    2. Switch on continuous ignition
    3. Open the thrust lever
    4. Shut off fuel and dry motor the engine to blow out the flames
    Show answer

    Answer: D. The fire is in the jet pipe, so airflow is the best tool. The extinguisher goes into the nacelle, not the jet pipe.

  4. Q4. The most important temperature limit on a jet engine is the:

    1. Oil temperature
    2. Fuel temperature
    3. Exhaust or turbine gas temperature
    4. Intake temperature
    Show answer

    Answer: C. EGT or TGT protects the turbine blades from overheating and creep.

  5. Q5. The APU on a transport aircraft provides:

    1. Electrical power and bleed air, on the ground and in flight within limits
    2. Thrust for take-off
    3. Hydraulic power only
    4. Fuel
    Show answer

    Answer: A. It makes the aircraft independent of ground power and air carts.
