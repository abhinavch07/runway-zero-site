How a gas turbine works

  • A jet engine takes in air, compresses it, adds fuel and burns it, and lets the hot gas expand through a turbine (which drives the compressor) and out of a nozzle. The engine pushes a mass of air backwards, so the engine is pushed forwards (Newton's third law).
  • The gas turbine works on the Brayton cycle: induction, compression, combustion and exhaust happen continuously, at the same time, in different parts of the engine. Combustion happens at (nearly) constant pressure. A piston engine's Otto cycle burns at constant volume.
  • Unlike a piston engine, the gas turbine does each job in its own section all the time, which makes it smooth and powerful for its weight.

Thrust = mass flow x (jet velocity - aircraft velocity), plus any pressure thrust at the nozzle

  • Thrust can come from moving a small mass very fast (turbojet) or a large mass a little faster (turbofan, propeller). The second way is much more efficient at normal airliner speeds.
  • Momentum drag: the air taken in already moves at the aircraft's speed, so as speed rises, the gain in velocity (and thrust) falls. Ram effect at higher speed partly makes up for it.

Propulsive efficiency = 2V / (V + Vj), V = aircraft speed, Vj = jet speed

Worked example: Aircraft at 300 mph, jet at 900 mph

  1. 2 x 300 / (300 + 900) = 600 / 1,200
  2. Propulsive efficiency = 50%
  3. The closer the jet speed is to the aircraft speed, the higher the efficiency.

  • Bypass ratio = mass of air going round the core / mass going through the core. Low bypass: about 1 or 2 : 1. High bypass: 5 : 1 or more (modern engines about 10 : 1 or higher).
  • High bypass engines have better propulsive efficiency, lower specific fuel consumption and much less noise.
  • Spools: a twin spool engine has a low pressure (LP) compressor driven by the LP turbine through an inner shaft (N1), and a high pressure (HP) compressor driven by the HP turbine through an outer shaft (N2). The spools turn at their own best speeds, which helps against stall. The fan is part of the N1 spool.

Intakes

  • The intake must deliver air to the compressor smoothly and at a steady speed, at all angles of attack and crosswinds.
  • A subsonic pitot intake is a divergent duct: it slows the air and raises its pressure before the compressor (ram compression). The compressor face must not meet supersonic air.
  • Supersonic aircraft use convergent-divergent intakes with shock waves (often adjustable ramps) to slow the air below Mach 1.
  • Disturbed or turbulent intake air (crosswinds, high AoA, ice, damage) can make the compressor stall or surge.
  • Foreign object damage (FOD): stones, ice or birds sucked in. Keep power low while taxiing and in reverse thrust. A vortex forms from the ground in front of the intake at high power and low speed and can lift debris.

  • In an axial compressor the annulus gets smaller towards the back, because the air becomes denser. This keeps the axial velocity about steady.
  • Compressor stall: the airflow separates from compressor blades because their angle of attack is too high (or too low). It may affect a few blades or a stage.
  • Surge: a complete breakdown of airflow through the compressor. The flow may even reverse, with a bang, flames from the intake or exhaust, high EGT, loss of thrust, vibration and yaw.
  • Causes: turbulent intake air, too fast throttle movement, ice, FOD, worn or dirty blades, wrong fuel scheduling, bleed valves stuck closed.
  • Action for a surge: slowly close the thrust lever of that engine (do not slam it), then follow the checklist.

Combustion

  • Air leaves the compressor too fast to burn in (a flame would blow out), so a diffuser slows it first.
  • Only about 20% of the air (primary air) goes into the flame zone, where it mixes with fuel at about the chemically correct ratio (15 : 1). Swirl vanes slow the air and make it recirculate so the flame stays lit.
  • The rest (secondary and dilution air) cools the chamber walls and dilutes the hot gas, bringing it down from about 2,000°C to a temperature the turbine can stand (about 1,000 to 1,500°C). The overall air to fuel ratio is very weak (about 45 to 130 : 1).
  • If the mixture is too rich or too weak for the airflow, the flame goes out: a flameout. There is a rich and weak extinction limit, which gets narrower at high altitude and high speed.

Turbines and exhaust

  • Nozzle guide vanes (NGVs) before each turbine stage form convergent passages that speed up the gas and direct it onto the blades at the right angle.
  • Turbine blades may be impulse (gas pushes them), reaction (they act as small nozzles) or, most commonly, impulse-reaction (impulse at the root, reaction at the tip).
  • Turbine blades work in the hottest gas under huge centrifugal load, so they can creep (slowly stretch) and must be cooled by air through internal passages. They may be single crystal metal. Shrouds at the tips cut leakage and vibration.
  • Turbine gas temperature (TGT) or EGT is the most important engine limit. It is measured by thermocouples.
  • The exhaust cone and jet pipe straighten and smooth the flow. The propelling nozzle is convergent on subsonic engines, which speeds up the gas. At high power it may be choked (gas at the speed of sound), giving pressure thrust as well.
  • Noise comes mainly from the fast jet mixing with still air. It is reduced by high bypass ratio, lobed (corrugated) mixers, chevron nozzles and acoustic linings.
  • Afterburning (reheat): burning extra fuel in the jet pipe for a large thrust boost, at very high fuel use. Military aircraft.
  • Water (or water methanol) injection: injected into the compressor inlet or combustion chamber to restore thrust on hot days and at high airfields by cooling the air and adding mass flow.

Thrust reversers

  • Reversers turn the gas flow forwards to help slow the aircraft after landing, saving brakes and shortening the landing on wet or icy runways.
  • Clamshell or bucket (target) doors close behind the jet and turn the hot gas forwards. Used on low bypass engines.
  • Cascade reversers on high bypass engines: blocker doors close the fan duct, and the fan air goes out through cascade vanes angled forwards. Only the fan air is reversed.
  • Reverse is most effective at high speed. It is reduced to idle reverse by about 60 to 80 kt so hot gas and debris are not re-ingested, which can cause surge and FOD.
  • Reversers have locks and interlocks so they can only deploy on the ground (squat switch) with the thrust levers at idle. Unwanted deployment in flight gives a big yaw and loss of lift.
  • Reverse thrust is not counted in normal dry runway landing distance calculations.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Newton's third law. Brayton cycle: combustion at constant pressure, all strokes continuous.
  • Thrust = mass flow x (Vj - V). Propulsive efficiency = 2V / (V + Vj).
  • Bypass ratio: bypass air / core air. High bypass: efficient, quiet.
  • Twin spool: N1 = LP (fan), N2 = HP.
  • Subsonic intake: divergent, slows air, raises pressure.
  • Centrifugal: about 4 : 1, robust, big frontal area. Axial: high overall ratio, efficient, small frontal area.
  • Axial annulus converges to keep axial velocity steady.
  • Stall: blade AoA wrong. Surge: total breakdown of flow, bang, high EGT. Close the lever slowly.
  • Bleed valves open at low rpm and acceleration. Stuck closed: stall.
  • About 20% primary air burns. Rest cools and dilutes.
  • Annular chamber: shortest, lightest.
  • NGVs: convergent, speed up the gas. Turbine blades creep.
  • Highest pressure: compressor outlet. Highest temperature: combustion. Highest velocity: nozzle.
  • Cascade reverser: fan air only. Cancel reverse by about 60 to 80 kt.

Sample questions

5 of the 29 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. A gas turbine engine works on the:

    1. Otto cycle
    2. Diesel cycle
    3. Brayton cycle
    4. Rankine cycle
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The Brayton (constant pressure) cycle, with each process going on continuously.

  2. Q2. The bypass ratio of a turbofan is the ratio of:

    1. Fan rpm to core rpm
    2. Air going round the core to air going through the core
    3. Thrust to weight
    4. Fuel to air
    Show answer

    Answer: B. A high bypass engine sends much more air round the core than through it.

  3. Q3. The annulus of an axial compressor gets smaller towards the rear in order to:

    1. Keep the axial velocity of the air about constant as its density rises
    2. Save weight
    3. Raise the temperature
    4. Reduce the number of blades
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Denser air needs a smaller passage to keep moving at the same speed.

  4. Q4. Variable inlet guide vanes help prevent stall by:

    1. Cooling the air
    2. Opening the bleed valves
    3. Adding fuel
    4. Changing the angle at which air meets the first compressor blades
    Show answer

    Answer: D. They give the air the right swirl for the rpm, keeping the blade angle of attack correct.

  5. Q5. In a gas turbine, the highest gas velocity occurs:

    1. At the intake
    2. In the combustion chamber
    3. At the propelling nozzle
    4. At the compressor
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The convergent nozzle accelerates the gas to produce thrust.
