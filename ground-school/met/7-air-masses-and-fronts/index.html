What an air mass is and where it comes from, how it changes as it travels, the air masses that reach India, and the warm, cold, occluded and stationary fronts with the clouds, weather and wind changes each one brings.

What an air mass is

An air mass is a huge body of air, thousands of km across, in which temperature and humidity are nearly the same at each level across the whole area. It gets these properties by sitting for days over a large, even surface: a cold continent, a warm ocean, a hot desert.

Good source regions are wide, flat areas with light winds, so the air stays long enough to take on the surface's character. These are mostly the big high pressure areas: the polar regions, the subtropical highs over the oceans, and the winter highs over Siberia and Canada.

How an air mass changes as it travels

As an air mass moves, the surface below warms or cools it from beneath and adds or removes moisture. The key question: is the air colder or warmer than the ground it is moving over?

Cold air over a warmer surfaceWarm air over a colder surface
Heated from below, so unstableCooled from below, so stable, often with an inversion
Cumulus and CB, showers, thunderstormsStratus, stratocumulus, drizzle, fog
Good visibility outside showersPoor visibility: haze, mist, fog
Bumpy, gustySmooth, light winds near the ground

Memory trick

Cold on warm: clouds go up. Warm on cold: clouds stay down.

Air masses over India

  • Winter: dry continental air from the north and north west covers most of India. It starts as very cold polar continental air over Siberia but warms a great deal crossing Central Asia and the Himalayas. It brings clear skies, dry air, cold nights and poor visibility in haze and fog.
  • After a western disturbance passes, a fresh push of this cold continental air can give a cold wave over north and north west India.
  • Pre-monsoon: very hot, dry continental tropical air over north west India and Pakistan, with moist sea air creeping in along the coasts.
  • Monsoon: very moist equatorial maritime air crosses the equator from the southern Indian Ocean and reaches India as the south west monsoon. North of the monsoon trough, north east India also gets moist tropical air from the Pacific side.

Old exam answer

One prescribed book calls India's main winter air mass "tropical continental air from the Siberian high". Air from the Siberian high is polar continental air, much modified on the way. If an exam asks which air mass gives the most extreme cold, the answer is polar continental (cP).

Fronts

A front is the narrow boundary zone between two air masses of different temperature (and so different density). The frontal surface slopes, with the cold, dense air underneath like a wedge. The front is the line where this surface meets the ground. Crossing a front, you find changes in temperature, dew point, wind, pressure tendency, cloud and weather. A wind shift is the one change you always get.

  • Frontogenesis: a front forming or getting stronger as the temperature contrast grows.
  • Frontolysis: a front weakening and dying away as the contrast fades.
  • In the tropics there is little temperature contrast, so real fronts are rare. A tropical low is told apart from a mid latitude low by having no fronts.

The warm front

At a warm front, warm air is advancing and riding up over the retreating cold air. The slope is gentle, about 1 in 150, so the cloud spreads a very long way ahead of the surface front. Warm fronts move slowly, about 10 to 15 kt.

  • Cloud sequence as it approaches: Ci → Cs → As → Ns, lowering and thickening. Cirrus can be seen up to about 1,000 km ahead. The halo in Cs is an early sign.
  • Rain begins from As and Ns some 300 to 400 km ahead, light at first, becoming steady and continuous.
  • Just ahead of the front: low stratus, drizzle, frontal fog, very poor visibility.
  • If the rising warm air is unstable, CBs can be hidden (embedded) in the layer cloud.
  • In winter, rain falling into the cold air below can be freezing rain, with severe icing.

The cold front

At a cold front, cold air is advancing and pushing under the warm air, lifting it sharply. The slope is steep, about 1 in 50 to 1 in 100, so the weather is in a narrow band, but it is violent. Cold fronts move about twice as fast as warm fronts.

  • CB, heavy showers, thunderstorms, hail, squalls, severe turbulence and windshear along the front.
  • A squall line of thunderstorms can form ahead of a fast moving cold front, often 100 to 300 km in front of it.
  • Behind the front: the sky clears quickly, then cumulus and isolated CB with showers in the cold air, and excellent visibility.

Ana and kata fronts. At an ana front the warm air is rising along the frontal surface: plenty of cloud and rain, an active front. At a kata front the warm air above is sinking: thinner cloud and much less weather. Either a warm or a cold front can be ana or kata.

The warm sector (the warm air between the warm and cold fronts) is mild and humid, with low stratus or stratocumulus, drizzle and poor visibility, especially over a cold surface.

Memory trick

In the NH the wind always veers as any front passes. Remember "veer at the front, back before it".

Occluded and stationary fronts

Because the cold front moves faster, it catches up with the warm front and lifts the warm sector air off the ground. The combined front is an occluded front (occlusion). Occlusion happens because the cold front moves faster.

TypeWhat happensWeather
Cold occlusionThe air behind the cold front is colder than the air ahead of the warm front, so it undercuts bothWarm front weather first, then cold front type showers. More common in summer
Warm occlusionThe air behind the cold front is less cold than the air ahead, so it rides up over itMostly warm front type weather. More common in winter, when the air ahead is very cold continental air

An occluded front can be very active where it is young, with embedded CBs in widespread layer cloud. The western disturbances that reach India are usually in this occluded stage.

A stationary front hardly moves. The winds on both sides blow parallel to the front, in opposite directions. Its weather is like a weak warm front and can sit over the same area for days.

The life of a frontal depression

  • It starts as a wave on the polar front, the boundary between cold polar air and warm subtropical air in mid latitudes.
  • The wave deepens into a low with a warm front ahead, a cold front behind, and a warm sector between them.
  • The depression moves roughly in the direction of the isobars in the warm sector.
  • The cold front catches up and the low occludes. It deepens most just as occlusion begins, then slowly fills.
  • New waves often form one after another on the trailing cold front, giving families of depressions.

Other fronts: the Arctic front further north, and the Mediterranean front in winter between cold European air and warm North African air. Lows forming on the Mediterranean front are among the parents of India's western disturbances (Module 10).

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Air mass: huge, uniform temperature and humidity in the horizontal. Source regions: highs, light winds, uniform surface.
  • Named by temperature (A, P, T, E) and moisture (c dry, m moist). Land at low latitude: cT. Land in polar regions: cold and dry (cP).
  • Extreme cold: polar continental (cP).
  • Cold air over warm surface: unstable, Cu and CB, showers, good visibility. Warm over cold: stable, St, drizzle, fog, poor visibility.
  • Front: boundary between air masses of different density. Always a wind shift.
  • Tropical lows have no fronts.
  • Warm front: slope 1 in 150, slow, Ci Cs As Ns St, long steady rain, frontal fog, embedded CB if unstable.
  • Cold front: slope 1 in 50 to 100, about twice as fast, CB, squalls, squall line ahead, clears behind with showers and good visibility.
  • At both fronts in NH the wind veers. Pressure stops falling at a warm front, rises sharply after a cold front.
  • Warm sector: mild, humid, St, drizzle, poor visibility.
  • Occlusion happens because the cold front moves faster. Cold occlusion: cold air undercuts. Warm occlusion: it rides over.
  • Stationary front: winds parallel to the front.
  • Ana: warm air rising, active. Kata: warm air sinking, weak.
  • Depressions form as waves on the polar front and move with the warm sector isobars.

What an air mass is

  • Huge body of air, same temperature and humidity across it
  • Formed over highs with light winds
  • Named by temperature and moisture

The letters

  • A Arctic, P Polar, T Tropical, E Equatorial
  • c continental (dry), m maritime (moist)
  • cP: extreme cold. cT: hot and dry
  • mT: warm and moist. mE: hot, humid, unstable

Changing on the way

  • Cold air over warm ground: unstable
  • Cu, CB, showers, good visibility
  • Warm air over cold ground: stable
  • St, drizzle, fog, poor visibility

India's air masses

  • Winter: dry continental air from the north west
  • Cold wave after a WD passes
  • Pre-monsoon: hot dry cT over the north west
  • Monsoon: moist equatorial air from the south

Fronts

  • Boundary between air masses of different density
  • Cold air is the wedge underneath
  • Always a wind shift across it
  • Tropical lows have no fronts

Warm front

  • Warm air rides over cold air
  • Gentle slope, 1 in 150, slow
  • Ci, Cs, As, Ns, St
  • Long steady rain, frontal fog, maybe embedded CB

Cold front

  • Cold air pushes under warm air
  • Steep, 1 in 50 to 100, about twice as fast
  • CB, squalls, hail, squall line ahead
  • Clears behind, showers, great visibility

As the front passes (NH)

  • Wind veers at both
  • Warm front: pressure stops falling, temperature rises
  • Cold front: pressure jumps up, temperature drops
  • Warm sector: St, drizzle, poor visibility

Occlusions

  • Cold front catches the warm front
  • Cold occlusion: cold air undercuts
  • Warm occlusion: rides over
  • Embedded CBs in layer cloud

Stationary front

  • Hardly moves
  • Winds parallel to the front, opposite ways
  • Weak warm front type weather for days

Frontal depressions

  • Wave on the polar front
  • Warm front, warm sector, cold front
  • Moves with the warm sector isobars
  • Occludes, then fills

Sample questions

5 of the 42 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. An air mass is best described as a large body of air with:

    1. Uniform temperature and humidity in the horizontal
    2. The same wind everywhere
    3. The same pressure everywhere
    4. Constant temperature with height
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Its temperature and moisture are nearly the same across the whole area at any given level.

  2. Q2. Warm moist air moving over a colder surface is likely to give:

    1. Thunderstorms and good visibility
    2. Strong gusty winds
    3. Low stratus, drizzle and fog with poor visibility
    4. Clear skies
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Cooling from below makes the air stable and can bring it to saturation near the ground.

  3. Q3. The average slope of a warm front is about:

    1. 1 in 10
    2. 1 in 50
    3. 1 in 150
    4. 1 in 1,000
    Show answer

    Answer: C. A warm front is gentle, about 1 in 150, which is why its cloud spreads so far ahead. A cold front is 1 in 50 to 100.

  4. Q4. Which weather is typical of an active cold front?

    1. Ci, Cs and halo
    2. CB, heavy showers, thunder, squalls in a narrow belt
    3. Long periods of drizzle
    4. Radiation fog
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The steep, fast lifting gives violent convective weather in a narrow band.

  5. Q5. A cold occlusion forms when:

    1. The air behind the cold front is colder than the air ahead of the warm front and undercuts it
    2. Warm air undercuts cold air
    3. The warm front overtakes the cold front
    4. The front stops moving
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The coldest air goes underneath. If the air behind is less cold and rides over, it is a warm occlusion.
