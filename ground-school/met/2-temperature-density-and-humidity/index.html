Heat and temperature

Heat is energy: the total energy of movement of all the molecules in something. Temperature is how hot or cold it is: the average energy of its molecules. A full bucket of warm water has more heat than a cup of boiling water, even though the cup is at a higher temperature.

ScaleWater freezesWater boilsConvert
Celsius (°C)0100Used in aviation and science
Fahrenheit (°F)32212F = (9/5 × C) + 32
Kelvin (K)273373K = C + 273. 0 K is absolute zero

Memory trick

-40 °C = -40 °F. It is the one temperature where both scales read the same, a favourite exam question.

Specific heat is the heat needed to warm 1 gram of something by 1 °C. Water has a very high specific heat (1), land only about 0.2. So land heats up and cools down much faster than water. This one fact explains sea breezes, the monsoon and why coastal places have mild days and nights.

How temperature is measured

Surface air temperature is measured in the shade, in a Stevenson screen: a white wooden box with louvred sides so air flows through but sunlight does not. The thermometers sit about 1.25 m (4 ft) above the ground, over grass. The door opens on the side facing the pole (north, in India), so the sun never shines in when it is opened.

InstrumentWhat it does
Dry bulb thermometerOrdinary air temperature
Wet bulb thermometerBulb wrapped in wet muslin. Reads lower in dry air. Used with the dry bulb to find humidity
Maximum thermometerMercury, with a narrow kink so it stays at the highest reading
Minimum thermometerAlcohol, with a small index that is pulled back to the lowest reading
ThermographBimetallic strip that draws a continuous record
RadiosondeBalloon borne. Measures temperature, humidity and pressure at upper levels

How the sun heats the earth

Every object gives out radiation. The hotter it is, the more it gives out and the shorter the wavelength. (These are Stefan's law: energy goes up with the fourth power of absolute temperature, and Wien's law: wavelength is shorter for a hotter body.)

  • The sun, at about 6000 °C, sends short wave radiation: about half infrared, a bit less than half visible light, and a small part ultraviolet.
  • The earth, being much cooler, gives out long wave (infrared) radiation, day and night. At night this is called nocturnal radiation.
  • Air lets most short wave sunlight pass straight through. But water vapour, CO₂ and pollution absorb long wave radiation from the earth. This is how the air gets most of its heat.

So the troposphere is heated mainly from below: by long wave radiation from the earth, and by conduction and convection from the warm ground.

Insolation is the amount of sunlight received on an area. It is greatest when the sun is overhead (rays hit straight on) and least when the sun is low. So the tropics get the most and the poles the least.

Albedo is the share of sunlight a surface reflects. Fresh snow has the highest albedo, reflecting about 80% or more. Thick cloud also reflects a lot. The earth as a whole reflects about 30%.

Heat budget, rounded figures used in the books
What happens to the sun's radiationAbout
Reflected or scattered back to space (by cloud, air and ground)30%
Absorbed by the air, water vapour, dust, ozone and cloud20%
Absorbed by the earth's surface50%

Over a year the earth gives back to space as much heat as it receives, so its average temperature stays nearly steady.

How heat moves in the atmosphere

ProcessHow it worksWhere it matters
RadiationEnergy travels as waves, needs no mediumSun to earth, earth to air and space

Memory trick

Convection is up and down. Advection is across. "Ad" like "add": wind adds air from another place.

Temperature through the day

  • The ground keeps losing heat all night, so the minimum temperature comes just after sunrise, about half an hour to an hour after.
  • The sun peaks at noon, yet for some hours afterwards the ground still takes in more heat than it gives out. The maximum comes about 2 to 3 hours after noon, around 1400 to 1500 local time.
Big day to night range whenSmall range when
Sky is clearSky is cloudy, especially low cloud
Wind is calmWind is strong (it mixes the air)
Over land, deserts, dry airOver the sea (under 1 °C) or very humid air
Wind blowing off the land at a coastWind blowing off the sea at a coast

Cloudy nights are warmer. Cloud absorbs the long wave heat from the ground and sends part of it back down. By day the same cloud blocks sunshine. So cloud cuts both the maximum and the minimum. The lower and thicker the cloud, the stronger the effect.

Temperature with height

The real fall of temperature with height on a given day is the environmental lapse rate (ELR). It averages about 6.5 °C per km (2 °C per 1000 ft), which is why ISA uses that figure, but on any day it can be more or less.

  • Inversion: temperature rises with height. Very stable. It traps smoke, haze and fog below it, so visibility under an inversion is often poor.
  • Isothermal layer: temperature stays the same with height. Also stable.
Type of inversionHow it forms
Radiation (surface) inversionGround cools on a clear, calm night and chills the air touching it
Subsidence inversionAir sinking in a high pressure area warms as it is squeezed. Found aloft
Frontal inversionWarm air lies over cold air along a front
Turbulence inversionStrong mixing in the lowest layer leaves warmer air above the mixed layer

Inversions as a flight hazard (windshear at the top of a night inversion, trapped pollution) are covered again in Module 12.

Air density

Density is the mass of air in a given volume, in kg/m³ (ISA at MSL: 1.225 kg/m³). It comes from the gas law: density = pressure ÷ (gas constant × absolute temperature). So:

If this goes upDensityWhy
PressureGoes upAir is squeezed into less space
TemperatureGoes downWarm air expands
HumidityGoes down (a little)A water vapour molecule is lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen it replaces
HeightGoes downPressure falls faster than temperature

At equal pressure and temperature, moist air weighs less than dry air. Many students expect the opposite, so it is a common exam trap.

A handy rule: density drops by about 1% for a pressure fall of 10 hPa, or a temperature rise of 3 °C, or a climb of 300 ft. Low down it falls about 3% for every 1000 ft, and it is about half the MSL value at about 6.5 km (22,000 ft).

From place to place: at the surface, cold polar air is denser than warm equatorial air. But higher up this turns round. At about 8 km density is nearly the same everywhere, and above 8 km the air over the equator is denser than over the poles. (It ties in with Module 1: up there the equator is colder.)

Through the day: density is lowest in the afternoon (hottest) and highest around sunrise (coolest). Lines joining places of equal density are called isopycnals (some books say isosteres).

Density altitude and performance

Density altitude is the height in the ISA at which you would find the density you have now. It tells you how the aircraft will "feel". High density altitude means low density, and the aircraft performs as if it were at a higher airfield.

  • Wings make less lift and engines make less power and thrust.
  • Longer take-off and landing runs, because the true airspeed for the same indicated airspeed is higher.
  • Poorer rate of climb.

Hot, high and humid is the worst case. Each makes the air thinner. In ISA, density altitude equals pressure altitude. For every 1 °C warmer than ISA, density altitude is about 120 ft higher than pressure altitude.

Worked example: Delhi in May: pressure altitude 800 ft, OAT +44 °C. Find the density altitude.

  1. ISA at 800 ft = 15 - (0.8 × 2) = about +13 °C
  2. ISA deviation = 44 - 13 = +31
  3. Extra height = 31 × 120 = 3,720 ft
  4. Density altitude = 800 + 3,720 = about 4,500 ft. The aircraft performs as if the runway were 4,500 ft up

If pressure rises and temperature stays the same, the air gets denser, so the density altitude goes down.

Old exam answer

One of the prescribed books states that density altitude increases when pressure increases at constant temperature. That is a slip. Higher pressure means denser air and a lower density altitude, and the book's own question key gives "lowers". Go with "lowers".

Water changing state: latent heat

Water exists in the air as gas (water vapour), liquid (cloud drops, rain) and solid (ice, snow, hail). Latent heat is the heat taken in or given out when water changes state, without any change in its temperature.

ChangeNameLatent heat
Ice → waterMeltingAbsorbed (taken from the air, so it cools)
Water → vapourEvaporationAbsorbed
Ice → vapourSublimationAbsorbed
Vapour → waterCondensationReleased (warms the air)
Water → iceFreezingReleased
Vapour → iceDeposition (older books also call it sublimation)Released

Memory trick

Going "up" from ice to water to vapour, the water takes in heat. Coming "down", it gives it back. Evaporation needs much more heat than melting: about 600 calories per gram against 80.

The heat released by condensation is what keeps a thunderstorm growing and what powers a tropical cyclone, which gets its energy from the latent heat of water evaporated from a warm ocean.

Humidity: the words to know

Air can hold only so much water vapour. When it holds the most it can, it is saturated. Warm air can hold far more than cold air: roughly double for every 10 °C rise.

TermWhat it meansUnit
Vapour pressureThe part of the air pressure due to the water vapour in ithPa
Saturation vapour pressureVapour pressure when saturated. Depends only on temperaturehPa
Absolute humidityMass of water vapour in a volume of airg/m³
Specific humidityMass of water vapour per kg of moist airg/kg
Humidity mixing ratio (HMR)Mass of water vapour mixed with 1 kg of dry airg/kg
Relative humidity (RH)Water vapour present ÷ the most it could hold at that temperature, × 100%

HMR stays the same when air rises or sinks, as long as no water is added or removed (no condensation). That makes it the most useful measure for following a parcel of air upwards.

Relative humidity changes with temperature. Warm the same air and its RH falls, cool it and RH rises, even though the amount of water has not changed. So RH is highest around dawn and lowest in the afternoon.

Dew point, frost point and wet bulb

  • Dew point: the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure, to become saturated. Cool it more and water condenses as dew, mist, fog or cloud.
  • Frost point: the same idea, but saturated over ice. Cooling below it gives hoar frost.
  • Wet bulb temperature: the lowest temperature air can be cooled to by evaporating water into it. A desert cooler works this way. The drier the air, the bigger the cooling.

Dew point is a measure of how much moisture the air holds. It changes only if water vapour is added or removed. Warming or cooling the air (above the dew point) changes the RH but not the dew point. More moisture, higher dew point.

Remember for the exam

  • Unsaturated air: dry bulb > wet bulb > dew point.
  • Saturated air (RH 100%, in fog or rain): dry bulb = wet bulb = dew point.
  • The gap between temperature and dew point is the dew point spread. A small spread means the air is close to saturation: expect mist, fog or low cloud.

Measuring humidity: a psychrometer compares the dry and wet bulb readings to give RH. A hair hygrometer uses human hair, which gets longer as RH rises. A hygrograph draws a continuous record.

Base of a heap (cumulus) cloud: rising air cools 3 °C per 1000 ft while its dew point drops only about 0.5 °C per 1000 ft, so the gap closes by about 2.5 °C per 1000 ft. That gives:

Worked example: Cloud base = (temperature - dew point) × 400 ft

  1. Surface temperature 16 °C, dew point 12 °C
  2. Spread = 16 - 12 = 4 °C
  3. Cloud base = 4 × 400 = 1,600 ft above the ground

Worked example: Afternoon temperature +12 °C, dew point +5 °C. What must happen for saturation?

  1. Dew point is the temperature at which saturation happens
  2. So the air must cool to +5 °C (a drop of 7 °C)

Water and ice below 0 °C

Water drops do not freeze at 0 °C unless there is something to freeze on. In clouds they often stay liquid well below 0 °C. These are supercooled drops. Small drops can stay liquid down to about -40 °C. Supercooled drops freeze the moment they hit an aircraft, which is the cause of airframe icing (Module 12).

Below 0 °C, water drops have a higher saturation vapour pressure than ice at the same temperature. So in a cloud with both supercooled drops and ice crystals, the drops evaporate and the vapour deposits onto the crystals. The crystals grow big enough to fall. This is how most rain starts outside the tropics (Module 6).

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • F = (9/5 × C) + 32. K = C + 273. -40 °C = -40 °F.
  • Stevenson screen: shade, 1.25 m (4 ft) high, door facing the pole. Max thermometer mercury, min thermometer alcohol.

  • Convection is vertical, advection is horizontal.
  • Density goes up with pressure, down with temperature, humidity and height. Moist air is lighter than dry air.
  • Surface density is higher at the poles. Above about 8 km it is higher over the equator.

  • Evaporation, melting, sublimation absorb latent heat. Condensation, freezing, deposition release it.
  • HMR is g of vapour per kg of dry air and stays constant in rising or sinking air without condensation.

  • Saturated: T = wet bulb = dew point. Psychrometer measures RH.
  • Cumulus base = (T - dew point) × 400 ft.
  • Supercooled drops down to about -40 °C. SVP over water > over ice.

Quick revision

The 15 revision slides for this module, as a list.

Heat is not temperature

  • Heat = total energy. Temperature = average energy
  • F = (9/5 × C) + 32, K = C + 273
  • -40 °C = -40 °F
  • Water has a high specific heat: land heats and cools faster

Measuring temperature

  • Stevenson screen: shade, louvres, 1.25 m (4 ft) up
  • Door faces the pole so the sun never shines in
  • Max thermometer mercury, min thermometer alcohol
  • Thermograph uses a bimetallic strip. Radiosonde for upper air

Short wave in, long wave out

  • Sun (about 6000 °C) sends short wave radiation
  • Earth sends back long wave radiation, day and night
  • Water vapour and CO₂ absorb the long wave and warm the air
  • So the troposphere is heated from below

Insolation and albedo

  • Insolation is greatest with the sun overhead
  • Albedo = share reflected. Highest from fresh snow
  • About 30% reflected, 20% absorbed by air, 50% by the ground

How heat moves

  • Radiation: waves, no medium needed
  • Conduction: contact, only near the ground
  • Convection: up and down. Advection: sideways
  • Latent heat from condensation powers storms

The daily cycle

  • Minimum just after sunrise
  • Maximum about 2 to 3 hours after noon
  • Big range: clear, calm, over land
  • Small range: cloudy, windy, over the sea. Cloudy nights are warmer

Inversions

  • Temperature rises with height: very stable
  • Traps haze and smoke, so poor visibility below
  • Types: radiation, subsidence, frontal, turbulence

What makes air dense

  • Density = pressure ÷ (R × absolute temperature)
  • Up with pressure. Down with temperature, humidity, height
  • Moist air is lighter than dry air
  • 1% less dense: -10 hPa, +3 °C or +300 ft

Density around the globe

  • At the surface: denser at the poles
  • At about 8 km: about the same everywhere
  • Above 8 km: denser over the equator
  • Lowest in the afternoon, highest at sunrise

Density altitude

  • ISA height that has today's density
  • Hot, high, humid = high DA = thin air
  • Longer take-off and landing, poorer climb
  • About 120 ft per 1 °C above ISA

Latent heat

  • Melting, evaporation, sublimation take in heat
  • Condensation, freezing, deposition give out heat
  • Evaporation needs about 7 times the heat of melting
  • Cyclones run on latent heat from warm seas

Humidity words

  • HMR: g of vapour per kg of dry air. Constant in rising air
  • Absolute humidity: g per m³
  • RH = present ÷ maximum × 100. Falls when air warms
  • Warm air holds about double for every 10 °C

Dew point and wet bulb

  • Dew point: cool at constant pressure until saturated
  • Dew point = moisture content. Only changes if water is added or removed
  • Unsaturated: T > wet bulb > dew point. Saturated: all equal
  • Small spread: expect mist, fog or low cloud

Two quick sums

  • Cumulus base = (T - dew point) × 400 ft
  • T 16, dew point 12: base 1,600 ft
  • Density altitude = pressure altitude + 120 × ISA deviation
  • PA 800 ft, ISA +31: DA about 4,500 ft

Below freezing

  • Cloud drops stay liquid well below 0 °C: supercooled
  • Down to about -40 °C
  • SVP over water is higher than over ice
  • So ice crystals grow while the drops shrink

Sample questions

5 of the 54 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. At what temperature do the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales show the same number?

    1. 0°
    2. -40°
    3. -32°
    4. 100°
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Put C = F in F = (9/5 × C) + 32 and you get -40. So -40 °C is exactly -40 °F.

  2. Q2. Land heats up and cools down faster than the sea mainly because:

    1. Land is darker
    2. The specific heat of land is much lower than that of water
    3. Land is higher than the sea
    4. Water has a higher albedo
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Water needs about five times more heat than land to warm by the same amount. It also mixes the heat downwards and loses heat to evaporation.

  3. Q3. With pressure constant, if the temperature of air rises its density will:

    1. Increase
    2. Not change
    3. Double
    4. Decrease
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Warm air expands, so the same mass takes up more space and density falls.

  4. Q4. For every 1 °C that the temperature is above ISA, density altitude is higher than pressure altitude by about:

    1. 120 ft
    2. 30 ft
    3. 300 ft
    4. 1000 ft
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The rule of thumb is about 120 ft per 1 °C of ISA deviation.

  5. Q5. Relative humidity is normally highest:

    1. In the early afternoon
    2. Around noon
    3. In the early evening
    4. Around dawn
    Show answer

    Answer: D. RH is highest when temperature is lowest, around dawn, and lowest in the hot afternoon.
