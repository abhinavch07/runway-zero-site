What air is made of, how the atmosphere is stacked in layers, why the tropopause matters to pilots, and the International Standard Atmosphere with ISA deviation sums.

What the atmosphere is

The atmosphere is the layer of air around the earth. Gravity holds it in place, so it turns with the earth. It has no sharp top edge. With height the air keeps thinning out and slowly fades into space.

  • Air has weight, so it presses down on everything. This push is atmospheric pressure.
  • Air can be squeezed and expanded. That is why it is thick near the ground and thin up high.
  • Air moves and carries heat and moisture from one place to another. This is what makes weather.
  • Air conducts heat badly. Only the air touching the ground is warmed or cooled directly.

What air is made of

GasBy volumeWhat to know
Nitrogen (N₂)78%Does not take part in weather
Oxygen (O₂)21%Needed for breathing and burning fuel
Argon (Ar)0.93%Third biggest, an inert gas
Carbon dioxide (CO₂)about 0.04%Greenhouse gas, more over cities and industry
Water vapour (H₂O)0 to 4%Varies a lot. The most important gas for weather
Ozone (O₃)tinyMostly in the stratosphere. Absorbs UV from the sun

Nitrogen to oxygen is about 4 : 1 by volume. By weight it is about 3 : 1, because an oxygen molecule is heavier than a nitrogen molecule.

Old exam answer

Older books and the DGCA question bank give carbon dioxide as 0.03% (some say 0.035%). Today it is about 0.042% (420 ppm) and still rising. In the exam, pick the option closest to 0.03% if 0.04% is not given.

Water vapour is the gas that makes weather. Without it there would be no cloud, fog, rain or thunderstorm. Warm air can hold more of it than cold air. In warm, humid tropical air it can reach about 4% by volume. Over the poles and above about 30,000 ft it is almost nil because the air is so cold. Nearly all of it (about 99%) is in the troposphere.

Greenhouse gases such as water vapour, CO₂, methane and ozone let the sun's short wave radiation pass through, but they absorb part of the long wave radiation that the earth gives out. They send some of it back down, which keeps the earth warmer.

Ozone forms high up, where UV light splits oxygen molecules. Ozone is found from about 10 to 50 km, with the most ozone at 20 to 25 km. By absorbing UV it protects life on earth, and it also heats the stratosphere.

Air also carries tiny solid particles: salt from the sea, dust, smoke and pollution. They cut visibility, and they act as condensation nuclei, the tiny specks on which water vapour condenses to form cloud and fog droplets. This is why fog and smog are common over big cities.

Because air thins out quickly with height, pilots need supplementary oxygen above about 10,000 ft.

Up to about 80 km the gases are well mixed, so the make up of air stays almost the same (only water vapour and ozone change). This part is called the homosphere. Above it, in the heterosphere, the gases sort themselves into layers by weight.

The atmosphere is heated from below. Sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground. The ground then warms the air above it by conduction, convection, radiation and by the latent heat released when water vapour condenses into cloud. That is why it usually gets colder as you climb.

The layers of the atmosphere

The layers are named by how temperature changes with height. The fall of temperature with height is called the lapse rate. A rise of temperature with height is an inversion (a negative lapse rate). No change with height is isothermal.

LayerHeightTemperatureWhat to know
TroposphereSurface to 8 to 18 kmFalls, about 6.5 °C per kmAll weather. Most flying. Unstable. Most of the air and water vapour
StratosphereTropopause to about 50 kmConstant at first, then risesOzone heats it. Very stable, very dry. Nacreous clouds
Mesosphere50 to about 80 to 85 kmFalls againColdest point of the whole atmosphere at its top. Noctilucent clouds
ThermosphereAbove about 85 kmRises a lotVery thin air. Exosphere above about 500 to 700 km

The top of each layer is a pause: tropopause, stratopause, mesopause. At the pause the temperature trend changes.

Troposphere. From the Greek word tropos, meaning turning or mixing. Air here keeps rising, sinking and mixing, which is what creates weather. Temperature falls with height, so this layer is generally unstable. It holds about three quarters of all the air and about 99% of the water vapour and dust.

Stratosphere. In the lower part the temperature stays about the same (isothermal). Higher up it rises because ozone absorbs UV, reaching about 0 °C near the stratopause at 50 km. Warm air on top of cold air means the stratosphere is very stable. There is almost no water vapour and no weather. Nacreous clouds (another name is mother of pearl clouds) can form here at about 20 to 30 km over high latitudes in winter.

Mesosphere. Temperature falls again with height. The mesopause has the lowest temperatures in the atmosphere, about -90 °C or below. Rare noctilucent clouds form near the mesopause over polar regions.

Thermosphere and exosphere. Temperature climbs steeply, but the air is so thin that there is very little heat in it. The outer part is the exosphere, where the air fades into space.

Ionosphere. From about 60 km upwards the sun's radiation turns some molecules into charged ions. This region reflects HF radio waves, which is how HF radio reaches far beyond the horizon. Solar flares disturb it and can black out HF radio.

Memory trick

Order going up: T S M T. Think "Tall Ships Make Trips": Troposphere, Stratosphere, Mesosphere, Thermosphere. Temperature goes down, up, down, up.

Memory trick

Noctilucent means "night shining". These are the highest clouds of all, up near the mesopause, still lit by the sun after dark. Nacreous clouds are lower down, in the stratosphere.

The tropopause

The tropopause is the boundary between the troposphere and the stratosphere. It is where the temperature stops falling with height. By the WMO rule it is the lowest level where the lapse rate drops to 2 °C per km or less.

WhereHeightTemperature
Over the equator16 to 18 km (about 55,000 ft)About -75 °C to -80 °C
Mid latitudesAbout 11 km (36,000 ft)About -55 °C
Over the poles8 to 10 km (about 28,000 ft)About -45 °C to -55 °C

Rule: the warmer the surface, the higher the tropopause. Warm air rises and mixes higher before it stops cooling. So the tropopause is:

  • Higher over the equator, lower over the poles (latitude).
  • Higher in summer, lower in winter (season).
  • Higher over warm land in summer than over the cooler sea.
  • Higher over a warm high pressure area, lower over a cold low pressure area.

The equatorial tropopause is colder than the polar one. This sounds wrong but is easy to see: over the equator the air keeps cooling at about 6.5 °C per km for 16 km or more, so it ends up very cold. Over the pole the cooling stops at about 8 km. So above about 8 km, the air over the poles is warmer than the air over the equator at the same level.

The tropopause has breaks (folds) at about 40° and 60° latitude, where its height jumps. The break near 40° is the more marked one. This splits it into three parts:

  • Tropical tropopause: from the equator to about 35° to 40°, near the 100 hPa level. Over India it is at about 16 to 16.5 km.
  • Middle tropopause: between about 40° and 60°, near 200 hPa, about 11 to 12 km. Over north India it is seen in winter at about 11.5 km.
  • Polar tropopause: towards the poles from about 60°, near 300 hPa, about 8 to 9 km. It is sometimes seen over Srinagar in winter when polar air comes south.

Why pilots care about the tropopause:

  • It is the ceiling for most cloud. Big CB clouds spread out into an anvil when they reach it. Only very strong ones punch a little way above it.
  • Jet streams blow near the breaks, and the strongest winds are found just below the tropopause.
  • Clear air turbulence (CAT) is common near the tropopause and the jet streams.
  • Above it the air is very dry and stable, so flying above the tropopause is usually smooth.

The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)

The real atmosphere changes every day. To build, test and compare aircraft and instruments, everyone needs one agreed "average" atmosphere. ICAO has fixed one: the International Standard Atmosphere, or ISA. It is used to calibrate altimeters and airspeed indicators and to work out aircraft performance.

In the ISA, -56.5 °C is the lowest temperature, found from 11 km to 20 km.

ISA temperature at any height (below 36,090 ft): start at +15 °C and take off 2 °C for every 1000 ft.

Worked example: ISA temperature at FL250

  1. Height = 25 thousand ft
  2. Drop = 25 × 2 = 50 °C
  3. ISA = 15 - 50 = -35 °C

ISA deviation tells you how much warmer or colder the real air is than the standard. Always do actual minus ISA. A plus answer means warmer than ISA, a minus answer means colder.

Worked example: OAT at FL100 is +5 °C. Find the ISA deviation.

  1. ISA at FL100 = 15 - (10 × 2) = -5 °C
  2. Deviation = actual - ISA = +5 - (-5) = +10
  3. So the air is ISA +10, warmer than standard

Worked example: OAT at FL300 is -50 °C. Find the ISA deviation.

  1. ISA at FL300 = 15 - (30 × 2) = -45 °C
  2. Deviation = -50 - (-45) = -5
  3. So the air is ISA -5, colder than standard

Worked example: OAT at FL410 is -60 °C. Find the ISA deviation.

  1. FL410 is above the ISA tropopause (36,090 ft), so ISA stays at -56.5 °C
  2. Deviation = -60 - (-56.5) = -3.5
  3. So the air is ISA -3.5

Worked example: MSL pressure is 1002.25 hPa. How far is it from ISA?

  1. Deviation = actual - ISA = 1002.25 - 1013.25 = -11 hPa

Memory trick

Mind the minus signs. Write the ISA value in brackets first, then subtract: actual - (ISA). "Minus minus" becomes plus.

The Jet Standard Atmosphere (JSA) was made for jet engine work. It also starts at +15 °C at MSL but uses a lapse rate of 2 °C per 1000 ft with no tropopause. It is rarely asked.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • N₂ 78%, O₂ 21%, argon 0.93%. N₂ : O₂ is 4 : 1 by volume, 3 : 1 by weight.
  • Water vapour has the greatest effect on weather. Up to about 4% by volume.
  • Ozone: most at 20 to 25 km, absorbs UV, warms the stratosphere.
  • Half the air is below 18,000 ft (500 hPa). About 3/4 is in the troposphere. Homosphere up to 80 km.
  • The atmosphere is heated from below by the earth's surface.
  • Layers: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere. Troposphere is unstable, stratosphere is stable.
  • Tropopause: 16 to 18 km at the equator (about -75 °C), 8 to 10 km at the poles (about -45 °C), about 11 km in mid latitudes.
  • Warmer surface, higher tropopause. Higher in summer. The equatorial tropopause is colder than the polar one.
  • Tropopause breaks at about 40° and 60°. Jet streams near the breaks, max winds and CAT just below the tropopause.
  • Stratosphere goes up to about 50 km. Nacreous clouds in the stratosphere, noctilucent clouds in the mesosphere.
  • Lowest temperatures in the whole atmosphere are at the mesopause.
  • ISA: dry air, +15 °C, 1013.25 hPa, 1.225 kg/m³, 1.98 °C per 1000 ft to 36,090 ft, then -56.5 °C to 65,617 ft.
  • ISA deviation = actual - ISA.
  • Supplementary oxygen above about 10,000 ft.

Quick revision

The 10 revision slides for this module, as a list.

What is the atmosphere?

  • A layer of air held to the earth by gravity
  • It has weight, so it exerts pressure
  • It can be squeezed: thick low down, thin up high
  • It moves heat and moisture around, making weather
  • It conducts heat badly of heat

What air is made of

  • Nitrogen 78%, oxygen 21%, argon 0.93%, CO₂ about 0.04%
  • N₂ : O₂ = 4 : 1 by volume, 3 : 1 by weight
  • Water vapour: 0 to 4%, the most important gas for weather
  • Particles act as condensation nuclei for cloud and fog

Ozone and greenhouse gases

  • Ozone mostly at 20 to 25 km
  • It absorbs UV and warms the stratosphere
  • Greenhouse gases let sunlight in but trap some of the earth's heat
  • The atmosphere is heated from below, by the ground

Where the air is

  • Half of all the air is below 18,000 ft (500 hPa)
  • About three quarters is in the troposphere
  • About 99% is below 35 km
  • Well mixed up to 80 km (homosphere)
  • Oxygen needed above about 10,000 ft

The four layers

  • Troposphere: temperature falls, all the weather
  • Stratosphere: constant, then rising. Stable and dry
  • Mesosphere: falls again. Coldest at the mesopause
  • Thermosphere: rises steeply, very thin air

Clouds in the high layers

  • Nacreous (mother of pearl) clouds: stratosphere, high latitudes, winter
  • Noctilucent clouds: near the mesopause, polar regions
  • Ionosphere from about 60 km reflects HF radio

The tropopause

  • Where temperature stops falling (lapse rate 2 °C per km or less)
  • Equator 16 to 18 km, about -75 °C
  • Poles 8 to 10 km, about -45 °C
  • Warmer surface, higher tropopause. Higher in summer
  • Equator tropopause is colder than the polar one

Three tropopauses

  • Breaks at about 40° and 60° latitude
  • Tropical ~100 hPa, middle ~200 hPa, polar ~300 hPa
  • Over India the tropical tropopause is about 16 to 16.5 km
  • Jet streams near the breaks, CAT and max winds just below

ISA at a glance

  • Dry air, +15 °C, 1013.25 hPa, 1.225 kg/m³ at MSL
  • Falls 1.98 °C per 1000 ft (6.5 °C per km)
  • Tropopause 36,090 ft, -56.5 °C
  • Constant -56.5 °C up to 65,617 ft (20 km)

ISA deviation in three steps

  • ISA temp = 15 - 2 × (thousands of feet)
  • Above 36,090 ft, ISA is just -56.5 °C
  • Deviation = actual - ISA
  • Example: FL100, OAT +5 °C. ISA -5. Deviation ISA +10

Sample questions

5 of the 48 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. In which layer of the atmosphere does almost all weather occur?

    1. Stratosphere
    2. Thermosphere
    3. Mesosphere
    4. Troposphere
    Show answer

    Answer: D. The troposphere has nearly all the water vapour and the rising and sinking air that make cloud and rain.

  2. Q2. The atmosphere is mainly heated:

    1. Directly by the sun's rays from above
    2. By the jet streams
    3. By the ozone layer
    4. From below, by the earth's surface
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Sunlight mostly passes through the air and warms the ground. The ground then warms the air above it, so temperature usually falls with height.

  3. Q3. The higher the surface temperature, the tropopause will be:

    1. Lower
    2. Broken
    3. At the same height
    4. Higher
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Warm air rises and mixes higher before it stops cooling, so a warmer surface gives a higher tropopause.

  4. Q4. If you climb through the lower stratosphere, the temperature:

    1. Keeps falling at 6.5 °C per km
    2. Stays about the same at first, then rises
    3. Falls sharply
    4. Changes at random
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The lower stratosphere is about isothermal. Higher up the temperature rises because ozone absorbs UV.

  5. Q5. Which statement about the ISA is correct?

    1. The air contains 1% water vapour
    2. The air is perfectly dry
    3. The air is saturated
    4. The humidity is 50%
    Show answer

    Answer: B. ISA assumes perfectly dry air. Real air always has some water vapour.
