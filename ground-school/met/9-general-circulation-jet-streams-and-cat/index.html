Why the atmosphere circulates, the three cell model with its pressure belts and wind belts, the ITCZ, the main jet streams that affect India (subtropical, tropical easterly, polar front and the low level monsoon jet), and clear air turbulence: where to expect it and what to do.

Why the air circulates

Over a year, the tropics receive more heat from the sun than they lose, and the polar regions lose more than they receive. If nothing moved heat around, the tropics would keep getting hotter and the poles colder. The winds and ocean currents carry the extra heat towards the poles. The large, long term pattern of these winds is the general circulation.

The trade winds are the surface part of the sinking air from the subtropical highs, flowing back towards the equator and turned westwards (to their right in the NH) by the Coriolis force.

Upper winds: apart from a belt near the equator, the upper troposphere is dominated by westerlies (the zonal westerlies), because the poles are colder than the tropics (thermal wind, Module 4). Over the equatorial belt the upper winds are easterly. The whole pattern shifts north in the northern summer and south in winter, following the sun about one to two months behind it.

The ITCZ

The Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) is the zone where the north east trades of the NH meet the south east trades of the SH. The converging air rises, giving lines and clusters of CB, heavy showers and thunderstorms. It is also called the equatorial trough. It is not one solid wall of storms: there are active stretches with heavy weather and quieter gaps.

  • It moves with the sun, about one to two months behind, and moves much further over land than over the sea.
  • In July it lies far north over India, at about 20° to 25°N, where it merges with the monsoon trough along the Gangetic plains.
  • In January it lies south of the equator over the Indian Ocean and northern Australia.
  • Flying from India to south east Asia or Australia in the northern winter, you cross it south of the equator.

Jet streams

A jet stream is a fast, narrow river of wind high in the troposphere, usually just below the tropopause. WMO defines it as a core of at least 60 kt. A typical jet is thousands of km long, a few hundred km wide and a few km deep, with strong wind shear around it.

  • The axis is the line of fastest wind. The core is the tube of strongest wind round it. Faster patches moving along the axis are jet streaks.
  • Cause: a strong temperature contrast across a narrow zone. The thermal wind builds up with height and peaks near the tropopause. The bigger the contrast, the stronger the jet, so jets are stronger in winter.
  • Jets sit near the breaks in the tropopause (Module 1).
  • Vertical shear near the core is about 5 m/s per km (about 3 kt per 1000 ft) or more. Vertical shear is much stronger than horizontal shear for the same distance.
  • Core speeds of 100 to 200 kt are common in winter.

  • The STJ is the most constant jet of the NH, present all year in some form. Over India it is a winter feature.
  • During the monsoon, the temperature contrast reverses (Tibet and north India are hottest), so the upper winds over India turn easterly: at 30,000 ft they are easterly, and the TEJ blows above.
  • Cloud signs: long bands or streaks of cirrus on the warm (equatorward) side of the jet, often with a sharp poleward edge along the jet axis.
  • A strong STJ moving south with a western disturbance helps trigger severe thunderstorms over north India. Divergence near jet streaks favours rising air below.

Flying with jets: eastbound across north India in winter, a jet can add 100 kt or more of tailwind. Westbound, routes and levels are chosen to avoid its core. Changing level by a few thousand feet, or moving to one side of the axis, often cuts the headwind a lot.

Clear air turbulence (CAT)

CAT is turbulence met in clear air at high levels, not caused by convection, usually above about 20,000 ft and near the tropopause. It gives no warning on weather radar and often none to the eye, which makes it dangerous to unbelted passengers and crew.

Cause: strong wind shear, mainly vertical shear. Layers of air sliding past each other at very different speeds roll up into large eddies, like waves breaking on a beach.

  • Near jet streams, especially on the cold (low pressure) side, just below the core, and also above it. Looking downstream in the NH, the cold side is on the left. CAT is on the edges of the jet, not in the core itself.
  • Where the jet is strong, changing fast or sharply curved, as round a deep upper trough or ridge.
  • Near the tropopause and upper frontal zones with strong temperature contrast.
  • Above and downwind of mountain ranges with strong winds (mountain waves reaching high levels), and near the tops of CBs.
  • Rough guide: vertical shear of about 5 to 6 kt per 1000 ft or more makes CAT likely.
  • Over India, CAT is most common over north India in winter (with the STJ, worst from December to February) and over south India in July and August (with the TEJ). Western disturbances and the Himalayas add to it.
  • Forecast CAT is shown on significant weather (SIGWX) charts. Severe CAT reported by aircraft leads the Met office to issue a SIGMET.
  • If you meet CAT: seat belt signs on, slow to the turbulence penetration speed, and change level (often by a few thousand feet) or move away from the jet core, preferably towards the warm side. Report it to ATC.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Circulation carries heat from the tropics to the poles.
  • Hadley (0 to 30°), Ferrel (30 to 60°), Polar (60 to 90°).
  • Doldrums at the equator, trades 5 to 30° (NE in NH, SE in SH, strongest low down over the oceans), horse latitudes at 30°, westerlies 35 to 60°, polar front at 60°, polar easterlies.
  • Trades: air from the subtropical highs going to the equator, turned west by Coriolis.
  • Upper troposphere: mostly westerly, easterly over the equatorial belt.
  • ITCZ: where the NE and SE trades meet. CB, heavy showers. Moves with the sun. July about 20 to 25°N over India, January south of the equator.
  • Jet: core 60 kt or more (WMO), long, narrow, shallow. Cause: strong temperature contrast. Stronger in winter.
  • Vertical shear in a jet is stronger than horizontal shear.
  • STJ: about 12 km, 27°N over India, October to May, southernmost in February, strongest in winter, most constant jet.
  • PFJ: moves south and strengthens in winter.
  • TEJ: easterly, 15 to 16 km, about 13°N over south India, June to August. Monsoon upper winds over India are easterly.
  • Somali low level jet: 1 to 3 km, feeds the monsoon.
  • Jet cirrus on the warm side.
  • CAT: clear air, high levels, wind shear. Worst on the cold side, just below the jet core, at the edges, not in the core.
  • India: CAT over north India in winter (STJ), south India in July and August (TEJ).
  • CAT on SIGWX charts. Severe CAT reports lead to a SIGMET.

Quick revision

The 9 revision slides for this module, as a list.

Why the air moves

  • Tropics gain heat, poles lose it
  • Winds and currents carry heat polewards
  • The long term pattern is the general circulation

Three cells

  • Hadley: rises at the equator, sinks at 30°
  • Ferrel: 30° to 60°
  • Polar: sinks at the pole
  • Surface winds: trades, westerlies, polar easterlies

Wind belts

  • Doldrums: calm, CB
  • Trades: NE in NH, SE in SH
  • Horse latitudes at 30°: deserts
  • Westerlies 35° to 60°, Roaring Forties in the SH

The ITCZ

  • Where the NE and SE trades meet
  • CB, heavy showers, active and quiet stretches
  • Follows the sun: July 20 to 25°N over India
  • January south of the equator

Jet streams

  • Core 60 kt or more
  • Thousands of km long, hundreds wide, a few km deep
  • Caused by strong temperature contrast
  • Stronger in winter

India's jets

  • STJ: 12 km, 27°N, October to May, westerly
  • TEJ: 15 to 16 km, 13°N, June to August, easterly
  • Somali jet: 1 to 3 km, feeds the monsoon
  • PFJ: further north, south in winter

Jet signs and flying

  • Cirrus bands on the warm side
  • Big tailwinds eastbound in winter
  • Change level or move aside to escape headwinds
  • Monsoon upper winds over India are easterly

Clear air turbulence

  • Clear air, high levels, wind shear
  • Worst on the cold side, below the core
  • Also near the tropopause, mountains, CB tops
  • Rough guide: 5 to 6 kt per 1000 ft shear

CAT over India

Sample questions

5 of the 38 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The main reason for the general circulation of the atmosphere is:

    1. The earth's magnetic field
    2. Unequal heating, with surplus in the tropics and deficit at the poles
    3. The tides
    4. Mountains
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Winds carry the tropics' extra heat towards the poles.

  2. Q2. Away from the equator, which way do the upper tropospheric winds mostly blow?

    1. Easterly
    2. Westerly
    3. Northerly
    4. Variable
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The poles are colder than the tropics, so the thermal wind is westerly and grows with height.

  3. Q3. In a jet stream, how does vertical shear compare with horizontal shear over the same distance?

    1. Weaker
    2. Stronger
    3. The same
    4. Zero
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Over the same distance, wind changes far more with height than sideways. That is why vertical shear causes most CAT.

  4. Q4. The tropical easterly jet over India blows at about:

    1. 3 km
    2. 9 km
    3. 15 to 16 km
    4. 25 km
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The TEJ is near 100 to 150 hPa, about 15 to 16 km, just below the high tropical tropopause.

  5. Q5. The main cause of CAT is:

    1. Strong wind shear, especially vertical shear
    2. Surface heating
    3. Icing
    4. Low pressure
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Layers of air moving at very different speeds roll up into eddies.
