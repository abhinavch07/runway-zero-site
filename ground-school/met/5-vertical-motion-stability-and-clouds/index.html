Why rising air cools, the dry and saturated adiabatic lapse rates, how to tell stable from unstable air, the four ways air is made to rise, and all ten cloud types with their heights, looks and hazards.

Rising air cools: adiabatic change

Air conducts heat badly, so a rising or sinking bubble of air (a parcel) hardly swaps heat with the air around it. When it rises, pressure on it drops, it expands and cools. When it sinks, it is squeezed and warms. A change of temperature with no heat added or removed is called adiabatic.

RateValueWhen
Dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR)9.8 °C per km = 3 °C per 1000 ft. FixedUnsaturated air rising or sinking
Saturated adiabatic lapse rate (SALR)About 5 to 6 °C per km = 1.5 °C per 1000 ft near the ground. VariesSaturated air (in cloud) rising
Environmental lapse rate (ELR)Whatever it is today. ISA average 6.5 °C per kmThe real air around the parcel

Why SALR is smaller than DALR: as saturated air rises and cools, water vapour condenses and releases latent heat, which makes up for part of the cooling. In warm, moist tropical air a lot of vapour condenses, so SALR is small (as low as 4 °C per km). Very high up, where it is colder than about -40 °C, there is hardly any vapour left, so SALR gets close to DALR.

The DALR and SALR are what a rising parcel does. The ELR is what the surrounding air is like. Comparing them tells you whether the parcel keeps going up.

Worked example: A dry parcel at 35 °C at the surface is lifted 1 km. How warm is it now?

  1. Dry air cools at the DALR, about 10 °C per km
  2. 35 - 10 = 25 °C

Worked example: Temperature +8 °C at an aerodrome of elevation 1,350 ft. With ISA lapse rate, where is the freezing level?

  1. ISA lapse rate is 2 °C per 1000 ft
  2. To lose 8 °C you climb 8 ÷ 2 = 4,000 ft
  3. Freezing level = 1,350 + 4,000 = 5,350 ft AMSL

Stable, unstable and neutral air

Give a parcel a small push upwards. If it ends up colder than the air around it, it is heavier and sinks back: the air is stable. If it ends up warmer, it is lighter and keeps rising: the air is unstable. If it ends up at the same temperature, it stays put: neutral.

ELR compared with DALR and SALRStability
ELR less than SALR (including isothermal and inversion)Absolutely stable, for dry and saturated air
ELR more than DALRAbsolutely unstable, for dry and saturated air
SALR less than ELR less than DALRConditionally unstable: stable if dry, unstable if saturated
ELR equal to DALRNeutral for dry air
ELR equal to SALRNeutral for saturated air

Memory trick

The steeper the ELR, the less stable the air. A fast drop of temperature with height lets a warm parcel stay warmer than its surroundings. An inversion is the most stable of all.

Most of the time the real ELR lies between the SALR and the DALR, so the air is conditionally unstable. Lift it until it saturates and forms cloud, and it can take off on its own into cumulus or CB. A lapse rate steeper than the DALR (super-adiabatic) is found only near very hot ground on sunny afternoons.

Potential (convective) instability: a layer that is moist at the bottom and dry at the top may be stable now, but if the whole layer is lifted (by a front or hills) the bottom saturates first and cools slowly while the top cools fast. The layer becomes unstable, and CBs can grow hidden inside layer cloud. These embedded CBs are a known danger at warm fronts.

Stable airUnstable air
Layer cloud (stratus, stratiform)Heap cloud (cumulus, CB)
Steady, continuous rain or drizzleShowers, thunderstorms, hail
Poor visibility: haze, smoke and fog are trappedGood visibility except in showers
Smooth flyingBumpy, gusty, strong up and down draughts
Steady windGusty wind

Stability is decided by the environmental lapse rate on the day. It is measured with a radiosonde and plotted on a thermodynamic chart (in India the tephigram). On it, the DALR from the temperature, the SALR from the wet bulb and the mixing ratio line from the dew point all meet at one point, the Normand point. Starting from surface readings, this point is the lifting condensation level: the expected cloud base.

How air is made to rise

Cloud forms when air rises and cools to its dew point. The level where this happens is the condensation level, the cloud base. There are four main ways to make air rise:

LiftingHowTypical cloud
Convection (thermal)Sun heats the ground, warm bubbles riseCumulus by day, CB on hot afternoons
OrographicWind is forced up over hills and mountainsLayer cloud or CB on the windward side, lenticular cloud downwind
FrontalWarm air rides up over cold air at a frontLayer cloud at warm fronts, CB at cold fronts (Module 7)
ConvergenceAir flows into a low or trough and has to go upWidespread cloud and rain, monsoon cloud

Turbulence (mechanical mixing) near the ground also lifts air a little and can give a sheet of low stratus or stratocumulus on a windy day.

Convergence and divergence. When more air flows into an area than out (convergence) near the ground, it must rise: lows and troughs bring cloud. When air diverges high up, air from below rises to replace it, the surface pressure falls and cloud forms. The opposite, convergence aloft with divergence at the surface, makes air sink. Sinking air warms and dries, so a high has little cloud because of subsidence.

Fair weather cumulus follows the sun. It forms in the late morning as the ground heats, grows most in the afternoon and melts away in the evening when heating stops.

The ten cloud types

Clouds are named by their shape and their height. Shapes: stratus (layer), cumulus (heap), cirrus (wispy, of ice). Nimbus means rain bearing. Alto means medium level. There are 10 main types (genera).

LevelBase height in the tropicsClouds
HighAbout 20,000 to 60,000 ft (6 to 18 km)Cirrus (Ci), Cirrocumulus (Cc), Cirrostratus (Cs)
MediumAbout 6,500 to 25,000 ft (2 to 8 km)Altocumulus (Ac), Altostratus (As). Nimbostratus (Ns) usually starts here and spreads down
LowSurface to about 6,500 ft (2 km)Stratus (St), Stratocumulus (Sc)
Vertical growthBase low, top can be very highCumulus (Cu), Cumulonimbus (Cb)

In mid latitudes, medium cloud is 6,500 to 23,000 ft and high cloud 16,500 to 45,000 ft. In polar regions everything is lower. A cloud is placed by the height of its base: a cloud with base at 3 km and top at 6 km is a medium cloud.

Memory trick

Halo goes with High cloud (cirrostratus, ice). Corona goes with the middle Clouds (altostratus and altocumulus, water drops).

Cloud features and contrails

TermMeaning
LenticularisSmooth lens or almond shaped cloud (Ac, Sc, Cc), staying still in the wind. Sign of mountain waves

Contrails (condensation trails) form when the water vapour in jet exhaust condenses and freezes in very cold air, usually above about 30,000 ft. The mintra level is the lowest level at which contrails can form. Below it, no contrails. Wing tip trails are short lived, made by the pressure drop over wing tips. Distrails are clear lanes cut through a thin cloud by the heat of the exhaust.

Cloud amount, base and ceiling

Cloud amount is measured in oktas: eighths of the sky covered.

OktasReported as
1 to 2FEW
3 to 4SCT (scattered)
5 to 7BKN (broken)
8OVC (overcast)
  • Cloud base in METAR, SPECI and TAF is given above the aerodrome (ground). On area forecasts and significant weather charts it is above MSL.
  • Ceiling: the height of the base of the lowest layer below 20,000 ft (6,000 m) covering more than half the sky (BKN or OVC).
  • Cloud of operational significance: cloud with base below 5,000 ft (1,500 m) or below the highest minimum sector altitude, whichever is higher, and any CB or TCU at any height.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Adiabatic: temperature change with no heat added or removed. Rising air expands and cools.
  • DALR 3 °C per 1000 ft (9.8 per km), fixed. SALR about 1.5 °C per 1000 ft near the ground, varies.
  • SALR is less than DALR because of latent heat released by condensation. SALR nears DALR at very low temperatures.
  • ELR is the real lapse rate and changes with time. Stability depends on the ELR.
  • ELR < SALR: absolutely stable. ELR > DALR: absolutely unstable. In between: conditionally unstable.
  • Inversion and isothermal layers are absolutely stable.
  • Stable air: layer cloud, steady rain, poor visibility, smooth. Unstable air: heap cloud, showers, good visibility, bumpy.
  • Moist below, dry above, lifted: potential instability, embedded CB.
  • Four lifts: convection, orographic, frontal, convergence.
  • Upper divergence: surface pressure falls, cloud forms. A high is clear because of sinking air.
  • Tephigram: DALR, SALR and mixing ratio lines meet at the Normand point. From the surface it gives the LCL, the cloud base.
  • Cloud level is by base height. Base 3 km, top 6 km: medium cloud.
  • Cs: halo. As and Ac: corona. Ns: continuous rain. St: drizzle, fog when on the ground. Cb: all hazards, spans all three levels.
  • Lenticular: mountain waves. Castellanus: thunderstorms later. Capillatus: Cb with anvil. Virga: rain not reaching the ground.
  • Mintra level: the lowest level at which contrails form.
  • FEW 1 to 2, SCT 3 to 4, BKN 5 to 7, OVC 8 oktas.
  • Ceiling: lowest BKN or OVC layer below 20,000 ft. METAR cloud base above the aerodrome.

Quick revision

Rising air cools

  • Rising air expands and cools, sinking air warms
  • No heat in or out: adiabatic
  • DALR 3 °C per 1000 ft, fixed
  • SALR about 1.5 °C per 1000 ft, varies

Why SALR is smaller

  • Condensation releases latent heat
  • It makes up part of the cooling
  • Warm tropical air: SALR very small
  • Below about -40 °C: SALR close to DALR

Stable or unstable?

  • Parcel colder than surroundings: sinks back, stable
  • Parcel warmer: keeps rising, unstable
  • ELR < SALR: absolutely stable
  • ELR > DALR: absolutely unstable
  • In between: conditionally unstable

What you see

  • Stable: layer cloud, steady rain, haze, smooth
  • Unstable: heap cloud, showers, clear, bumpy
  • Inversion: most stable of all
  • Moist below, dry above, lifted: embedded CB

Four ways to lift air

  • Convection: hot ground, thermals
  • Orographic: over hills
  • Frontal: warm air over cold
  • Convergence: air piling into a low

Up and down aloft

  • Surface convergence: air rises, cloud
  • Upper divergence: surface pressure falls, cloud
  • Upper convergence: air sinks, clear skies
  • A high has no low cloud because of subsidence

  • High: Ci, Cc, Cs (ice)
  • Medium: Ac, As, (Ns)
  • Low: St, Sc
  • Vertical: Cu, Cb

Clouds and what they bring

  • Cs: halo. As: corona, steady rain

  • St: drizzle, fog on the ground
  • Cb: thunder, hail, turbulence, icing

Warning signs in the sky

  • Lenticular: mountain waves
  • Castellanus: thunderstorms later
  • Capillatus: Cb with an anvil

Cloud amount and ceiling

Sample questions

5 of the 48 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. A process in which no heat enters or leaves a parcel of air is called:

    1. Isothermal
    2. Adiabatic
    3. Isobaric
    4. Conduction
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Adiabatic means no heat exchange. The temperature changes only because the parcel expands or is squeezed.

  2. Q2. The atmosphere is absolutely stable when the ELR is:

    1. More than the DALR
    2. Less than the SALR
    3. Equal to the DALR
    4. Between the SALR and DALR
    Show answer

    Answer: B. If the surroundings cool more slowly than even the SALR, any lifted parcel ends up colder and sinks back.

  3. Q3. Air temperature is +8 °C at an aerodrome 1,350 ft AMSL. With the ISA lapse rate, the freezing level is about:

    1. 3,350 ft AMSL
    2. 5,350 ft AMSL
    3. 9,350 ft AMSL
    4. 4,000 ft AMSL
    Show answer

    Answer: B. At 2 °C per 1000 ft, losing 8 °C takes 4,000 ft. 1,350 + 4,000 = 5,350 ft AMSL.

  4. Q4. A cloud has its base at 3 km and its top at 6 km. It is classed as a:

    1. Low cloud
    2. Medium cloud
    3. High cloud
    4. Cloud of vertical development
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Clouds are classed by the height of their base. A 3 km base is in the medium level.

  5. Q5. Lenticular clouds indicate the presence of:

    1. A warm front
    2. A cold front
    3. Mountain waves
    4. A tropical cyclone
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Lens shaped clouds that sit still in a strong wind form at the crests of mountain waves.
