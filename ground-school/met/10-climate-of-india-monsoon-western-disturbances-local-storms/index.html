India's four seasons and the flying hazards of each: winter fog and western disturbances, pre-monsoon dust storms and norwesters, the south west monsoon with its trough, breaks and depressions, and the post-monsoon retreat with cyclones and the north east monsoon.

What shapes India's weather

  • The Himalayas block the bitter winter winds from Central Asia, so most of India is warmer than other places at the same latitude. In summer they hold the moist monsoon air in and turn it westwards along the plains.
  • The Thar desert and north west India heat up enormously in summer, creating the heat low that draws in the monsoon.
  • The Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal supply the moisture.
  • The seasonal reversal of winds, the monsoon, rules everything. The word comes from the Arabic mausim, meaning season.

About 75% of India's annual rain falls from June to September. The wettest places are Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in Meghalaya (over 11,000 mm a year). The far west of Rajasthan gets under about 200 mm.

Some older books treat December to February as the cold weather season and October and November as post-monsoon. The weather is the same, only the month boundaries differ.

Winter (January and February)

  • Pressure: high over north west India (the edge of the Siberian high), lower over the south. Weak gradient.
  • Surface winds: light, north westerly over north India, turning north easterly over the peninsula (the winter or north east monsoon).
  • Upper winds: westerly above about 10,000 ft, strengthening with height. The subtropical jet lies over north India at about 12 km and 27°N with 80 to 150 kt. Flying Delhi to Kolkata at FL100 in January, expect north westerly to westerly winds of about 25 to 30 kt.
  • Weather: clear skies, dry air, cold nights, large day to night range. Broken by western disturbances.

Hazards: fog over the Indo-Gangetic plains (worst after a WD), smog and haze near cities, poor visibility in the mornings, CAT near the subtropical jet, mountain waves over the Himalayas, a lower freezing level so more icing, and snow and low cloud over the hills during WDs. Tamil Nadu and the south east coast get rain and thunderstorms from the north east monsoon, mostly in October to December.

A cold wave follows many WDs as cold, dry north westerly air spreads over north and central India. IMD declares a cold wave in the plains when the minimum temperature is 10 °C or less and well below normal (about 4.5 °C or more below).

Western disturbances (WDs)

A western disturbance is a mid latitude low or trough that starts over the Mediterranean, Caspian or Black Sea region and travels from west to east in the westerlies across Iran, Afghanistan and Pakistan into north India. They belong to the extra-tropical (frontal) cyclone family, and by the time they reach India they are usually occluded. In the upper air they show as troughs in the westerlies.

  • Most frequent in winter: about 4 to 7 a month. Fewer in the pre-monsoon, almost none in the monsoon, coming back from October.
  • They pick up moisture from the Arabian Sea and grow stronger over the Himalayas.
  • Weather is heaviest over Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal, Uttarakhand, Punjab and Haryana, with rain over the plains and snow over the hills. Strong ones affect UP, MP, Bihar and even north east India.
  • A low that forms to the south of a WD under its influence is an induced low.
  • In the pre-monsoon, WDs trigger dust storms in the north west and thunderstorms over the northern plains.

Pre-monsoon (March to May)

The land heats up fast. Pressure falls over the north west and a heat low develops over Pakistan and Rajasthan. Day temperatures reach 40 to 45 °C or more over north and central India in May. The subtropical jet weakens and moves north. Many pilots call this the most hazardous season for flying in India.

Over the peninsula, a dry line (a boundary between hot dry air from the north and moister air from the sea) often sets off afternoon thunderstorms with hail and squalls.

The south west monsoon (June to September)

As the sun moves north, the land heats far more than the sea. The ITCZ shifts north over India as the monsoon trough, the heat low deepens, and the south east trades of the southern Indian Ocean cross the equator, turn right and arrive as the south west monsoon: very moist equatorial air. The low level Somali jet carries it across the Arabian Sea, and high above, the tropical easterly jet blows from the east.

Old exam answer

The older normals, still in many books and question banks, were: whole country covered by 15 July, and withdrawal starting from north west India on 1 September. Onset over Kerala stays 1 June. Use the new dates unless only the old ones are offered.

The monsoon trough is the line of lowest pressure running from north west Rajasthan, across UP, to the head of the Bay of Bengal. It slopes southwards with height. Monsoon rain over India depends mainly on the position of the trough's axis. Winds are westerly to south westerly south of it and easterly to south easterly north of it.

  • Active monsoon: trough near its normal place or south of it, strong winds over the sea, widespread heavy rain.
  • Break monsoon: the trough moves north to the Himalayan foothills. Rain stops over central and north west India and becomes very heavy over the foothills and north east India, causing floods in Bihar and Assam. Hot, humid, hazy weather over the plains.
  • Monsoon depressions form over the north Bay of Bengal (head Bay), move west north west along the trough, and last about 3 to 5 days. The heaviest rain is in their south west sector. They bring most of the rain to central India.
  • Offshore troughs and vortices along the west coast and mid-tropospheric cyclones over Gujarat and Maharashtra give spells of extremely heavy rain (as in Mumbai in 2005).

Flying in the monsoon: cloud lies in layers with clear gaps between, so a smooth level can often be found, but CBs embedded in the layers give heavy rain and turbulence. Visibility is excellent outside rain but drops sharply in heavy rain. Coastal airports (Mumbai, Goa, Mangaluru, Kochi) get low cloud, heavy rain, gusty crosswinds and wet runways. The freezing level is high, about 5 km. Upper winds over India are easterly.

Post-monsoon (October to December)

  • The monsoon withdraws from north west India first, and dry northerly air takes over. Skies clear, and the hot, humid October heat gives way to cooler nights.
  • The winds over the Bay of Bengal turn north easterly: the north east monsoon brings the main rainy season to Tamil Nadu, south coastal Andhra Pradesh and south Kerala from October to December.
  • This is the main cyclone season, especially in the Bay of Bengal in October and November, with the strongest storms of the year.
  • WDs return, the subtropical jet comes back over north India, and fog begins over the plains by December.

El Niño and the monsoon

El Niño is a warming of the sea surface in the central and eastern Pacific, off Ecuador and Peru, every few years. It often goes with a weaker Indian monsoon and drought, though not every time. La Niña, the opposite cooling, often goes with a good monsoon. A positive Indian Ocean Dipole (warmer western Indian Ocean) tends to help the monsoon.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • IMD seasons: winter Jan to Feb, pre-monsoon Mar to May, SW monsoon Jun to Sep, post-monsoon Oct to Dec.
  • About 75% of India's rain falls June to September.
  • Winter: NW winds over north India, NE over the peninsula. Upper westerlies, STJ at 27°N, 12 km.
  • Winter hazards: fog after WDs, haze, smog, CAT near the STJ, mountain waves.
  • WDs: mid latitude lows from the Mediterranean, moving west to east, usually occluded, most frequent in winter. Induced lows form to their south.
  • WD sequence: wind backs, temperature up, pressure down, Ci to As and Ns, rain or snow, then CB, then clearing, cold wave and fog.
  • Pre-monsoon: dust storms in the north west, norwesters (Kalbaisakhi) in the east and north east, March to May, loo, mango showers, heat waves, Bay cyclones in May.
  • Monsoon onset Kerala 1 June, Mumbai about 11 June, Delhi end of June, whole country 8 July (old normal 15 July).
  • Withdrawal from west Rajasthan about 17 September (old normal 1 September), complete by 15 October.
  • Monsoon advances as two branches: Arabian Sea (stronger, Western Ghats) and Bay of Bengal (north east, then up the Ganges).
  • Rain depends on the monsoon trough's position. Break: trough at the Himalayan foothills.
  • Monsoon depressions form in the north Bay of Bengal, move WNW, heaviest rain south west sector.
  • Monsoon flying: layers with embedded CB, good visibility outside rain, easterly upper winds, TEJ.
  • Post-monsoon: NE monsoon rain over Tamil Nadu, strongest cyclones in October and November.
  • El Niño often means a weaker monsoon.

Sample questions

5 of the 39 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. About what share of India's annual rainfall comes during the south west monsoon (June to September)?

    1. 25%
    2. 50%
    3. 75%
    4. 95%
    Show answer

    Answer: C. About three quarters of the year's rain falls in these four months.

  2. Q2. Western disturbances are most frequent over India in:

    1. Winter
    2. Pre-monsoon
    3. Monsoon
    4. Post-monsoon
    Show answer

    Answer: A. About 4 to 7 a month in winter. Almost none during the monsoon.

  3. Q3. Dust storms are most common over:

    1. Kerala in July
    2. Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana and Delhi in the pre-monsoon
    3. Assam in winter
    4. Tamil Nadu in November
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Hot, dry, loose soil and strong gusty winds in April to June raise dust storms over the north west.

  4. Q4. The south west monsoon advances over India as:

    1. A Bay of Bengal current only
    2. An Arabian Sea current only
    3. Both an Arabian Sea branch and a Bay of Bengal branch
    4. A north easterly current
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Two branches: the Arabian Sea branch hits the west coast, the Bay branch hits the north east and turns up the Ganges valley.

  5. Q5. During the south west monsoon, upper winds at 30,000 ft over India are mainly:

    1. Easterly
    2. Westerly
    3. South westerly
    4. Northerly
    Show answer

    Answer: A. In summer the north is warmer than the south, so the upper winds reverse to easterly. The tropical easterly jet blows above.
