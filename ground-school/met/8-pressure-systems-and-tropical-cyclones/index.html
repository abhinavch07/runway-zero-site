Where the world's main highs and lows sit and how they shift with the seasons, the different kinds of anticyclone and non-frontal depression, and tropical cyclones: how they form, their structure, movement, IMD classes, and the seasons they hit India.

The principal pressure areas

Averaged over a year, the world's surface pressure falls into belts (more in Module 9):

BeltLatitudeWeather
Equatorial low (trough)Near the equator, shifting with the sunRising air, CB, heavy showers, light winds (doldrums)
Subtropical highsAbout 30° N and SSinking air, clear skies, the great deserts
Subpolar lowsAbout 60° N and SDepressions and fronts, the polar front
Polar highsOver the polesCold, sinking air

Land heats and cools far more than the sea, so the belts break up into cells that change with the seasons. In winter a huge, cold Siberian high sits over Asia, and India lies on its southern edge with northerly and north westerly winds. In summer the land heats up and a heat low forms over Pakistan and north west India, pulling in the moist south west monsoon. Over the oceans, semi-permanent highs (Azores, Pacific, south Indian Ocean) and lows (Icelandic, Aleutian) stay in place most of the year.

Anticyclones

In an anticyclone (high) the pressure is highest in the middle. Air sinks in the centre and spreads out (diverges) near the ground. Winds are light and blow clockwise in the NH, with isobars far apart. Sinking air warms and dries, giving a subsidence inversion.

TypeHow it formsFeatures
Cold anticycloneAir cooled over a cold continent in winter, e.g. Siberian highShallow, high pressure only near the ground. Very cold, clear, dry. Fog and frost
Warm anticycloneLarge scale sinking air, e.g. the subtropical highsDeep, high pressure through the whole troposphere. Warm, dry, long lasting
Blocking anticycloneA warm high that stays still for days or weeks in mid latitudesBlocks the usual west to east march of depressions, which are pushed round it
RidgeA tongue of high pressure from a highFair weather, short lived between depressions
  • Weather: mostly fair, little cloud and light winds. No low cloud because of the sinking air.
  • But: poor visibility in haze and smoke trapped under the inversion, and in winter fog and low stratus over land. A sheet of stratocumulus can also sit under the inversion for days.
  • Over land in winter, a stationary high brings a tendency for fog and low stratus.

Depressions without fronts

In a depression (low) air converges near the ground and rises, giving cloud and rain. Winds blow anticlockwise in the NH. Frontal depressions are in Module 7. Others form without fronts:

TypeHow it formsExample
Thermal (heat) lowStrong heating of land. Air rises and pressure falls near the ground. ShallowSummer heat low over Pakistan, Rajasthan and north west India
Orographic (lee) lowAir flowing over a mountain range leaves a low on the lee sideLows in the lee of the Alps or Rockies
Polar lowVery cold air over a warmer sea in winterSmall intense lows over northern seas
Tropical low and depressionConvergence in the tropics, no frontsMonsoon depressions over the Bay of Bengal
Tropical cycloneA tropical low that grows very strong over a warm seaBay of Bengal and Arabian Sea cyclones

A heat low is shallow: above a few thousand feet it is often topped by a high. It gives hot, dusty, hazy weather rather than widespread rain unless moist air is drawn in.

Tropical cyclones: how they form

A tropical cyclone (TC) is an intense, non-frontal low over a tropical ocean, with very strong winds spiralling round a calm eye, torrential rain and huge CBs. The same storm is called a hurricane in the Atlantic and north east Pacific, a typhoon in the north west Pacific, and a cyclone in the Indian Ocean and around Australia. In general terms it is a tropical revolving storm (TRS).

  • Warm sea: surface temperature of about 26.5 to 27 °C or more, through a good depth.
  • Latitude about 5° to 20°. Not within about 5° of the equator, where the Coriolis force is too weak to start the spin.
  • Moist, unstable air through a deep layer.
  • Little vertical wind shear, so the storm is not torn apart. Strong shear during the monsoon is why few cyclones form then.
  • A pre-existing disturbance, such as a low or an easterly wave, and divergence aloft to carry the rising air away.

Its energy comes from latent heat released when water vapour from the warm ocean condenses in the clouds. Cut off that supply, by crossing land or colder sea, and it weakens. Over land, friction also slows it.

Most cyclones form on the western side of the oceans, where the sea surface is warmest (warm currents flow there).

Structure of a tropical cyclone

PartSizeWeather
EyeAbout 20 to 50 km acrossLowest pressure, light and variable winds, little cloud or broken cloud. Air sinks and warms, so the eye is the warmest part (warm core)
Eye wallA ring round the eye, tens of km wideStrongest winds, towering CBs up to 15 to 18 km, torrential rain, severe turbulence. The most dangerous part
Rain bandsSpiral bands out to a few hundred kmBands of CB with heavy showers and squalls, gaps between
Outer areaOut to several hundred kmGale force winds, cirrus spreading out from the top
  • The whole storm is typically a few hundred km across, and the circulation reaches up to the tropopause.
  • Central pressure can drop to 950 hPa or lower, 30 to over 60 hPa below the surroundings in strong storms.
  • In the NH winds spiral anticlockwise and inwards. The strongest winds are in the eye wall, and stronger on the right side of the track in the NH.
  • Passing through the eye, the wind drops suddenly, then returns just as strong from the opposite direction.

Movement and dangers

  • Cyclones usually move west or north west at about 10 to 15 kt, steered round the subtropical high. Many then recurve to the north and north east and speed up.
  • In the NH the right side of the track is the dangerous semicircle: the storm's forward speed adds to its winds there, and the wind tends to push ships into the path. The right front quadrant has the worst winds and weather. In the SH it is the left side.
  • On landfall, the storm surge (sea level pushed up by wind and low pressure) floods low coasts and causes most of the loss of life.
  • Over land the storm weakens fast but gives very heavy rain far inland.

For pilots: never try to fly through or under a cyclone. Expect severe turbulence, heavy rain, icing above the freezing level, windshear, low cloud to the surface and crosswinds well outside the storm. Airports in its path close.

Old exam answer

Before 2015 IMD had no "extremely severe" class: very severe ran from 64 to 119 kt and super cyclone was 120 kt or more. Older books and question banks may use this. The cyclonic storm threshold, 34 kt, has not changed.

  • India has two cyclone seasons: pre-monsoon (April to early June, mostly May) and post-monsoon (October to December, mostly October and November). The east coast gets hit in both.
  • The post-monsoon storms are often the strongest. November has the most.
  • The Bay of Bengal gets about four times as many as the Arabian Sea.
  • Almost none form in January to March. During the monsoon the strong upper easterlies give too much shear, so monsoon depressions form instead (Module 10).
  • Bay of Bengal storms mostly strike Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Bangladesh and Myanmar. Arabian Sea storms hit Gujarat, Maharashtra, Oman or die out at sea.

Low pressure systems in India are also described by closed isobars at 2 hPa intervals: a low has one, a depression two, a deep depression three, and a cyclonic storm four or more.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Belts: equatorial low, subtropical highs at 30°, subpolar lows at 60°, polar highs.
  • Winter: Siberian high, India on its southern edge. Summer: heat low over Pakistan and north west India.
  • High: sinking air, divergence, light winds, clockwise NH, fair but hazy, fog in winter, no low cloud.
  • Cold high: shallow, continental winter. Warm high: deep, subtropical. Blocking high: stays still, diverts depressions.
  • Low: convergence, rising air, anticlockwise NH. Heat low: shallow, hot and dusty.
  • TC needs: sea 26.5 °C or more, latitude 5° to 20° (not within 5° of the equator), moist unstable air, low wind shear, a starting disturbance.
  • TC energy: latent heat from the warm ocean. Weakens over land or cold sea.
  • Hurricane (Atlantic), typhoon (NW Pacific), cyclone (Indian Ocean).
  • Eye: light variable winds, little cloud, lowest pressure, warm. Eye wall: strongest winds and worst weather.
  • NH: dangerous semicircle on the right of the track, worst in the right front quadrant.
  • Moves west or north west, then often recurves north east. Storm surge at landfall.
  • IMD: depression 17 to 27 kt, deep depression 28 to 33, cyclonic storm 34 to 47, severe 48 to 63, very severe 64 to 89, extremely severe 90 to 119, super 120+.
  • India: pre-monsoon and post-monsoon cyclones. Bay of Bengal about 4 times the Arabian Sea. Nearly none January to March.

Sample questions

5 of the 41 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The subtropical high pressure belts lie at about:

    1. The equator
    2. 30° latitude
    3. 60° latitude
    4. The poles
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Air sinking from the Hadley cells builds high pressure at about 30° N and S, where the great deserts are.

  2. Q2. A cold anticyclone is typically:

    1. Deep, extending to the tropopause
    2. Shallow, found over cold continents in winter
    3. Found at the equator
    4. Associated with heavy rain
    Show answer

    Answer: B. It comes from air cooled near the ground, so it is shallow. The Siberian high is an example.

  3. Q3. Tropical cyclones do not form within about 5° of the equator because:

    1. The sea is too cold there
    2. The Coriolis force is too weak to start rotation
    3. There is no moisture
    4. Pressure is too high
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Coriolis is zero at the equator, so air flows straight into a low and fills it instead of spinning round it.

  4. Q4. In the northern hemisphere, the winds round a tropical cyclone blow:

    1. Anticlockwise and inwards
    2. Clockwise and outwards
    3. Straight in to the centre
    4. Clockwise and inwards
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Like any NH low, they turn anticlockwise and spiral in towards the centre.

  5. Q5. In the current IMD scale, a storm with sustained winds of 100 kt is an:

    1. Severe cyclonic storm
    2. Very severe cyclonic storm
    3. Extremely severe cyclonic storm
    4. Super cyclonic storm
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Extremely severe is 90 to 119 kt. Super cyclonic is 120 kt or more.

    Old exam answer: Before 2015 there was no extremely severe class, and 100 kt was "very severe" (64 to 119 kt). Use the current scale unless only the old terms are offered.
