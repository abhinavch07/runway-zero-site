How rain and snow form and the different kinds that fall, how visibility and RVR are measured and reported, every kind of fog and haze with the conditions that make it, and the halos, coronas and other optical sights pilots see.

How precipitation forms

Precipitation is water, liquid or solid, falling from a cloud and reaching the ground. A cloud droplet is tiny, far too small to fall against the up currents. It must grow about a million times in volume to become a raindrop. There are two ways this happens.

ProcessWhereHow
Ice crystal (Bergeron) process"Cold" clouds that reach well above the freezing level, with ice crystals and supercooled drops togetherBelow 0 °C the drops have a higher vapour pressure than the ice. Drops evaporate, the vapour deposits on the crystals, and the crystals grow and fall. They melt into rain on the way down if it is warm enough
Collision and coalescence"Warm" clouds, all above 0 °C, common in the tropicsBigger drops fall faster, hit smaller ones and join them. Large salt nuclei over the sea give a quick start. This is why tropical clouds can rain without reaching the freezing level

How much a cloud rains depends on how deep it is and how strong its up currents are. Thin layer cloud gives drizzle at most. Deep layer cloud gives steady rain. Deep heap cloud gives heavy showers and hail.

Freezing rain is the most dangerous for aircraft. It coats everything in heavy, clear ice within minutes. Snow usually reaches the ground when the surface temperature is below about +4 °C and the air between cloud base and ground is below 0 °C on average.

Old exam answer

Older books give different rain categories (for example light up to 7.5 mm, moderate 7.6 to 35.5 mm). IMD uses the table above now. If the exam options only match the old figures, pick the old figures.

When and where it rains

  • In the tropics most rain is convective, so it falls mostly in the afternoon and evening, and more in summer.
  • On coasts and in valleys such as those of north east India, rain often peaks at night or early morning, when the land breeze or katabatic wind meets the moist air and lifts it.
  • In mid latitudes most rain comes from depressions and fronts, which are most active in winter. Jammu and Kashmir and the western Himalayas follow this pattern because of western disturbances.
  • The side of a mountain range facing the wind gets heavy rain. The far side is a rain shadow: for example the interior of Maharashtra and Karnataka behind the Western Ghats in the monsoon.

Cloud seeding puts nuclei such as silver iodide, dry ice or salt into suitable clouds to start the ice crystal or coalescence process. It can bring rain a little earlier or clear supercooled fog for a short time.

Visibility and RVR

Visibility for aviation is the greater of: (a) the greatest distance at which a black object of suitable size near the ground can be seen and recognised against a bright background, and (b) the greatest distance at which lights of about 1,000 candelas can be seen and identified against a dark background.

Visibility is often different in different directions. In India, the METAR gives the lowest visibility seen around the observing point. If the lowest visibility is below 5,000 m and the visibility in other directions is at least half as much again, the direction is added (for example 4000NE). If the lowest is below 1,500 m and another direction is over 5,000 m, the best visibility and its direction are added too (for example 1400SW 6000N).

Old exam answer

ICAO now prefers the prevailing visibility (the visibility reached or bettered over at least half the horizon), and many countries use it. India still reports the lowest visibility in METARs, as IMD's aviation procedures lay down. For the DGCA exam, the visibility reported by the Met office is the lowest visibility.

Runway visual range (RVR) is the distance over which a pilot of an aircraft on the runway centre line can see the runway surface markings or the runway edge or centre line lights. It is measured by transmissometers (or forward scatter meters) beside the runway, usually at the touchdown zone, mid point and stop end. RVR is reported when the visibility or the RVR is less than 1,500 m.

  • Vertical visibility: looking straight down or up. Through a shallow fog layer it can be good.
  • Slant visibility: looking along the approach path. You look through a much longer stretch of fog, so it can be very poor. The pilot may see the runway from overhead and lose it on final.
  • Looking into the sun, haze scatters light and glare cuts visibility badly. Looking down sun, objects stand out.

Memory trick

Mist is moist, haze is dry. Fog and mist are water drops with high humidity. Haze and smoke are dry particles with lower humidity. The cut-off between fog and mist is 1,000 m.

Old exam answer

Some older bank questions give mist as 1,000 to 2,000 m. The current ICAO limits for mist are 1,000 to 5,000 m. Choose 1,000 to 5,000 m if it is offered.

Dust haze is a pre-monsoon hazard over Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana, UP and Bihar. Dust raised by the gusty daytime wind (from about 0900 to 1700) can spread over hundreds of km and several km upwards, so nearby alternates are often just as bad. A low level inversion traps smoke and dust, so visibility is worst on calm mornings near industrial areas.

How fog forms

Fog forms when the air near the ground becomes saturated. There are two ways: cool the air to its dew point, or add water vapour until it saturates. Plenty of condensation nuclei (smoke, salt) help fog form even a little before 100% RH, which is why city fog is thicker.

Radiation fog forms over land on a clear night as the ground loses heat and cools the air touching it. It needs:

  • Clear sky, so the ground can radiate heat away.
  • Light wind, about 2 to 8 kt. Calm gives only dew or a very shallow ground fog. A stronger wind mixes the cooling through a deep layer and gives low stratus instead of fog.
  • Moist air, with a small dew point spread in the evening.
  • A long night, so winter is the season.
  • A high pressure area or col, with slack isobars and light winds.

Radiation fog is usually thickest around sunrise, because the temperature is lowest then. It normally clears 2 to 3 hours after sunrise as the sun warms the ground, often lifting first into a layer of low stratus. Deep fog, or fog under thick smoke, can last till midday or all day. It is usually a few hundred feet deep, with clear sky above. It does not form over the sea, where the temperature hardly changes at night.

In India radiation fog is a winter hazard (December and January worst) across the Indo-Gangetic plains from Punjab to Bihar and West Bengal, and in Assam. It is most widespread after a western disturbance has passed: the rain has moistened the ground, then the sky clears, the wind drops and cold air arrives. Smoke from cities and crop burning makes it thicker and longer lasting.

Advection fog appears when the wind brings warm, moist air over a colder surface whose temperature is below the air's dew point. It forms over the sea and over land, by day or night, and can last for days. Unlike radiation fog it can form with a moderate wind (up to about 15 kt). Stronger winds lift it into low stratus. Sea fog can drift inland on the sea breeze. Along the Bengal and Odisha coasts in winter, fog is often a mix of advection and radiation.

Worked example: Air 12 °C, dew point 10 °C, sea temperature 8 °C. The air blows over the sea. What forms?

  1. The sea (8 °C) is colder than the air's dew point (10 °C)
  2. The air is cooled from below to saturation as it moves
  3. Advection fog (sea fog)

Fog dispersal at airports has been tried by heating the air (costly), by seeding supercooled fog with dry ice or silver iodide, and by spraying hygroscopic particles. None is in routine use in India.

Optical phenomena

SightCauseWhat to know
RainbowSunlight refracted and reflected inside raindropsSeen opposite the sun, about 42° radius. Red outside, violet inside. A fainter secondary bow (about 51°) has the colours reversed
HaloSunlight refracted through ice crystalsRing of 22° (sometimes 46°) round the sun or moon in cirrostratus. Faint red on the inside. Means ice cloud, so little icing
CoronaLight diffracted by small water dropletsSmall coloured rings close round the sun or moon in thin altostratus or altocumulus. Blue inside, red outside. The smaller the drops, the bigger the corona. Can mean supercooled drops and icing
GloryDiffraction by cloud dropletsColoured rings round the shadow of your own aircraft on cloud below, with the sun behind you
MirageLight bent through layers of very different densityInferior mirage (watery look on a hot runway or road) over very hot ground. Superior mirage (objects lifted up) over a cold surface under an inversion
Bishop's ringDiffraction by fine dust high up, often volcanicWhitish ring of about 22° round the sun
AuroraCharged particles from the sun hitting the upper airGlowing curtains of light at high latitudes (Borealis in the north, Australis in the south). Disturbs HF radio
St Elmo's fireGlow discharge of static electricityBluish glow on wing tips, propeller tips and windscreen near CB. Harmless itself, but warns of strong electric fields and lightning risk, and causes radio static

Old exam answer

One prescribed book says a halo shows clear red on the outside. That is the corona and the rainbow. A 22° halo has its faint red on the inside edge. If the exam asks only which ring has red outside, the corona (and rainbow) is the safe choice.

Memory trick

Halo is High (ice, cirrostratus, 22°, big ring). Corona is Close to the sun (water drops, small rings). Rainbow is opposite the sun.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Ice crystal (Bergeron) process in clouds above the freezing level. Coalescence in warm tropical clouds.
  • Drizzle under 0.5 mm from stratus. Showers from cumuliform cloud. Hail only from CB. Continuous rain from Ns.
  • Freezing rain: supercooled drops freeze on impact. Rain falling through a below 0 °C layer, ahead of a warm front.
  • Rainy day: 2.5 mm or more. IMD heavy rain 64.5 to 115.5 mm. Cloudburst about 100 mm in an hour.
  • Tropics: rain mostly afternoon, in summer. Mid latitudes and western Himalayas: winter. Coasts: night or early morning.
  • Rain shadow on the lee side of the Western Ghats.
  • Visibility: black object by day, 1,000 candela lights by night. Indian METARs give the lowest visibility.
  • RVR from transmissometers, reported when visibility or RVR is below 1,500 m.
  • Over shallow fog: vertical visibility good, slant visibility poor.
  • Fog below 1,000 m. Mist 1,000 to 5,000 m. Haze 5,000 m or less. Mist is moist, haze is dry.
  • Worst visibility of all: fog. Smoke worst under a low level inversion.
  • Radiation fog: clear sky, light wind 2 to 8 kt, moist air, long night, high or col. Over land only.
  • Radiation fog thickest around sunrise, clears 2 to 3 hours after. In India: winter, Indo-Gangetic plains, after a WD passes.
  • Advection fog: warm moist air over a cold surface, day or night, can form with moderate wind.
  • Steam fog: cold air over warm water. Frontal fog: ahead of a warm front.
  • Halo: ice, Cs, 22°. Corona: water drops, As, red outside. Rainbow: opposite the sun, red outside.
  • St Elmo's fire: glow discharge on aircraft near CB.

Quick revision

The 12 revision slides for this module, as a list.

Making rain

  • Cloud drops are too small to fall
  • Ice crystal process: in cold clouds, ice grows at the drops' expense
  • Coalescence: in warm tropical clouds, drops bump and join
  • Deeper cloud, stronger up currents: heavier precipitation

What falls from which cloud

  • Drizzle from stratus
  • Steady rain from nimbostratus
  • Showers from cumulus and CB
  • Hail only from CB

The icy ones

  • Freezing rain: supercooled drops freeze on impact
  • Comes from rain falling into a cold layer, ahead of warm fronts
  • Ice pellets: drops frozen on the way down
  • Snow likely below about +4 °C at the surface

IMD rain words

  • Light 2.5 to 15.5 mm a day
  • Moderate 15.6 to 64.4, heavy 64.5 to 115.5
  • Very heavy to 204.4, extremely heavy above
  • Rainy day: 2.5 mm. Cloudburst: about 100 mm an hour

Visibility and RVR

  • Black object by day, 1,000 candela lights by night
  • Indian METAR gives the lowest visibility
  • RVR: what the pilot sees along the runway
  • Reported when visibility or RVR is below 1,500 m

Slant visibility

  • Over shallow fog, the runway is clear from overhead
  • On the approach you look through much more fog
  • Vertical good, slant poor
  • Into the sun, haze and glare make it worse

Fog, mist, haze

  • Fog: below 1,000 m, wet
  • Mist: 1,000 to 5,000 m, wet
  • Haze: 5,000 m or less, dry
  • Smoke and dust worst under a low inversion

Radiation fog

  • Clear sky, light wind 2 to 8 kt
  • Moist air, long night, high or col
  • Thickest around sunrise, clears 2 to 3 hours later
  • India: winter, Indo-Gangetic plains, after a WD

Advection fog

  • Warm moist air over a colder surface
  • Over sea or land, day or night
  • Can form with wind up to about 15 kt
  • Air 12, dew point 10, sea 8: advection fog

Other fogs

  • Steam fog: cold air over warm water
  • Frontal fog: rain into cold air, warm front
  • Hill fog: moist air up a slope
  • Freezing fog: rime ice

Rings round the sun

  • Halo: 22°, ice in cirrostratus, red inside
  • Corona: small rings, water drops in As, red outside
  • Rainbow: opposite the sun, red outside
  • Glory: rings round your own shadow on cloud

More sights

  • Inferior mirage: wet look on a hot runway
  • Superior mirage: over cold sea, under an inversion
  • Aurora: high latitudes, upsets HF radio
  • St Elmo's fire: glow on the aircraft near CB

Sample questions

5 of the 46 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. When supercooled water drops and ice crystals exist together in a cloud:

    1. The water drops grow while the ice crystals shrink
    2. The ice crystals grow while the water drops shrink
    3. Both stay the same size
    4. Both evaporate
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Below 0 °C the vapour pressure over water is higher than over ice, so drops evaporate and the vapour deposits on the crystals. This is the ice crystal (Bergeron) process.

  2. Q2. In current IMD terms, 80 mm of rain in 24 hours is:

    1. Moderate
    2. Heavy
    3. Very heavy
    4. Extremely heavy
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Heavy rain is 64.5 to 115.5 mm. Very heavy starts at 115.6 mm.

    Old exam answer: Older books used different bands. In the current IMD table 80 mm is heavy.

  3. Q3. RVR is measured with a:

    1. Transmissometer
    2. Ceilometer
    3. Hygrometer
    4. Anemometer
    Show answer

    Answer: A. A transmissometer (or a forward scatter meter) measures how much light the air lets through along the runway. A ceilometer measures cloud base.

  4. Q4. Fog forms when the air near the ground is:

    1. Warmed above its dew point
    2. Cooled to its dew point, or has moisture added until saturated
    3. Made to sink
    4. Dried out
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Saturation can come from cooling or from adding water vapour.

  5. Q5. Which statement about advection fog is correct?

    1. It forms only on calm nights
    2. It forms only over land
    3. It cannot form with any wind
    4. It can form by day or night and with moderate wind
    Show answer

    Answer: D. It depends on the air moving over a cold surface, not on night cooling, so it can form any time and with wind up to about 15 kt.
