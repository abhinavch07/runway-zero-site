What air pressure is and how it changes with height, place and time, how it is shown on charts, the pressure settings QFE, QNH, QFF and 1013, transition altitude and level, and the altimeter errors that put aircraft closer to the ground than the pilot thinks.

What pressure is

Atmospheric pressure at any point is the weight of the column of air above it, pressing on each unit of area. The higher you go, the less air there is above you, so pressure always falls with height.

UnitISA MSL valueNote
Hectopascal (hPa)1013.25Used by ICAO. 1 hPa = 100 N/m²
Millibar (mb)1013.25Exactly the same size as the hPa
Inches of mercury29.92Used in the USA and on some altimeters
Millimetres of mercury760Used on older barometers

Air at rest pushes equally in all directions. This is static pressure, the one a barometer reads. Moving air adds dynamic pressure in the direction of flow (what the pitot tube uses).

How pressure is measured

InstrumentHow it works
Mercury barometerAir pressure holds up a column of mercury. The most accurate, used as the standard at Met offices
Aneroid barometerA sealed metal capsule with a partial vacuum that squeezes or swells as pressure changes. Less accurate but handy and robust
BarographAn aneroid that draws a continuous record of pressure on a drum
AltimeterAn aneroid barometer whose scale reads height instead of pressure

A mercury barometer reading is corrected for the instrument (index error), for temperature and for gravity, which changes slightly with latitude. Then it can be reduced to mean sea level so stations at different heights can be compared.

Pressure and height

Pressure falls quickly near the ground and more slowly higher up, because the air is thinner there. In ISA, 1 hPa of pressure change matches about:

HeightHeight for 1 hPa
Near MSL27 ft (about 8 m). Use 30 ft for quick altimeter sums
10,000 ftabout 36 ft
18,000 ft (500 hPa)about 50 ft
40,000 ftabout 100 ft

So near sea level a climb of 1000 ft lowers the pressure by about 33 to 37 hPa. Pressure is half the MSL value at about 18,000 ft.

Warm and cold columns. Cold air is denser, so pressure falls faster with height in cold air and more slowly in warm air. A given pressure level (say 700 hPa) is therefore lower over cold air and higher over warm air. At the same height, the pressure is higher over the warm area. This is why highs aloft sit over warm air and lows aloft over cold air.

Pressure on charts

  • Isobars join places of equal pressure. On surface charts they show pressure reduced to MSL (QFF). In India they are drawn every 2 hPa.
  • Pressure gradient is how fast pressure changes across the isobars. Close isobars mean a steep gradient and strong wind. Widely spaced isobars mean light wind.
  • Contours are used on upper air charts. They join places where a pressure level (such as 500 hPa) is at the same height. Low contour heights work just like low pressure.
  • Thickness is the height gap between two pressure levels. Warm air makes a thick layer, cold air a thin one.
SystemShapeAirUsual weather
Low (depression)Closed isobars, lowest in the middle. Wind anticlockwise (NH)Converges and risesCloud and rain, but good visibility once out of rain
TroughV shaped tongue of low pressureConverges and risesBad weather. Wind veers as a trough passes (NH)
High (anticyclone)Closed isobars, highest in the middle. Wind clockwise (NH)Sinks and divergesFair, light winds, but haze and poor visibility. Fog in winter
RidgeU shaped (rounded) tongue of high pressureSinksFair weather
ColThe saddle between two highs and two lowsLittle movementLight and variable wind, mixed weather, fog or thunderstorms by season

Pressure systems are covered in more depth in Module 8.

Pressure through the day

Even with no weather system around, pressure rises and falls twice a day: highest around 1000 and 2200 local time, lowest around 0400 and 1600. This is the semi-diurnal variation. It is about 3 hPa in the tropics and almost nil near the poles.

Because this daily swing is large in India, pressure tendency (the change of pressure with time) is worked out over 24 hours in India. In higher latitudes it is taken over the last 3 hours. Lines joining equal pressure tendency are isallobars. A low tends to move towards the area of greatest pressure fall.

Altimeter settings: QFE, QNH, QFF and 1013

An altimeter only measures pressure. It shows height above whatever pressure you set in its subscale. Change the setting and you change the reference level.

SettingWhat it isAltimeter shows
QFEPressure at aerodrome levelHeight above the aerodrome. Zero on the runway
QNHAerodrome pressure brought down to MSL using ISAAltitude above MSL. Shows aerodrome elevation on the runway
QFFStation pressure brought down to MSL using the actual temperatureNot set on altimeters. Used to draw isobars on weather charts
Standard 1013.25 hPaThe ISA MSL pressurePressure altitude, used for flight levels
QNENot a setting. The height the altimeter shows on the runway with 1013 setThe aerodrome's pressure altitude

Memory trick

QFE = Field Elevation is zero. QNH = Nautical Height (above sea). QFF = For Forecasting charts.

  • Elevation: how high a point on the ground is above MSL.
  • Altitude: how high something in the air is above MSL.
  • Height: how high something is above a stated point, usually the ground or aerodrome.
  • Pressure altitude: altimeter reading with 1013.25 set.
  • Flight level: pressure altitude in hundreds of feet. FL 80 = 8,000 ft on 1013.

Quick sums with 30 ft per hPa. The gap between QNH and QFE comes from the aerodrome's elevation:

Worked example: Aerodrome elevation 900 ft, QNH 1012 hPa. Find QFE.

  1. Elevation in hPa = 900 ÷ 30 = 30 hPa
  2. Aerodrome is above MSL, so its pressure is lower
  3. QFE = 1012 - 30 = 982 hPa

Worked example: QNH 1003 hPa. What does the altimeter show on the runway with 1013 set?

  1. 1013 is 10 hPa more than QNH
  2. Setting a higher value makes the altimeter read higher: 10 × 30 = 300 ft
  3. It shows elevation + 300 ft. That is the aerodrome's pressure altitude
  • The higher the aerodrome, the bigger the gap between QNH and QFE.
  • For an aerodrome at MSL, QNH = QFE.
  • For an aerodrome below MSL, QFE is more than QNH.
  • If QNH is exactly 1013.2 hPa, the aerodrome's pressure altitude equals its elevation.
  • Two aerodromes with the same QNH: the lower one has the higher QFE.

QNH and QFF. Both bring pressure down to MSL, but QNH uses ISA temperature and QFF the real one. For an aerodrome above MSL: when the air is colder than ISA, the imaginary column is denser, so QFF is more than QNH. When it is warmer than ISA, QFF is less than QNH. In ISA they are equal.

Transition altitude and transition level

TermMeaning
Transition altitude (TA)The altitude at or below which you fly on QNH and report altitude
Transition level (TL)The lowest flight level you can use above the TA. At or above it you fly on 1013.2 and report flight levels
Transition layerThe airspace between the TA and the TL
  • Climbing: set 1013 as you pass the transition altitude.
  • Descending: set QNH as you pass the transition level.
  • Inside the transition layer, report flight level when climbing and altitude when descending.

Everyone above the TL uses the same setting, so aircraft stay safely apart without changing the setting at every town. Below the TA, QNH keeps the altimeter tied to terrain heights on the chart.

Altimeter errors from pressure and temperature

The altimeter assumes ISA. When the real air is different, it reads wrong. Over-reading (showing higher than you really are) is the dangerous one, because you are closer to the ground than you think.

You flyAltimeterTrue altitude is
From high to low pressure (setting not changed)Over-readsLower than shown
From low to high pressureUnder-readsHigher than shown
Into air colder than ISAOver-readsLower than shown
Into air warmer than ISAUnder-readsHigher than shown

Memory trick

"High to low, or hot to cold: look out below." Both make the altimeter read too high.

Rules for the size of the error:

  • Pressure: about 30 ft for every 1 hPa the setting is wrong.
  • Temperature: about 4% of the height above the setting datum for every 10 °C away from ISA. Some books say 1% for every 3 °C, which gives nearly the same answer.

Worked example: Departed with QNH 1020 set and forgot to change it. Now flying at 3,000 ft indicated where QNH is 1005.

  1. Setting is 15 hPa too high
  2. Error = 15 × 30 = 450 ft, altimeter over-reads
  3. True altitude = 3,000 - 450 = 2,550 ft

Worked example: Flying at 5,000 ft on QNH. Air is ISA -15 °C. Find the true altitude.

  1. Colder than ISA, so the altimeter over-reads
  2. Error = 4% × 1.5 = 6% of 5,000 = 300 ft
  3. True altitude = 5,000 - 300 = about 4,700 ft

Drift tells you too. In the northern hemisphere, with your back to the wind, low pressure is on your left (Buys Ballot's law, Module 4). So if the wind comes from your left (port), you drift to starboard and you are heading towards low pressure: the altimeter over-reads. Wind from the right (starboard), port drift: it under-reads. It is the other way round in the southern hemisphere.

  • The safest conditions for terrain clearance are warm air and high pressure. Then you are higher than the altimeter says.
  • The worst are cold air and low pressure. The lowest usable flight level is worked out from the lowest QNH and the coldest temperature expected.
  • A radio altimeter measures true height above the ground directly with radio waves, so it has no pressure or temperature error.
  • Setting a subscale value higher than the correct QNH makes the altimeter read too high, by 30 ft per hPa.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Pressure = weight of air above. 1013.25 hPa = 29.92 in = 760 mm. 1 hPa = 1 mb.
  • Mercury barometer is most accurate. Altimeter = aneroid barometer. Barograph records pressure.
  • 1 hPa is about 27 ft (8 m) near MSL, use 30 ft for sums. About 50 ft at 18,000 ft, 100 ft at 40,000 ft.
  • Pressure falls faster with height in cold air. Pressure levels are higher over warm air.
  • Surface chart isobars show QFF. India draws them every 2 hPa. Close isobars, strong wind.
  • Semi-diurnal: highs about 1000 and 2200, lows about 0400 and 1600 local. About 3 hPa in the tropics.
  • India uses 24 hour pressure tendency. Isallobars join equal pressure tendency.
  • Low: rising air, bad weather, better visibility. High: sinking air, fair, poor visibility, light winds.
  • Trough: V shaped, rising air, wind veers as it passes (NH). Ridge: U shaped. Col: between two highs and two lows.
  • QFE reads zero on the runway. QNH reads elevation. 1013 gives pressure altitude and flight levels. QFF for charts.
  • Higher aerodrome = bigger QNH to QFE gap. Below MSL: QFE > QNH.
  • Colder than ISA: QFF > QNH. Warmer than ISA: QFF < QNH.
  • Climbing: 1013 at TA. Descending: QNH at TL.
  • High to low or hot to cold: over-reads, look out below.
  • NH: wind from port (starboard drift) means over-read.
  • Safest for terrain: warm and high pressure. Lowest flight level uses lowest QNH and coldest temperature.

Sample questions

5 of the 50 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Atmospheric pressure at a place is best described as:

    1. The weight of the column of air above that place
    2. The temperature of the air
    3. The density of air at the surface
    4. The force of the wind
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Pressure is the weight of all the air above, pressing on each unit of area. That is why it falls with height.

  2. Q2. At the same height, pressure is usually:

    1. Higher over a cold area
    2. Higher over a warm area
    3. Equal everywhere
    4. Lowest over the sea
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Pressure falls more slowly in warm air, so at a given height a warm column keeps more pressure.

  3. Q3. In a trough of low pressure, the air is generally:

    1. Descending and diverging
    2. Descending and converging
    3. Ascending and diverging
    4. Ascending and converging
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Air flows in (converges) and rises in a trough, giving cloud and bad weather.

  4. Q4. For an aerodrome 50 ft below MSL:

    1. QFE is less than QNH
    2. QFE equals QNH
    3. There is no relation
    4. QFE is more than QNH
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Below MSL there is extra air above the aerodrome, so its station pressure is higher than the MSL value.

  5. Q5. On a hot summer day, an aircraft flying close to the ground has an altimeter reading that is:

    1. Higher than the actual altitude
    2. The same as the actual altitude
    3. Lower than the actual altitude
    4. Zero
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Warmer than ISA, the altimeter under-reads, so it shows less height than you actually have.
