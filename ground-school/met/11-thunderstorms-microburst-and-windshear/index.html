What a thunderstorm needs, the three stages of a storm cell, the kinds of thunderstorm (including squall lines and the Indian norwesters), every hazard a CB brings, how to avoid them, and low level windshear: its causes, what it does to an aircraft on approach or take-off, the microburst, and how windshear is reported.

What a thunderstorm needs

A thunderstorm is a cumulonimbus (CB) cloud with thunder and lightning. Three things are needed, and the exam often asks for all three together:

  • Deep unstable air: a steep lapse rate through a deep layer, so a rising parcel stays warmer than the air around it for many thousands of feet.
  • Plenty of moisture: high humidity in the lower layers. Condensation releases latent heat, which keeps the parcel warm and rising.
  • A trigger to start the air rising: surface heating, hills, a front, or air converging (as in the ITCZ or a low).

Without moisture you get dry thermals or dust devils. Without instability the cloud stays flat. Without a trigger the unstable air may just sit there until afternoon heating sets it off.

Memory trick

Think M I T: Moisture, Instability, Trigger.

Life cycle of a storm cell

StageAir movementWhat you see and meet
Cumulus (developing)Updraughts only, getting stronger with heightCu grows into towering Cu (TCU). No rain at the ground yet. Lasts about 10 to 15 minutes
MatureBoth updraughts and downdraughts, side by sideStarts when rain reaches the ground. The most violent stage: heavy rain, hail, lightning, severe turbulence, icing, the gust front and microbursts. Anvil forms at the top. Lasts about 15 to 30 minutes
DissipatingDowndraughts onlyRain gets lighter and stops. The cloud collapses, often leaving the anvil (a sheet of cirrus) behind
  • A single cell lives for about an hour from start to finish. A multicell storm keeps making new cells beside the old ones and can last several hours.
  • Each cell is a few km across. Tops reach 40,000 ft or more in the tropics, sometimes over 55,000 ft over India in the pre-monsoon.
  • The downdraught is fed by cold rain falling through the cloud and by evaporation cooling the air. That is why it is colder than the air around it and rushes down.

Kinds of thunderstorm

KindHow it formsNotes
Air mass (heat, thermal)Strong surface heating of moist unstable airAfternoon and evening over land. Scattered, single cells. Over the sea they peak at night and early morning
OrographicMoist unstable air forced up hills or mountainsOn the windward side. Can sit over the same ridge for hours
FrontalLifting along a front, mainly an active cold frontForm a line along the front and move with it, so they are the fastest moving kind. Warm front CBs are often embedded in layer cloud
Squall lineA line of CBs, often ahead of a cold front, or along a dry line or outflow boundaryCan be hundreds of km long. The most severe non-rotating storms: violent squalls, hail, heavy rain. Hard to get round
ConvergenceAir meeting and rising in a low or the ITCZLines and clusters over the tropics
  • Embedded thunderstorms are CBs hidden inside layers of cloud or in haze, so they cannot be seen from the cockpit. Weather radar is the only way to find them in flight.
  • A supercell is a single very large storm with a rotating updraught. It gives the worst hail, the strongest winds and sometimes tornadoes.
  • A thunderstorm is reported by the Met office when thunder is heard at the station. Lightning seen far away without thunder is reported as lightning, not as a thunderstorm at the airfield.

Over India (more in Module 10): norwesters (Kalbaisakhi) over the east and north east from March to May, dust storms (Andhi) in the north west, pre-monsoon storms over the south and Kerala, monsoon CBs along the trough and the west coast, and in October and November thunderstorms over the south peninsula, Kerala and Tamil Nadu with the north east monsoon.

Thunderstorm hazards

HazardWhere and why
TurbulenceSevere inside the cloud, where updraughts and downdraughts sit side by side. Also below the cloud, under the anvil, around the cloud out to 20 nm, and above the top
IcingStrong updraughts carry large supercooled drops high up, so clear ice can form quickly, from 0 °C down to about -20 °C, and some icing down to -40 °C
HailInside the cloud, below it, and thrown out under the anvil, even in clear air several km from the cloud. Can crack windscreens and dent leading edges
LightningMost strikes on aircraft happen near the freezing level, roughly between +10 °C and -10 °C. Can blind the crew for a moment, damage radio and electrics and upset the magnetic compass
Gust front and squallThe cold downdraught spreads out ahead of the storm as a first gust: a sudden wind shift, sharp rise in speed, temperature drop and pressure jump, often several km before the rain
Microburst and windshearStrong downdraughts hitting the ground and spreading out. The biggest danger near the ground (see below)
Heavy rainPoor visibility, water on the runway, and very heavy rain can affect engines
Pressure changesPressure jumps as the gust front passes and the altimeter may misread by a few hundred feet
Static and radio noiseStatic on HF and ADF. St Elmo's fire: a blue glow on the windscreen and wing tips from static discharge
TornadoRare over India but seen with the strongest norwesters

The exam often asks why a thunderstorm is a hazard and gives several reasons as options. All of them are real: lightning, downdraughts, turbulence, icing and hail all come from the same CB.

Avoiding thunderstorms

  • Keep well clear: at least 10 nm from any CB, and 20 nm from severe ones, more at high levels. Pass on the upwind side so you are away from hail thrown out under the anvil.
  • Never fly under the anvil or under the overhang of a storm. Never take off or land with a thunderstorm over or close to the airfield.
  • Do not try to fly through a gap between two strong cells that are close together. The gap can close.
  • Do not trust the look of the cloud. A storm can be embedded in layer cloud. Use weather radar and avoid all red and magenta returns and any echo with a hook, finger or sharp edge.
  • Clear the top by a big margin. A rough rule is 1,000 ft above the top for every 10 kt of wind at that level.

If you cannot avoid it and must go through: tighten belts and stow loose items, set turbulence penetration speed, keep the wings level and the attitude steady and let the height vary (do not chase the altimeter), switch on engine and airframe anti-icing, turn cockpit lights full bright against lightning glare, and keep the heading. Once in, it is usually safer to keep going than to turn back.

Windshear

Windshear is a change of wind speed or direction or both over a short distance. It can be vertical (the wind changes with height) or horizontal (the wind changes along your path). It can happen at any level wherever there is a wind shift or a strong change of wind speed. It matters most as low level windshear, within about 1,600 ft (500 m) of the ground on take-off and approach, where there is little height to recover.

CauseHow it gives windshear
Thunderstorms and CBGust front, microbursts and downdraughts. Found on all sides of the storm and directly under it. The worst kind
FrontsWind changes across the front, worst with a fast moving cold front and a large temperature difference
Low level inversionOn a clear night the surface air goes calm under an inversion while the wind just above it stays strong. Climbing through the top of the inversion, the wind changes suddenly
Low level jetA band of strong wind a few hundred to a few thousand feet up, often at night
Terrain and buildingsStrong wind round hills, hangars and big buildings near the runway
Sea breeze frontWind changes as the sea breeze front passes
Mountain wavesRotors and strong downdraughts in the lee of ridges (Module 12)

What it does to the aircraft. Because of its inertia, the aircraft keeps its speed over the ground for a moment when the wind changes. So the airspeed changes:

ChangeAirspeedFlight path on approachName
Headwind increases or tailwind decreasesGoes upFloats above the glide pathPerformance increasing
Headwind decreases or tailwind increasesGoes downSinks below the glide pathPerformance decreasing, the dangerous one
DowndraughtLittle change at firstPushed downDangerous close to the ground

On approach, a sudden loss of headwind or gain of tailwind is the most dangerous: speed falls, lift falls, and the aircraft sinks below the glide path just when it is lowest. An increasing headwind is easier to handle but it is often the first part of a microburst, with the tailwind still to come.

Vertical windshear is measured in knots per 100 ft (about 30 m). Older books give it in knots per 1,000 ft or in m/s per 30 m.
Intensity (ICAO)Vertical shearEffect
Light0 to 4 kt per 100 ftMinor changes of path and speed
Moderate5 to 8 kt per 100 ftSignificant effect on control
Strong9 to 12 kt per 100 ftHard to hold the path and speed
SevereMore than 12 kt per 100 ftHazardous effect on control

The microburst

A microburst is a small, very strong downdraught that hits the ground and spreads out in all directions. The air inside it is colder than the air around it, which is why it falls so fast. A larger one, over about 4 km across, is a macroburst. Both are kinds of downburst.

  • Size: the outflow is less than 4 km across.
  • Life: seldom more than 15 minutes from the time it hits the ground. The winds get stronger for the first 5 minutes after it touches down, and the strongest winds last only 2 to 4 minutes.
  • Strength: downdraughts up to about 6,000 ft per minute. With 45 kt of outflow each side, the change from headwind to tailwind can be 90 kt.
  • Wet microburst: comes out of heavy rain under a CB. Dry microburst: under high based cloud with virga (rain that evaporates before reaching the ground). Evaporation chills the air, which then plunges down. A ring of blowing dust may be the only sign.
  • Where there is one, expect more. They are common under norwesters and pre-monsoon CBs over India.

Flying through one on approach (follow the figure): (1) the headwind rises, speed rises and the aircraft floats high. The pilot is tempted to reduce power. (2) In the core, a strong downdraught pushes the aircraft down. (3) On the far side the headwind turns into a tailwind, airspeed falls sharply and the aircraft sinks fast, possibly into the ground.

  • Avoid: do not take off or land with a CB over or near the field, or with virga, blowing dust or a reported windshear on the path. Wait or divert.
  • If you meet it: apply full (go-around or take-off) power at once, pitch up to the windshear attitude (follow the flight director or windshear guidance), do not change flap or gear until clear, accept the stick shaker if needed, and do not chase airspeed.
  • Report it to ATC so others are warned.

How windshear is found and reported

  • Ground systems: LLWAS (a ring of anemometers round the airfield comparing winds) and Doppler weather radar, which sees air moving towards and away from it.
  • Aircraft systems: reactive windshear warning (the aircraft senses it is in shear) and predictive windshear on the weather radar (warns before you fly into it).
  • The Met office issues a windshear warning for the approach and take-off paths when it is observed or expected.
  • In a METAR, windshear is added at the end as supplementary information: WS R28 (windshear on runway 28) or WS ALL RWY. It is not part of the wind group.
  • Pilots should report windshear to ATC: where it was, at what height, and what it did (for example "loss of 20 kt at 500 ft on final runway 28").

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Thunderstorm needs: moisture, steep lapse rate (instability) and a trigger.
  • Stages: cumulus (updraughts only), mature (up and down, starts when rain reaches the ground, most severe), dissipating (downdraughts only).
  • Single cell about 1 hour. Multicell storms last hours.
  • Frontal storms are the fastest moving. Squall lines form ahead of a cold front.
  • Embedded TS: hidden in cloud layers or haze and cannot be seen.
  • TS is reported when thunder is heard at the station.
  • Hazards: turbulence, icing (clear ice), hail (also under the anvil), lightning, gust front, microburst, windshear, heavy rain, pressure jumps, static.
  • Lightning strikes most likely near the freezing level.
  • Keep 10 nm from CB, 20 nm from severe ones. Never under the anvil. Pass upwind.
  • In a storm: turbulence speed, hold attitude not height, anti-ice on, lights full bright, keep the heading.
  • Windshear: change of wind speed or direction or both, at any level. Low level means below about 1,600 ft (500 m).
  • TS windshear is found on all sides and directly under the storm.
  • Loss of headwind or gain of tailwind: airspeed falls, aircraft sinks below the path. The dangerous case.
  • Inversion windshear: calm below, strong wind above, on clear nights.
  • Vertical shear in kt per 100 ft. Light 0 to 4, moderate 5 to 8, strong 9 to 12, severe over 12.
  • Microburst: under 4 km, cold air, downdraught to 6,000 ft/min, lasts under 15 minutes, peak winds 2 to 4 minutes.
  • Microburst on approach: headwind, then downdraught, then tailwind.
  • Windshear in a METAR: supplementary information WS R28 or WS ALL RWY.

Quick revision

The 11 revision slides for this module, as a list.

What a thunderstorm needs

  • Moisture in the low levels
  • Instability: steep lapse rate through a deep layer
  • A trigger: heating, hills, front, convergence
  • Remember M I T

Three stages

  • Cumulus: updraughts only
  • Mature: up and down, rain at the ground, worst weather
  • Dissipating: downdraughts only
  • One cell lives about an hour

Kinds of thunderstorm

  • Air mass: afternoon heating over land
  • Orographic: over hills
  • Frontal: moves with the front, fastest
  • Squall line: ahead of a cold front

Hidden and severe storms

  • Embedded: hidden in cloud or haze
  • Supercell: rotating, worst hail and wind
  • Reported as TS when thunder is heard
  • India: norwesters, dust storms, pre-monsoon CB

CB hazards

  • Severe turbulence, clear ice, hail under the anvil
  • Lightning near the freezing level
  • Gust front: wind shift, temperature drop, pressure jump
  • Microburst, windshear, heavy rain, static

Keep away

  • 10 nm from CB, 20 nm if severe
  • Pass upwind, never under the anvil
  • No take-off or landing with a CB overhead
  • Use radar: avoid red, magenta, hooks

If you must go through

  • Turbulence speed, belts tight
  • Hold attitude, let height vary
  • Anti-ice on, cockpit lights full bright
  • Keep the heading, do not turn back

Windshear

  • Change of wind speed or direction
  • Vertical or horizontal, any level
  • Low level: below about 1,600 ft
  • Causes: CB, fronts, inversions, terrain, sea breeze

Effect on approach

  • More headwind: speed up, above the path
  • Less headwind or more tailwind: speed down, below the path
  • The second is the dangerous one
  • Downdraughts push you down

The microburst

  • Under 4 km, cold falling air
  • Downdraught up to 6,000 ft/min
  • Lasts under 15 minutes, worst for 2 to 4
  • Headwind, downdraught, tailwind

Escape and report

  • Full power, windshear pitch, no flap or gear change
  • Do not take off or land into it, wait or divert
  • LLWAS, Doppler radar, predictive windshear
  • METAR: WS R28, report it to ATC

Sample questions

5 of the 41 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. The conditions needed for a thunderstorm to form are:

    1. Plenty of moisture, a steep lapse rate and a lifting trigger
    2. Plenty of moisture and an inversion
    3. Plenty of moisture and full cloud cover
    4. Dry air and strong surface wind
    Show answer

    Answer: A. A CB needs moist air, deep instability (a steep lapse rate) and something to start the air rising.

  2. Q2. The term "embedded thunderstorms" means:

    1. Severe storms inside a squall line
    2. Storms that are forecast in stable air
    3. Storms hidden inside cloud layers or haze, so they cannot be seen
    4. Storms that have dissipated
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Embedded CBs are hidden from view. Weather radar is the only way to find them in flight.

  3. Q3. The minimum distance usually kept from a thunderstorm is about:

    1. 1 nm
    2. 10 nm, and 20 nm from severe storms
    3. 100 nm
    4. It is safe to fly under the anvil
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Turbulence and hail reach well outside the cloud, so a wide berth is needed, wider at high levels.

  4. Q4. Windshear associated with a thunderstorm can be found:

    1. Only in front of the storm
    2. Only behind the storm
    3. On all sides of the storm and directly under it
    4. Only above the storm
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The outflow spreads in every direction, and the downdraught is directly under the cell.

  5. Q5. The approximate diameter of a microburst is:

    1. More than 20 km
    2. Less than 4 km
    3. About 50 km
    4. About 100 m
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The outflow of a microburst is under 4 km across. Larger ones are called macrobursts.
