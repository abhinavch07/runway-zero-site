How ice forms on an aircraft, the kinds of ice (clear, rime, mixed, hoar frost) and where each is found, freezing rain, carburettor icing, the kinds and intensities of turbulence, mountain waves with their rotor and lenticular clouds, and inversions as a flying hazard.

How airframe icing forms

Airframe icing needs two things: liquid water drops that are below 0 °C (supercooled), and an airframe that is below 0 °C. When a supercooled drop hits the aircraft it freezes, wholly or partly, on the spot. Cloud drops often stay liquid well below 0 °C (Module 2). Below about -40 °C all drops are frozen, so there is little or no airframe icing in clouds that cold: ice crystals bounce off.

  • Icing is most likely between 0 °C and about -15 °C, and the risk is greatest just above the freezing level (at temperatures just below 0 °C), where there is the most supercooled water.
  • In layer cloud (St, Sc, As, Ns) icing is usually found from 0 °C down to about -15 °C.
  • In cumuliform cloud (Cu, TCU, CB) strong updraughts carry large supercooled drops high up, so severe icing is found from 0 °C down to about -20 °C and icing is possible down to -40 °C.
  • Cirrus is made of ice crystals and gives no airframe icing.
  • Icing can also happen in clear air, in freezing rain or drizzle falling from a warmer layer above.

Kinds of ice

KindHow it formsWhat it is likeWhere
Clear ice (glaze)Large supercooled drops. Each drop freezes slowly, because the latent heat released lets part of it flow back over the surface before it freezesClear, glassy, hard, heavy, sticks firmly. Spreads back over the wing and changes the shape of the aerofoil. Hard to removeCB and big Cu, Ns, freezing rain. Mostly 0 °C to about -10 °C
Rime iceSmall supercooled drops freeze at once on impact, trapping air between themWhite, opaque, rough, brittle, light. Builds forward on leading edges. Easier to removeSt, Sc, As, small Cu, freezing fog. Mostly -10 °C to -20 °C and colder
Mixed iceA mix of drop sizes, or snow caught in clear iceRough, white and clear together. Can build odd shapesCumuliform cloud, often -10 °C to -15 °C
Hoar frostWater vapour turns directly into ice (deposition) on a surface below 0 °C. No drops involvedThin, white, feathery crystalsOn a parked aircraft on a clear, cold night, or when a cold soaked aircraft descends quickly into warm moist air

Why clear ice is the most dangerous: it is heavy, hard to see, sticks firmly, flows back beyond the de-icing boots, and spoils the shape of the wing. Frost matters too: even a thin layer roughens the wing, spoils the airflow and raises the stall speed, so all frost must be removed before take-off.

Memory trick

Clear = big drops, slow freeze. Rime = small drops, quick freeze, trapped air makes it white.

Freezing rain

Freezing rain (FZRA) forms when rain falls out of a warm layer above 0 °C into a layer below 0 °C underneath, often ahead of a warm front where warm air rides over cold air (an inversion). The drops become supercooled but are very large, so they spread and freeze as clear ice very fast. It is the worst icing of all, worse than icing in freezing fog (FZFG), whose drops are tiny.

  • Escape: climb into the warm layer above (if it is safe to do so), or leave the area. Do not hold height in it.
  • Freezing rain or drizzle reported at an airfield (FZRA, FZDZ) also glazes runways, taxiways and parked aircraft.
  • Freezing rain is rare over India. It is a winter hazard of higher latitudes and is still asked in the exam.

What decides how much ice you collect

  • Amount of liquid water in the cloud (CB and Ns hold the most).
  • Size of the drops: large drops give clear ice and spread further back.
  • Temperature of the air and of the airframe. Drop size together with temperature decides the kind of ice and how bad it is.
  • Shape of the part: thin, sharp parts (probes, tail edges, aerials, thin wings) collect ice faster than thick, blunt ones.
  • Speed: a faster aircraft meets more drops, but high speed also warms the skin by friction and compression (kinetic heating), which can stop icing at high speed.
  • Time in the icing: the longer you stay, the more you collect.
IntensityWhat happens
LightSlow build up. No problem for short periods. Use de-icing now and then
ModerateFaster build up. Even short exposure is a hazard. De-icing or anti-icing needed, a change of level or route is wise
SevereIce builds faster than the systems can remove it. Leave the area at once

Effects of airframe ice: more weight and drag, less lift and thrust, a higher stall speed with less warning, controls that stiffen or jam, propeller vibration, blocked pitot and static sources (wrong airspeed and altitude readings), iced windscreens and aerials, and ice breaking off into engines.

Carburettor and engine icing

In a piston engine with a carburettor, the air speeds up through the venturi (so its pressure and temperature fall) and the fuel evaporating into it takes heat. The air inside can cool by 20 °C or more. If the outside air is humid, ice forms in the venturi and on the throttle valve, choking the engine.

  • It can happen in clear air, at outside temperatures well above 0 °C, up to about +25 °C or +30 °C when the humidity is high.
  • The risk is highest in humid air with temperatures around +5 °C to +20 °C, and at low power (descents and approach), when the partly closed throttle valve chills the air most.
  • First sign with a fixed pitch propeller: a slow drop in RPM. Apply full carburettor heat at once. The engine may run rough for a moment as the ice melts.
  • Jet engines can ice in cloud near 0 °C on the intake lips, and at high levels in thunderstorm tops with dense ice crystals.

Turbulence

Turbulence is irregular, gusty motion of the air that gives bumps, jolts and changes of height and attitude. Its strength depends mainly on how strong the wind shear is (vertical and horizontal) and on how unstable the air is.

KindCauseWhere and when
Convective (thermal)Rising thermals and sinking air between themHot afternoons over land, below Cu bases. Usually smooth above the Cu tops. Worst in and near CB
MechanicalWind blowing over rough ground, hills, trees and buildingsNear the ground in strong winds. Worse with stronger wind, rougher ground and unstable air. Gusts and eddies downwind of hangars near runways
FrontalLifting and shear along a frontNear active cold fronts
Wind shear turbulenceLayers of air moving at different speedsAt inversions, near jet streams (CAT, Module 9)
Mountain waveWaves and rotors downwind of ridgesSee below
Wake turbulenceWing tip vortices of other aircraftNot weather, but strongest behind heavy, slow, clean aircraft. They sink and drift with the wind
Intensity (ICAO)What it feels like
LightSlight, quick changes in height or attitude. Belts feel a slight strain
ModerateChanges in height and attitude, airspeed changes, but the aircraft stays in positive control. Walking is hard, loose things move
SevereLarge, sudden changes in height and attitude and big airspeed changes. The aircraft may be out of control for short periods. People are thrown against their belts
ExtremeThe aircraft is tossed violently and is practically impossible to control. Structural damage is possible

In turbulence: belts on, slow to turbulence penetration speed (it protects against both stalling and overstressing), hold the attitude and let height and speed wander a little, and report moderate or severe turbulence to ATC. Severe turbulence leads to a SIGMET.

Mountain waves

When a strong wind blows across a mountain range into stable air, the air goes over the ridge and then bounces up and down in waves downwind, like water flowing over a rock in a stream. These mountain waves (lee waves) can stretch a hundred km or more downwind and reach up into the stratosphere.

  • Wind blowing roughly at right angles to the ridge (within about 30°).
  • Wind speed at ridge top at least about 15 to 25 kt, more for big mountains, increasing with height with little change of direction.
  • A stable layer near the ridge top, with less stable air above and below.
CloudWhereMeaning
Cap cloud (foehn wall)Sitting over the ridge topAir cooling as it rises over the ridge. Strong downdraughts on its lee edge
Lenticular cloudAt the crests of the waves, often in rows, at medium and high levelsSmooth, lens shaped, stays still although the wind blows through it. A sure sign of waves
Rotor (roll) cloudUnder the wave crests, at or below ridge height, downwindAir rolling round under the wave. Severe turbulence

Waves can be there with no cloud at all if the air is dry. So no lenticulars does not mean no waves.

  • Downdraughts on the lee side of the ridge and in the down part of each wave can be 2,000 ft per minute or more, enough to pull a light aircraft into the ground.
  • Severe turbulence in the rotor zone, below the wave crests near ridge height.
  • Smooth up and down motion in the waves themselves, which can make it hard to hold a level.
  • The altimeter can over-read in strong winds over mountains, because pressure is lower in fast flowing air, so you are lower than you think.
  • Icing in cap and lenticular clouds.

Flying near them: cross ridges with plenty of height, often 3,000 to 5,000 ft above the ridge or more in strong winds. Approach the ridge at about 45° so you can turn away easily. Stay out of rotor cloud. Expect downdraughts when flying into wind towards a ridge from its lee side. Over India, mountain waves are common over the Himalayas in winter with strong westerlies, and also occur over the Western Ghats. Severe mountain waves lead to a SIGMET (SEV MTW).

Inversions as a flying hazard

An inversion is a layer where temperature rises with height (Module 2). It is the most stable layer there is and acts as a lid: air below cannot rise through it.

KindWhen and whereHazard
Radiation (night) inversionClear, calm nights over land. Strongest at dawn. Burnt off by morning sunFog, smoke and haze trapped near the ground. Windshear at its top
Subsidence inversionSinking air in a high, a few thousand feet upHaze and smog trapped for days. Flat topped St or Sc under it. Delhi winter smog
Frontal inversionWarm air over cold air at a frontFreezing rain under it in cold climates. Low cloud
Turbulence inversionTop of a well mixed surface layerCloud tops level off. Bumpy below, smooth above
  • Poor visibility under it: fog, mist, smoke and haze cannot mix upwards.
  • Windshear: light winds below and strong winds above. On climb out the wind can change suddenly at the inversion top. Low level windshear is often linked to a low level inversion.
  • Smooth air above it and cloud tops flat at its base. Cumulus cannot grow through it, so it stops showers and thunderstorms until it breaks.
  • Over hot land, an inversion aloft can let heat build up below until it breaks suddenly in the afternoon, setting off violent storms (as before some norwesters).
  • Radio and radar waves can bend in a strong inversion, giving unusually long ranges or false echoes (ducting).

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Icing needs supercooled water drops and an airframe below 0 °C.
  • Icing risk greatest just above the freezing level. Most icing 0 to -15 °C. CB: severe icing 0 to -20 °C, possible to -40 °C. Below -40 °C: little or none.
  • Clear ice: large drops, slow freezing, heavy, hard, changes the aerofoil. In CB, Ns and freezing rain.
  • Rime: small drops, instant freezing, trapped air, white, brittle, light. In St, Sc, freezing fog.
  • Hoar frost: vapour to ice directly, clear cold nights or descent into moist air. Remove all frost before take-off.
  • Freezing rain: rain from a warm layer above into sub-zero air. Worst icing. Ahead of warm fronts.
  • Worst combination for icing: drop size and temperature. Thin parts ice faster.
  • Carburettor icing: clear air, up to about +25 °C, humid air, low power. Sign: RPM drop. Full carb heat.
  • Turbulence strength depends on wind shear and instability.
  • Mechanical turbulence grows with wind speed, rough ground, unstable air.
  • Moderate: aircraft in positive control. Severe: momentarily out of control. Extreme: practically impossible to control.
  • Mountain waves need wind across the ridge, speed rising with height, and a stable layer near the ridge top.
  • Lenticular: wave crests, stationary. Rotor cloud: below the crests, severe turbulence. Cap cloud over the ridge.
  • Waves can exist without cloud. Strong lee side downdraughts. Cross ridges at 45° with plenty of height.
  • Inversion: poor visibility below, windshear at the top, smooth above, flat cloud tops.
  • Severe icing, turbulence or mountain waves lead to a SIGMET.

Quick revision

The 10 revision slides for this module, as a list.

Airframe icing

  • Needs supercooled drops and a sub-zero airframe
  • Most likely 0 to -15 °C
  • Greatest just above the freezing level
  • Below -40 °C: almost none

Clear and rime

  • Clear: big drops, slow freeze, heavy, hard
  • Rime: small drops, instant freeze, white, brittle
  • Clear ice changes the wing shape
  • Mixed ice: both together

Frost and freezing rain

  • Hoar frost: vapour straight to ice
  • Clear cold nights, remove before take-off
  • Freezing rain: warm layer above, cold below
  • The worst icing of all

How much ice

  • Liquid water and drop size
  • Temperature
  • Thin parts collect ice fastest
  • Time in cloud, speed and kinetic heating

Effects of ice

  • More weight and drag
  • Less lift, higher stall speed
  • Blocked pitot and static
  • Severe: leave at once

Carburettor ice

  • Venturi and fuel evaporation chill the air
  • Clear air, up to about +25 °C, humid
  • Worst at low power
  • RPM drop: full carb heat

Turbulence

  • Convective, mechanical, frontal, shear, waves
  • Strength: wind shear and instability
  • Moderate: still in control
  • Severe: briefly out of control

Mountain waves

  • Wind across the ridge, rising with height
  • Stable layer near the ridge top
  • Cap, lenticular and rotor clouds
  • Can exist with no cloud

Wave hazards

  • Lee side downdraughts
  • Severe turbulence in the rotor
  • Altimeter over-reads in strong wind
  • Cross at 45° with plenty of height

Inversions

  • Temperature rises with height: a lid
  • Fog, haze, smog trapped below
  • Windshear at the top
  • Smooth above, flat cloud tops

Sample questions

5 of the 40 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. Airframe icing will be greatest when an aircraft flies through cloud made of:

    1. Ice crystals
    2. Supercooled water drops
    3. Water vapour only
    4. Snowflakes
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Supercooled drops freeze on impact. Ice crystals mostly bounce off.

  2. Q2. Rime ice is:

    1. Clear, heavy and hard to remove
    2. White, opaque, rough and brittle
    3. Only formed on the ground
    4. Only formed in freezing rain
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Trapped air makes rime white. It is light and easier to break off than clear ice.

  3. Q3. Flying in freezing rain, a sensible escape is to:

    1. Hold the same level
    2. Slow down and wait
    3. Climb into the warmer layer above, if safe, or leave the area
    4. Descend into colder air
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The rain comes from warmer air above, so climbing into it stops the icing.

  4. Q4. The intensity of turbulence depends mainly on:

    1. The strength of vertical and horizontal wind shear
    2. The amount of sunshine
    3. The height of the aircraft
    4. The time of day only
    Show answer

    Answer: A. Shear makes eddies. Instability then lets them grow.

  5. Q5. The absence of lenticular clouds over a mountain range in a strong wind means:

    1. There are certainly no mountain waves
    2. Icing is certain
    3. The wind must be calm
    4. Mountain waves may still be present in dry air
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Waves only show as cloud when there is enough moisture. Dry waves are invisible.
