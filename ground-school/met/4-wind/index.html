How wind is measured and reported, the forces that make it blow (pressure gradient, Coriolis, friction), geostrophic and gradient wind, Buys Ballot's law, how wind changes with height and through the day, crosswind sums, and the local winds every pilot meets.

Wind direction, speed and measurement

Wind is air moving horizontally. It has a direction and a speed. Direction is where the wind comes from. A westerly wind blows from the west towards the east. A southerly blows from the south.

  • Direction is given in degrees, to the nearest 10°. In METARs, TAFs and forecasts it is from true north. The wind ATC gives you for take-off and landing is magnetic, to match the runway.
  • Speed is in knots (nautical miles per hour). 1 kt = 1.852 km/h, about 0.5 m/s. Roughly, 1 m/s = 2 kt.
  • Surface wind is measured at a height of 10 m in open ground: an anemometer for speed (spinning cups) and a wind vane for direction. A windsock shows the wind at a glance.
  • The wind in a METAR is the average over 10 minutes. The wind given by ATC for take-off and landing is averaged over 2 minutes.
  • Upper winds are measured by tracking a balloon: with a theodolite (pilot balloon) or with radar or GPS (RAWIN).

Memory trick

Veer = clockwise. Picture a clock: V for "with the hands". Back = backwards (anticlockwise).

What makes the wind blow

Pressure gradient force (PGF) is the push from high pressure towards low pressure, at right angles to the isobars. The closer the isobars, the stronger the push. This is what starts the wind.

Coriolis force comes from the earth turning under the moving air. It is not a real push but it has a real effect: it turns moving air to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. It always acts at right angles to the wind.

  • Coriolis force = 2 × earth's spin rate × air density × wind speed × sin(latitude).
  • It is zero at the equator (sin 0° = 0) and greatest at the poles.
  • It gets stronger as the wind gets faster. Still air has no Coriolis force.
  • It changes the direction of the wind, never its speed.

Geostrophic and gradient wind

Air starts moving from high to low, Coriolis turns it to the right, and it keeps turning until the two forces balance. Then it blows parallel to straight isobars. This is the geostrophic wind: a balance of pressure gradient force and Coriolis force, with no friction (above about 2,000 to 3,000 ft).

  • Closer isobars, stronger geostrophic wind.
  • For the same isobar spacing, the geostrophic wind is stronger at low latitudes and weaker at high latitudes, because Coriolis is weaker near the equator. Wind at 30°N is stronger than at 70°N for the same gradient.
  • For the same gradient it is also stronger at height, where the air is less dense.
  • It does not work near the equator (within about 15°), where Coriolis is almost nil.

Gradient wind is the wind along curved isobars, round a low or a high. A third force comes in, the centrifugal effect of going round a curve.

Around aWhat happensWind compared with geostrophic
Low (cyclonic)Centrifugal effect works against the pressure gradient forceWeaker (sub-geostrophic)
High (anticyclonic)Centrifugal effect works with the pressure gradient forceStronger (super-geostrophic)

So for the same isobar spacing, wind round a high is stronger than round a low. (In practice highs have widely spaced isobars, so their winds are usually light.) With straight isobars, gradient wind = geostrophic wind.

Cyclostrophic wind is the balance of pressure gradient and centrifugal force alone, with Coriolis too small to matter. It is found in very tight, fast spinning systems: tornadoes, dust devils, and near the centre of a tropical cyclone, and close to the equator.

Buys Ballot's law

In the northern hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind and low pressure is on your left. High pressure is on your right. In the southern hemisphere it is the other way round.

  • So in the NH, wind blows anticlockwise round a low and clockwise round a high.
  • In the SH, it blows clockwise round a low and anticlockwise round a high.
  • This is how drift tells you which way the pressure is falling, and why the altimeter over-reads with starboard drift in the NH (Module 3).

Memory trick

Northern hemisphere: back to wind, Low on Left. Two L's.

Friction and the surface wind

Near the ground, friction slows the wind. Slower wind means less Coriolis force, so the pressure gradient force wins a little and the wind turns in across the isobars towards low pressure. This happens in the friction layer, the lowest 1 km or so (about 2,000 to 3,000 ft). It is deeper over rough ground and on hot, unstable days.

SurfaceSurface wind crosses isobars bySpeed compared with 2,000 ft wind
Over the seaAbout 10° to 15°About two thirds or more
Over landAbout 30°About half

So in the northern hemisphere, compared with the wind at 2,000 ft, the surface wind is backed and lighter. Climbing out of the friction layer, the wind veers and increases. (In the southern hemisphere the surface wind is veered and lighter.)

Worked example: The 2,000 ft wind over land is 270° 30 kt. Estimate the surface wind (NH).

  1. Back it by about 30°: 270 - 30 = 240°
  2. Halve the speed: about 15 kt
  3. Surface wind about 240° 15 kt

Through the day. On a sunny afternoon, rising thermals mix the faster upper wind down to the ground. The surface wind becomes stronger, gusty and veered, closer to the 2,000 ft wind. On a clear night the mixing stops, a ground inversion forms, and the surface wind drops and backs, sometimes to calm, while a strong wind keeps blowing a few hundred feet up. This gives low level windshear at night. Over the sea the wind hardly changes through the day.

Wind higher up: thermal wind

Above the friction layer, the wind still changes with height when temperature changes across the area. The difference between the wind at a lower level and a higher level is called the thermal wind.

  • The thermal wind blows along the isotherms (lines of equal mean temperature of the layer), with cold air on the left in the northern hemisphere.
  • Because the poles are colder than the tropics, the thermal wind is mostly westerly. This is why westerly winds get stronger with height in mid latitudes, ending in the jet streams (Module 9).
  • In the NH, wind that veers with height means warm air is being carried in. Wind that backs with height means cold air is being carried in.

Headwind and crosswind

Runways are laid out along the most common wind direction, but the wind is rarely straight down the runway. Split it into a headwind (along the runway) and a crosswind (across it). A headwind is what you want for take-off and landing. Every aircraft has a maximum crosswind limit.

Worked example: Runway 27, wind 300° 20 kt. Find the crosswind and headwind.

  1. Angle = 300 - 270 = 30°
  2. Crosswind = half of 20 = 10 kt, from the right
  3. Headwind = 0.9 × 20 = about 17 kt

Local winds

Sea breeze and land breeze come from the daily temperature difference between land and sea. By day the land heats faster, air rises over it, and cooler air flows in from the sea: a sea breeze, about 10 to 15 kt, felt up to 1,000 to 2,000 ft and some tens of km inland. At night the land cools faster and a weaker land breeze blows out to sea. The sea breeze is stronger than the land breeze.

Anabatic and katabatic winds come from heating and cooling of slopes. By day a sunny slope warms the air on it, which rises up the slope: an anabatic wind, light and often hidden by thermals. At night the slope cools the air on it, which drains down the slope: a katabatic wind. Katabatic winds are stronger and collect cold air in valleys, giving frost, mist and fog. Over snow or ice they can blow by day too and become very strong.

Memory trick

Anabatic goes Ascending, Katabatic Komes down. Day up, night down.

Föhn wind. Moist air forced up a mountain range cools, forms cloud and drops rain on the windward side. Inside the cloud it cools only at the slower saturated rate. On the lee side, now dry, it sinks and warms at the faster dry rate. So it reaches the lee foothills warmer and drier than it started. The Chinook of the Rocky Mountains is a föhn wind. Föhn needs a big mountain range, a strong wind blowing across it and moist air.

Valley and ravine winds: when a pressure difference exists across a hill range, air is forced through gaps and valleys and speeds up, like water through a narrow pipe. Strong turbulence is found near the exit.

Remember for the exam

Remember for the exam

  • Wind direction is where it comes from. METAR and forecasts true, ATC runway wind magnetic. Speed in knots.
  • Surface wind measured at 10 m. METAR wind averaged over 10 minutes, ATC take-off and landing wind over 2 minutes.
  • Veer clockwise, back anticlockwise. Gusts reported when 10 kt or more above the mean. Gale 34 kt or more.
  • Squall: sudden, lasts a minute or more, with CB.
  • Coriolis: right in NH, left in SH. Zero at the equator, maximum at the poles. Grows with wind speed.
  • Geostrophic wind: PGF = Coriolis, straight isobars, no friction, blows along the isobars.
  • Same gradient: geostrophic wind stronger at low latitudes. Not used within about 15° of the equator.
  • Gradient wind: curved isobars. Weaker than geostrophic round a low, stronger round a high.
  • Cyclostrophic: PGF and centrifugal only. Tornadoes, TRS centre, near the equator.
  • Buys Ballot (NH): back to the wind, low on the left. Anticlockwise round a low in the NH.
  • Friction layer about 1 km. Surface wind crosses isobars towards low: about 30° and half speed over land, 10 to 15° and two thirds over sea.
  • NH: surface wind is backed and lighter than 2,000 ft wind. Climbing, it veers and increases.
  • By day surface wind veers, strengthens, gusty. At night it backs and drops.
  • Thermal wind along isotherms, cold air on the left (NH). Veering with height = warm advection.
  • Crosswind at 30° = half, at 60° = 0.9. Headwind at 60° = half.
  • Sea breeze by day, stronger. Land breeze by night. Caused by the land and sea temperature difference.
  • Anabatic up the slope by day. Katabatic down the slope by night, stronger.
  • Föhn and Chinook: warm and dry on the lee side. Bora: cold katabatic. Harmattan: dust, poor visibility.

Quick revision

The 14 revision slides for this module, as a list.

Reading the wind

  • Direction is where it comes from
  • METAR: degrees true. ATC runway wind: magnetic
  • Speed in knots. 1 m/s is about 2 kt
  • Measured at 10 m, averaged 10 min (METAR), 2 min (ATC)

Wind words

  • Veer: clockwise. Back: anticlockwise
  • Gust: 10 kt or more over the mean, for seconds
  • Squall: sudden, a minute or more, with CB
  • Gale: 34 kt or more

Two forces

  • Pressure gradient: high to low, across the isobars
  • Coriolis: turns wind right in NH
  • Coriolis is zero at the equator, maximum at the poles
  • Grows with wind speed, changes direction only

Geostrophic wind

  • PGF = Coriolis. Blows along straight isobars
  • Closer isobars, stronger wind
  • Same gradient: stronger at low latitudes
  • Not used within about 15° of the equator

Curved isobars

  • Gradient wind: PGF, Coriolis and centrifugal
  • Round a low: weaker than geostrophic
  • Round a high: stronger than geostrophic
  • Cyclostrophic: tornadoes, TRS centre

Buys Ballot's law

  • NH: back to the wind, low on the left
  • NH: anticlockwise round lows, clockwise round highs
  • SH: the other way round

Friction

  • Friction layer: lowest 1 km
  • Surface wind turns towards low pressure
  • Land: about 30°, half speed
  • Sea: 10 to 15°, two thirds speed

Day and night

  • NH surface wind is backed and lighter than at 2,000 ft
  • Climbing out: wind veers and increases
  • Afternoon: thermals mix it down, gusty and veered
  • Night: backs and drops, windshear above

Thermal wind

  • Change of wind with height due to temperature
  • Blows along isotherms, cold on the left (NH)
  • Makes westerlies stronger with height
  • Veering with height = warm air coming in

Crosswind sums

  • 30°: crosswind half
  • 45°: 0.7 each
  • 60°: crosswind 0.9, headwind half
  • Rwy 27, wind 300/20: 10 kt cross, 17 kt head

Sea and land breeze

  • Land heats and cools faster than sea
  • Sea breeze by day, 10 to 15 kt, stronger
  • Land breeze by night, weaker

Slope winds

  • Anabatic: up the slope by day, light
  • Katabatic: down the slope at night, stronger
  • Katabatic fills valleys with cold air: frost and fog
  • Bora: a cold katabatic wind

Föhn

  • Moist air rises, rains out on the windward side
  • Comes down the lee side warm and dry
  • Chinook in the Rockies
  • Needs big mountains, strong wind, moist air

Named winds

  • Loo: hot, dry, north India summer
  • Harmattan: dusty, West Africa, poor visibility
  • Mistral: cold, Rhône valley
  • Sirocco / Khamsin: hot, dusty, North Africa

Sample questions

5 of the 50 practice questions for this module. Tap "Show answer" to check yourself.

  1. Q1. A southerly wind is one that blows:

    1. From the north towards the south
    2. From the south towards the north
    3. From the east
    4. Along the south coast
    Show answer

    Answer: B. Wind is named for where it comes from. A southerly comes from the south.

  2. Q2. In the northern hemisphere, the Coriolis force deflects moving air:

    1. To the left
    2. Upwards
    3. To the right
    4. Towards the equator only
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The earth's spin makes moving air turn right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern.

  3. Q3. For the same isobar spacing, the wind would be strongest around:

    1. A low
    2. A col
    3. A trough
    4. A high
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Round a high the centrifugal effect adds to the pressure gradient force, so the gradient wind is stronger than geostrophic.

  4. Q4. The surface wind is usually strongest and most gusty:

    1. Around dawn
    2. At midnight
    3. On a calm winter morning
    4. On a sunny afternoon
    Show answer

    Answer: D. Afternoon thermals mix the faster upper wind down to the ground, making it stronger, veered and gusty.

  5. Q5. Anabatic winds occur:

    1. During the night
    2. Only over the sea
    3. During the day
    4. Only in winter
    Show answer

    Answer: C. Sunny slopes warm the air on them, which flows up the slope by day.
